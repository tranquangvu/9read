Incident Management, On-call & Postmortems

The burn-rate alerts from SR1 tell you that patients cannot book. This module is about what happens in the next hour and the next two weeks: declaring an incident with the right severity, running it with clear roles, communicating with the clinic and its patients, designing an on-call rotation people can sustain, writing runbooks that work at 3 a.m., and turning each incident into a blameless postmortem whose action items actually get done.

Why it matters in production: the difference between a 20-minute incident and a 3-hour one is rarely technical skill. It is whether someone declared it early, whether one person was in charge, whether the responder found a runbook or started from zero, and whether the obvious mitigation (roll back) happened before the interesting investigation. Clients forgive outages that are handled well and communicated honestly. They do not forgive silence, and they do not forgive the same outage twice.
Part I · The incident lifecycle

An incident is a process you declare, not a feeling you have

An incident is any unplanned event that hurts users or threatens to, and that needs a coordinated response. "Declaring" one is a deliberate act: it opens a channel, assigns a commander, starts the clock and tells everyone else to stay out of the way. Declaring too often costs a few minutes. Declaring too late costs hours. So the rule is simple: if you are wondering whether it is an incident, declare it, and downgrade later.

Severity levels decide who is woken and how often you talk

Severity is about user impact, not about how hard the fix is or how worried the engineer feels. Define it with examples from your own system, because "major degradation" means nothing at 3 a.m. Severity is set by the incident commander, and it can change as you learn more.

SEV1No patient can book; patient data exposed; data lossPage primary, secondary and IC now; ack in 5 minStatus page in 15 min, updates every 30 min, client lead phonedAlways, within 5 working days

Note what is not a factor: whether it is a weekend, whether the cause is AWS, or whether a VP noticed. A security incident (leaked key, compromised task) is always at least SEV2 and also follows the security playbook in SE1.

Six phases and the clocks between them

Every incident moves through the same phases. Naming them lets you measure where the time went. In the clinic incident reviewed in Part IV, detection took 10 minutes, acknowledgement 3, and mitigation 24 more. The biggest chunk was the gap between "we know it's broken" and "we rolled back", which is the gap a good process shrinks.

Track these per incident in the postmortem, and look at the median across a quarter rather than the mean: one six-hour incident distorts the mean for months. The DORA "time to restore" key (FN1) is the same TTM measured across all failed changes.

Mitigate first, find the root cause later

During an incident your job is to restore service, not to understand it. If the incident started within an hour of a deploy, roll back before you have proved the deploy is the cause: a rollback that turns out to be unnecessary costs five minutes, an investigation that delays it costs the error budget. Keep a short list of levers and how long each takes on the clinic platform.

A rollback outside Git does not stay rolled back. Argo CD re-syncs the bad version if auto-sync is on, and on ECS the deploy pipeline owns the running image (Terraform ignores task_definition), so the next pipeline run re-deploys the tag still in Git. The IC's checklist must include "open the revert PR" as part of mitigation, not as clean-up.

Closing an incident is a decision too

"Mitigated" and "resolved" are different states. Mitigated means users are fine but the system may be held together by a manual change. Resolved means the IC has checked a short list and is willing to hand the service back to normal on-call. Closing too early is how the same incident reopens at 2 a.m. with a different IC and no context.

  • The SLI is back at baseline for 30 minutes, not just for one dashboard refresh (the October incident waited from 10:01 to 10:30).
  • Every manual change is in Git: the revert PR is merged, or the console change is reproduced in Terraform.
  • Silences and temporary scale-ups have an expiry and an owner, written in the channel.
  • The final status page update is posted and the clinic lead has been told directly.
  • A postmortem owner and due date are set before the channel is archived.

Part II · Roles & communication

One commander, one channel, one voice to the outside

Incidents go wrong in predictable social ways: five engineers debug the same thing, nobody updates the client, two people roll back and forward at the same time, and the person who knows the system best is also trying to answer the account manager. The Incident Command System, borrowed from firefighting, fixes this by separating roles. On a small team one person may hold two roles, but every role has a name next to it.

The roles and what each one does not do

Status page and client updates that say something

External updates have one job: let the clinic decide what to do (switch to phone bookings, warn front-desk staff). Say what users experience, what you are doing, and when the next update comes. Never speculate on cause, never blame a vendor publicly, and never miss a promised update time. "No change, still working on it" posted on time builds more trust than a detailed update posted late.

When the cause is AWS

Check the provider early, in parallel with your own investigation, but never wait for it: AWS Health often confirms a regional problem 15–30 minutes after customers see it. The account-specific AWS Health events are more useful than the public status page because they name your affected resources. The Health API needs a Business or higher support plan, and its global endpoint lives in us-east-1.

Part III · On-call & runbooks

On-call is a product your team designs, not a punishment it endures

A sustainable on-call has four parts: enough people in the rotation, few enough pages per shift, a clear escalation path when the first person does not answer, and runbooks that let a tired engineer act without deep context. Leading the on-call program as a whole (compensation, hiring for it, cross-team policy) is a senior skill covered in AR5. Here you learn to build and run one.

Designing the rotation

The maths of a rotation is unforgiving. With a weekly primary shift, each engineer is on call one week in N. Below about six people, on-call becomes every month or more, people burn out, and they leave. The clinic rotation mixes DevOps and backend engineers, because half of all pages end in application code.

ModelRight whenPeople neededCatch

  • Shift change on a weekday morning (Monday 10:00 ICT), never Friday evening, so handoff happens with everyone awake.
  • Primary is shadowed for two shifts before a new engineer goes on the rotation alone, with the secondary as a named coach.
  • A night page earns the morning off. Write it into the policy so nobody has to ask.
  • Page budget: at most two paging incidents per 12-hour shift averaged over a month, as defined in SR1. Above that, fixing alerts beats new features.

At every shift change the outgoing primary posts a short handoff in #clinic-oncall and the two engineers talk for ten minutes. The written note is also raw data for toil reduction in Part IV: every page and every manual task goes in it.

Runbooks that work at 3 a.m.

A runbook is linked from the alert, starts with the impact and the safest mitigation, and contains copy-paste commands with expected output. It is not a design document. Every page has exactly one runbook, every runbook has a "last tested" date, and anything not tested in six months is assumed broken.

Part IV · Postmortems & toil

The postmortem is where the incident pays for itself

You already paid for the incident in error budget, client trust and a ruined morning. The postmortem is how you get something back: a shared understanding of how the system really behaves and a small number of changes that make this class of failure less likely or less painful. It only works if it is blameless, specific and followed up.

Blameless means looking at the system, not excusing everyone

Blameless does not mean nobody did anything. It means assuming everyone acted reasonably given what they knew, and asking why the system let a reasonable action cause harm. If the answer is "Minh should have been more careful", the next engineer will make the same mistake, because nothing changed. Language is the quickest test.

BlamefulBlamelessWhat it leads to
"Minh set the pool size too high.""The PR raised the pool to 50. Nothing in review or CI compared pool × max tasks with max_connections."A CI check, not a reprimand
"On-call was slow to respond.""The page fired 10 minutes after errors started because the 1 h window had to fill."Look at detection design
"Human error.""The console allowed an unreviewed change to prod."Permission boundaries, GitOps

A worked postmortem: the October booking 5xx

The timeline is the spine of the document. Build it from the Slack channel, deploy history and metrics, not from memory. Mark where the system changed state and where people made decisions, because the gaps between them are where the action items hide.

Action items that actually get done

The most common postmortem failure is a list of good intentions nobody does. Limit it to three to five items, each with an owner, a priority, a due date and an issue in the tracker. Prefer items that remove a whole class of failure (a CI check) over items that rely on people remembering (a wiki note).

Agents are good at the paperwork around incidents: turning a noisy Slack channel into a timeline, drafting status updates in a consistent voice, and checking runbook commands still work. Use them with read-only credentials and never let one take a production action during an incident without the IC approving it in the channel. AI-assisted triage in depth is AR6.

Red flags in AI-written incident material: a confident root cause in a timeline that should only contain facts; a status update that blames AWS or a vendor; "human error" or an engineer's name as a contributing factor; ten action items with no owners; runbook commands that write (update-service, kubectl delete) presented as diagnosis; and timestamps silently converted from UTC to ICT, or not converted at all.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ The IC who debugs

The most senior person becomes IC and then opens a terminal. Coordination stops, comms slip, and two changes collide. If you are the best debugger, hand IC to someone else.

✗ Waiting for proof before rolling back

"The deploy was 12 minutes before the errors, so it can't be that" cost 15 minutes in the October incident. If a change is recent, roll back first and prove later.

✗ Postmortems as punishment

If writing one feels like a trial, people stop declaring incidents and you lose the data. Managers must visibly thank the people whose change triggered an incident for a good write-up.

✓ Practise on boring days

A 30-minute tabletop each month, rotating the IC role, makes the first real SEV1 feel familiar. Pair it with the game days in SR3.

✓ Write SEV4 near misses too

A one-paragraph review of "RDS failed over and nobody noticed" is cheap and tells you which defences work. Near misses are free lessons.
