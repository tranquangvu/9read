Technical Leadership for Senior DevOps

Every module before this one made you better at the work. This one is about making the work go well when you are not the one doing it. A senior DevOps engineer turns a vague client worry into a written decision, reviews other people's Terraform so the whole team gets safer, estimates honestly, evaluates tools without falling for the demo, grows the L1 engineers coming through this program, hires the next ones, and runs an on-call rotation that people do not quit over. The yardstick is the promotion rubric on the program page: this module turns its L3 column into habits and artifacts you can show.

Why it matters in production: the outages that hurt most at L3 scope are rarely a typo. They are a migration nobody wrote down, a review that waved through a security group because the author was senior, an estimate of "two days" that became six weeks of half-finished state, or an on-call rotation of two burnt-out people who stopped reading alerts. None of these is fixed by better Terraform. They are fixed by the senior engineer who writes the RFC, asks the awkward question in review, and says no to the work that should not exist.
Part I · What senior means

Senior is measured by what changes when you are not in the room

At L2 you owned a service and nobody had to check your work. At L3 the question changes: does the platform, and the team around it, get better because of decisions you made and people you taught? That shows up in three dimensions. Scope: the size of the system you are trusted with. Ambiguity: how unclear the problem is when it reaches you. Influence: how many people change what they do because of you, without you having authority over them.

Scope, ambiguity, influence

A mid-level engineer receives "add a read replica to the booking database". A senior engineer receives "the clinic's reports are slow and the client is unhappy" and has to find out whether the answer is a replica, a reporting pipeline, an index, or a conversation about expectations. The work is mostly turning ambiguity into a scoped, written, agreed problem. The diagram shows how the same incident looks at each level.

Deciding what not to build, and writing it down

The most valuable senior output is often a "no" with a reason. A service mesh for six services, a custom Kubernetes operator for something Argo CD already does, a multi-region active-active setup for a clinic platform with a 4-hour RTO. Saying no out loud in a meeting is forgotten in a week. Keep a decline log in the infra repo so the same proposal does not come back every quarter and the reasoning survives you.

clinic-infra/docs/decline-log.md

The "revisit when" line is the point. A decline without a trigger reads as "never" and makes people work around you. A decline with a measurable trigger (a service count, a contract clause, a traffic number) reads as "not yet", and tells everyone exactly what would change your mind.

Owning the risk register

Seniors carry a list of things that could hurt the client and have not been fixed yet: the single NAT gateway in staging, the RDS major version going out of standard support, the one engineer who understands the VPN. A risk you have written down, scored and given an owner is a managed risk. A risk in your head is a future incident with your name on it. Keep it as data in Git so it is reviewed like code and diffed every month.

Your first 90 days on an inherited platform

Most senior work at Golden Owl starts with inheriting a client platform someone else built. The temptation is to start fixing on day three. Resist it: the first month is for listening and writing down, because the thing that looks wrong often has a reason you have not heard yet. The plan below is what we expect a new senior on the clinic account to produce, in this order.

clinic-infra/docs/onboarding/senior-90-days.md 
## Days 1–30 · Listen and map (change nothing non-urgent)
- 1:1 with client tech lead, product owner, finance contact, every engineer on the rotation
- Draw the current architecture from the AWS accounts, not from the old diagram
- Read the last 6 postmortems and the last 3 months of pages (Part IV query)
- Output: "state of the platform" doc · first draft of risk register (top 10)

## Days 31–60 · Pick two wins, propose one big change
- Two small, visible fixes with a measured result (e.g. noisy alert, NAT endpoint)
- One RFC for the biggest risk-register item, reviewed by the client
- Output: 2 merged PRs with numbers · RFC in review · decline log started

## Days 61–90 · Make it run without you
- Monthly FinOps + on-call health review on the client's calendar
- Mentoring plan agreed for one L1 on the account
- Output: RFC decided · first monthly report sent · mentoring record opened

Each phase ends in an artifact a reviewer can read. That is also how you show the "Scope" row of the rubric in your first quarter instead of your first year.

Part II · Writing and deciding

If the decision is not written down, it was not made

Writing is the senior engineer's main tool for influence. A good RFC lets six people disagree with you asynchronously in two days, instead of in three meetings over three weeks. It also forces you to find the holes in your own idea before production finds them. This Part covers the RFC itself, estimating the work honestly, and evaluating the tools the RFC might choose.

RFC, ADR or just a PR?

Not every change needs a document, and over-documenting is its own failure: people stop reading. Use the cost of reversing the decision as the test. ADRs, short records of one decision already made, are covered in AR1. An RFC is the longer document that comes before a big decision, with alternatives, and ends in one or more ADRs.

ChangeReversal costArtifactClinic example
Routine, inside one serviceMinutes: revert the PRPR descriptionRaise booking-worker max tasks from 10 to 20
One component, one teamDaysADRUse Valkey instead of Redis OSS for ElastiCache
Cross-team, costly or one-wayWeeks to months, or neverRFC → ADRsMove booking-api from ECS to EKS; buy a 1-year Savings Plan; add a DR region
EmergencyWhatever stops the bleedingIncident log now, ADR within a weekDisable a WAF rule that blocks real bookings

An RFC skeleton that forces the hard parts

Most bad RFCs are a description of the solution the author already likes. The skeleton below is ordered to stop that: problem and goals before design, a required "do nothing" alternative, and explicit sections for cost, rollout, rollback and the decision. If you cannot fill the non-goals and rollback sections, you are not ready to write the design section.

clinic-infra/docs/rfcs/0000-template.md 
# RFC-NNNN: <one-line decision being asked for>
Status: Draft | In review (closes YYYY-MM-DD) | Accepted | Rejected | Superseded by RFC-MMMM
Author: · Reviewers (named, max 5): · Decider: <one person>

## 1. Problem         — what hurts, for whom, with a number (latency, cost, toil hours, risk score)
## 2. Goals / Non-goals — what success looks like; what this RFC explicitly does NOT solve
## 3. Constraints     — compliance, budget, team skills, deadlines, existing contracts
## 4. Options
   4.0 Do nothing     — always first: what happens in 6 months if we change nothing?
   4.1 Option A       — design sketch, diagram, cost/month, effort (range), risks
   4.2 Option B       — same headings, so options compare side by side
## 5. Recommendation  — which option and WHY the others lose; what would change our mind
## 6. Cost            — run cost delta (AR2 numbers), one-off effort, commitments created
## 7. Rollout         — phases, which environments, feature flags, success metric per phase
## 8. Rollback        — the exact trigger and steps; what is one-way (data, DNS, commitments)
## 9. Security & ops  — new IAM, data flows, on-call impact, runbooks to write
## 10. Open questions — each with an owner and a date
## 11. Decision log   — date, decider, outcome, link to the ADR(s) it produced

An RFC without a named decider and a closing date never closes. "Let's get consensus" turns into a three-week comment thread that the loudest person wins. Name one decider (often the client tech lead or the senior DevOps engineer), give reviewers five working days, and record disagreement in the decision log instead of waiting for it to disappear. "Disagree and commit" only works if the disagreement was written down.

Estimating infra work: ranges, hidden work, confidence

Infra estimates are wrong in a predictable direction. The Terraform takes two days. The other eight are state migration, an IAM permission in an account you do not own, a quota increase that takes 48 hours, a change window the client must approve, and the runbook. Estimate in ranges with an explicit confidence, list the hidden work line by line, and re-estimate at each milestone rather than defending the first number.

Hidden workTypical costHow to shrink it
State moves and imports0.5–2 days per stackmoved/import blocks, rehearsed in staging (IA1)
Waiting on others (quotas, client IAM, DNS owners)2–10 calendar days, 0 effortRequest on day 1, in parallel with the build
Change windows and approvals1 window per prod step, often weeklyMake each step small and reversible so it fits a standard change
Observability, alerts, runbook20–30% of build effortPut it in the estimate as its own line, never "if time permits"
Decommissioning the old thingOften as long as building the new oneMake "old path deleted" part of the definition of done

Notice the two numbers: effort (days of engineering) and calendar (weeks until done). Stakeholders hear only the second. Giving them only the first is how "about a week" becomes a broken promise.

Evaluating vendors and tools without falling for the demo

Every quarter someone proposes a new tool: a cost platform, an internal developer portal, a secrets manager, an AI ops assistant. Demos are designed to win. Your job is to evaluate against our constraints, run a time-boxed proof of concept on a real clinic workload, and price the total cost of ownership, including the engineer-hours to run it and the exit cost if the vendor disappears or triples its price.

Set the weights before anyone sees a demo, and get the client to agree to them. Weights chosen after the demo are rationalisations. Default to the boring, managed, already-in-the-stack option unless a criterion that matters scores it below 3.

Part III · Growing engineers

Your review, your mentoring and your hiring are the team's real quality gate

Checkov, tflint and policy gates (IA2, SE2) catch the mechanical mistakes. What they cannot catch is a design that is valid but wrong: a new account with its own NAT gateways when it should share the central egress VPC, a Karpenter NodePool with no limits, a migration with no rollback. That is caught by people. At L3 you make more of those people, through how you review, mentor and hire.

Reviewing other people's infra: blast radius first, style last

PR mechanics (CODEOWNERS, rulesets, small diffs) are in FN4. A senior review is about what could go wrong at what scale. Read the plan before the code: the plan is what will actually happen. Then work down a fixed order so you never spend your attention on naming while a -/+ destroy and then create replacement on the prod database slips through.

.github/pull_request_template.md — clinic-infra senior review checklist 
### Reviewer: work top to bottom, stop at the first blocker

        resources (RDS, ElastiCache, S3, EBS, KMS, Route 53 zones)?  → blocker unless planned
- [ ] 2. BLAST RADIUS: which accounts/envs/state files? Does it apply to prod in the
        same merge as staging?                                         → split it
- [ ] 3. IAM & NETWORK: new `*` actions/resources, 0.0.0.0/0 ingress, public S3/RDS,
        new cross-account trust?                                       → needs SE review
- [ ] 4. ROLLBACK: can we revert this PR and re-apply cleanly? One-way changes
        (data, DNS TTLs, KMS key deletion) called out in the description?
- [ ] 5. COST: new NAT GW, interface endpoints, larger instance class, log volume?
        Number in the description (AR2)?
- [ ] 6. OPERABILITY: alarms, dashboards, runbook link for anything new in prod?
- [ ] 7. CONVENTIONS: module versions pinned, tags via module inputs, naming.  → nit

How you write the comment decides whether people want your review next time. Label every comment so the author knows what is blocking and what is taste, and ask questions about intent before asserting a fix:

LabelMeaningExample on a clinic PR
blocker:Must change before merge, with the reason"blocker: the plan replaces aws_db_instance.booking because identifier changed. That is a new empty database in prod."
question:I may be missing context; please explain"question: is the new NAT gateway in gol-staging intentional, or should it use the shared egress VPC?"
suggestion:Better option, author decides"suggestion: for_each over the queue map avoids index churn when one is removed."
nit:Style only, never blocks"nit: booking_worker_sqs to match the naming in the other envs."
praise:Name what was done well, specifically"praise: the moved blocks mean zero resources change. Exactly right."
Do not rubber-stamp seniors. The highest-risk PRs in our history were approved within minutes because the author was the most experienced person on the team. Review the plan, not the author. And when your own PR gets a blocker from an L1, thank them in the thread: that is how a team learns that review is about the system, not status.

Mentoring an L1 through this program

The L3 capstone requires a mentoring record for one L1 engineer. Mentoring is not answering questions on Slack. It is a plan with goals tied to module scores, a weekly rhythm, deliberately chosen stretch tasks, and evidence that the person needs you less each month. Part IV · Running the operation

A sustainable on-call and honest communication are system design too

SR2 taught you how to be on call and how to run an incident. At L3 you own the program: who is on the rotation, how much it pages them, what it costs them, and whether it gets better each quarter. And you own how the platform's state is explained to people who do not read dashboards: the client's product owner, their finance lead, our account manager.

Designing the rotation

The minimum healthy rotation is about six people, so each is primary one week in six. Fewer than four and people burn out or start ignoring pages; the clinic team runs a combined app-plus-DevOps rotation for exactly this reason. Write the policy down and keep it in the repo, so that changes to it are reviewed like changes to alerting.

clinic-infra/docs/oncall-policy.yaml 
rotation:
  name: clinic-platform
  members: 6                       # 3 DevOps + 3 backend; min 5, alert the lead at 4
  shift: weekly, handoff Tue 10:00 ICT   # never Monday: weekend context is still fresh on Tuesday
  roles: [primary, secondary]      # secondary = escalation at 15 min, not a second pager
  shadow_before_primary: 2 shifts  # L1s shadow, then reverse-shadow
paging:
  pages_only_for: SLO burn-rate alerts + data-loss risks   # SR1: symptoms, not causes
  ack_sla: 5m business hours, 15m out of hours
  everything_else: ticket in #clinic-alerts, handled in hours
health_targets:                    # reviewed monthly; breach = on-call improvement work next sprint
  pages_per_shift: "<= 2"
  out_of_hours_pages_per_shift: "<= 1"
  pages_without_action_taken: "< 10%"   # noise; delete or demote the alert
compensation: per client contract — out-of-hours allowance + time off in lieu after any night page

Measuring on-call health, and acting on it

On-call quality is measurable. Export pages from the paging tool to the same S3 + Athena setup used for the CUR (AR2) and review four numbers monthly with the rotation. If the numbers are bad for two months in a row, the next sprint's top priority is fixing the alerts or the system, not shipping features. Get the client to agree to that rule before you need it.

oncall/queries/monthly_health.sql 
SELECT alert_name,
       count(*)                                                    AS pages,
       count_if(hour(created_at AT TIME ZONE 'Asia/Ho_Chi_Minh') NOT BETWEEN 9 AND 18) AS out_of_hours,
       round(avg(date_diff('minute', created_at, acked_at)), 1)  AS avg_ack_min,
       count_if(resolution = 'no_action')                         AS noise
FROM oncall.pages
WHERE service LIKE 'booking-%' AND month = '2026-09'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY pages DESC;
alert_name                         pages  out_of_hours  avg_ack_min  noise
BookingWorkerQueueAgeHigh             9             6          7.4      7   <- 78% noise: fix first
BookingApiAvailabilityBurnFast        2             1          3.1      0
RdsFreeStorageLow                     1             0         11.0      0

In this month, one alert caused 9 of 12 pages, and 7 of those needed no action because the queue drained by itself after a KEDA scale-out. The fix is not "be more careful". It is to alert on queue age above the SLO for 15 minutes instead of 2, and that one PR cut out-of-hours pages for the rotation from 7 to 1.

Talking to stakeholders: translate, quantify, ask

Non-engineers do not need less information. They need it in their units: money, risk, dates and what you need from them. A senior DevOps update is short, starts with the conclusion, gives a number and a confidence, and ends with an explicit ask. Never let a client find out about a slipped date or a risk from someone else.

Influence without authority: pre-wire the decision

You rarely manage the people whose behaviour you need to change: the client's backend team, their security officer, our account manager. Decisions that surprise people in a meeting get blocked in that meeting, even good ones. So by the time an RFC reaches its decider, every person who could block it should already have seen it, in a 1:1, with their concern either addressed in the document or recorded as an open question.

Working with Claude Code

Agents are useful sparring partners for leadership work: they will argue against your RFC without caring about your feelings, draft a rubric in minutes, and summarise a month of pages. They cannot decide for you, and they do not know the client, the politics or the people. Treat every output as a first draft from a smart new hire who has never met anyone on the team. Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0
No exposure. Works only on tasks handed to them; has never written a design document or reviewed someone else's infra.
1
Awareness. Knows what an RFC is and has read a few; reviews PRs for style and syntax but not for blast radius.
2
Works with support. Writes an RFC for a component with a senior's help, estimates in ranges when reminded, pairs with juniors informally.
3
Works independently (target). Writes cross-team RFCs that reach a decision on time, keeps a decline log and risk register, reviews plans for blast radius and rollback, gives estimates with hidden work and confidence, runs a structured mentoring plan for an L1, interviews with a rubric, and owns on-call health metrics.
4
Leads & coaches. Sets the RFC and review culture for the whole team, trains other seniors to mentor and interview, changes how the client makes infra decisions, and is the person both engineers and the client call when a decision is stuck.
Self-check before claiming 3: show a mentor two RFCs you wrote that were accepted or rejected with a recorded reason, one decline-log entry with a "revisit when" trigger, and the last month's on-call health numbers with the change you made because of them. Then review a seeded terraform plan in under 15 minutes and find the data-loss line first.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Being the bottleneck hero

If every prod change, incident and hard question routes through you, you are a single point of failure with a nice title. Measure yourself by how many things now happen well without you.

✗ Writing the RFC after building it

An RFC for a decision already made is a press release. Reviewers can tell, and they stop engaging with your future RFCs too.

✗ Giving the effort number as the date

"Five days" of work across three change windows and a quota request is four to six weeks of calendar. Always give both, and lead with the calendar date.

✓ Delegate the work you are best at

The tasks you could do fastest are the best stretch tasks for others, because you can review them most precisely. Keep the work only you can do: framing, deciding, unblocking.

✓ Make on-call health a standing agenda item

Ten minutes a month on pages per shift and noise rate, with the client present, is how alert fixes get prioritised above features before people quit.
