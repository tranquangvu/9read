Resilience & Disaster Recovery

Everything on AWS fails eventually: tasks, nodes, whole Availability Zones, occasionally a regional service, and far more often your own deploys and your own DELETE statements. This module is about deciding, with numbers, how much of that the clinic booking platform must survive and how fast it must recover. You will design multi-AZ properly (static stability, not just "Multi-AZ: true"), set RTO and RPO per service, pick a DR strategy and defend its cost, build AWS Backup with cross-region copies and restore tests in Terraform, and prove the whole thing with AWS FIS experiments and game days.

Why it matters in production: nearly every team believes it has DR because backups are switched on. Then the day comes and nobody knows the restore takes five hours, the snapshots live in the same account the attacker deleted, the DR region has no ECR images, and the runbook was last tested by someone who left. A clinic that loses a morning of bookings loses patients; one that loses a day of booking data has a regulatory problem. Resilience is a design decision with a price, and the senior engineer's job is to make that price explicit.
Part I · Failure modes on AWS

Design for the failures you will actually get, in the order you will get them

DR conversations jump straight to "what if Singapore goes down?" That is the rarest failure on the list. The common ones are boring and mostly self-inflicted. A senior engineer ranks failure modes by likelihood times blast radius, and spends money in that order.

The failure-domain ladder

Each rung is a larger, rarer failure with a different defence. Note that the two most frequent rungs, bad changes and data corruption, are not solved by any amount of multi-AZ or multi-region: a bad migration replicates to every replica in milliseconds.

Multi-AZ done properly means static stability

"Multi-AZ" on its own only means "has resources in more than one AZ". The property you want is static stability: when one AZ fails, the survivors already have enough capacity, so nothing needs to launch, scale or call a control plane to recover. During an AZ event, the EC2 and ECS APIs are exactly what is busiest, and capacity in the healthy AZs is what everyone else is also trying to get.

The arithmetic: peak needs 12 tasks. To survive the loss of one of three AZs without scaling, the other two must hold 12, so each AZ runs 6 and the service runs 18. That is 50% over peak, and it is the real price of a 99.9% SLO. With two AZs you would need 100% headroom, which is why we always use three.

Grey failures and zonal shift

Real AZ events are rarely clean. More often one AZ is partly broken: packet loss, slow EBS, elevated latency to RDS. Health checks pass, so the ALB keeps sending a third of traffic there. Amazon Application Recovery Controller (ARC) zonal shift lets you tell the ALB and EKS to stop using an AZ for a set time, and zonal autoshift lets AWS do it for you when it detects an impairment. Both only help if the other two AZs can carry the load, which is why static stability comes first. 

# move booking-api's ALB traffic away from AZ apse1-az1 for 2 hours (auto-expires)
aws arc-zonal-shift start-zonal-shift \
  --resource-identifier arn:aws:elasticloadbalancing:ap-southeast-1:444455556666:loadbalancer/app/clinic-prod-api/50dc6c495c0c9188 \
  --away-from apse1-az1 --expires-in 2h \
  --comment "INC-20261102 elevated latency in az1"
{
    "zonalShiftId": "a3f1c2d4-...",
    "awayFrom": "apse1-az1",

Deep health checks make AZ failures worse. If /healthz queries Postgres, a 90-second RDS failover marks every task in every AZ unhealthy at once. The ALB then fails open and sends traffic to all of them anyway, while ECS starts killing and replacing tasks that were fine. Keep the load balancer health check shallow (process up, can serve HTTP) and let the SLO alerts report dependency failures.

Dependency failure at the infrastructure level

The application owns retries, circuit breakers and fallbacks (see intern SD10). The infrastructure owns the settings that make those patterns work or break them. Review these whenever you review an ECS or EKS change, because they are invisible until the day a dependency slows down.

Part II · RTO, RPO & DR strategies

Choose the DR strategy from RTO, RPO and a price the client accepts

Disaster recovery covers the failures multi-AZ cannot: losing the region, losing the account, or losing the data. There are four standard strategies, and each one is a point on a curve where faster recovery costs more money and more operational effort. Your job is to place each service on that curve on purpose and write down why.

RTO and RPO, per service, from the business

RTO (recovery time objective) is how long the service may be down after a disaster. RPO (recovery point objective) is how much recent data you may lose, measured in time. Neither is an engineering choice. Ask the business: "If online booking is gone for four hours, what happens? If we lose the last hour of bookings, what happens?" For the clinic, front desks can take bookings by phone for a morning, but patients turning up for appointments the system forgot is unacceptable.

Data / serviceRPORTO (region loss)Reasoning
Bookings + patients (RDS)≤ 5 min≤ 1 hLost bookings mean patients turned away; phones cover a short outage
Uploaded referral PDFs (S3)≤ 15 min≤ 4 hNeeded at the appointment, not at booking time
Reminder queue (SQS)Rebuildable≤ 4 hThe worker regenerates reminders from the bookings table
Sessions + slot cache (Valkey)None neededWith the APIPatients log in again; cache warms in minutes
Accidental delete / corruption≤ 5 min, any point in 35 days≤ 2 hPITR restore to a new instance, then copy the damaged rows back

Notice the last row: the most likely "disaster" is a bad migration or a mistaken UPDATE, and its recovery is a restore within the region, not a failover. A plan that only covers region loss has missed the case you will actually face.

Part III · Backups & replication

A backup you have not restored is a hope, not a backup

AWS Backup gives you one place to define what is backed up, how often, how long it is kept, where copies go and who can delete them, across RDS, S3, EBS, EFS and DynamoDB. Three properties matter more than the schedule: the copies must survive the loss of the account, nobody (including an attacker with admin) may delete them early, and restores must be tested automatically.

AWS Backup plans in Terraform

Select resources by tag rather than by ARN, so a new database tagged backup = "gold" is protected the day it is created. Continuous backup gives point-in-time recovery for RDS and S3 with a 5-minute RPO for up to 35 days, which is what meets the clinic's in-region RPO.

clinic-infra/prod/backup/main.tf 
resource "aws_backup_plan" "clinic" {
  name = "clinic-prod"
  rule {
    rule_name                = "daily-with-pitr"
    target_vault_name        = aws_backup_vault.prod.name
    schedule                 = "cron(0 18 * * ? *)"     # 01:00 ICT
    enable_continuous_backup = true                     # PITR, RPO 5 min, max 35 days
    lifecycle { delete_after = 35 }
    copy_action {                                        # lands as a snapshot in Sydney
      destination_vault_arn = aws_backup_vault.dr.arn    # provider = aws.apse2
      lifecycle { delete_after = 90 }
    }
  }
  rule {
    rule_name         = "monthly-keep-1y"
    target_vault_name = aws_backup_vault.prod.name
    schedule          = "cron(0 18 1 * ? *)"
    lifecycle { delete_after = 365 }
  }
}
resource "aws_backup_selection" "gold" {
  name         = "tier-gold"
  plan_id      = aws_backup_plan.clinic.id
  iam_role_arn = aws_iam_role.backup.arn
  selection_tag {
    type  = "STRINGEQUALS"
    key   = "backup"
    value = "gold"                                  # RDS clinic-db, S3 uploads bucket
  }
}
RDS automated backups and AWS Backup continuous backup are not the same switch. If AWS Backup manages PITR for an instance, the RDS backup retention setting is taken over by the plan; changing backup_retention_period in the aws_db_instance resource afterwards fights the plan and shows up as perpetual drift. Pick one owner (we use AWS Backup) and set lifecycle { ignore_changes = [backup_retention_period] } on the instance.

Make backups survive an attacker with admin

Ransomware on AWS does not encrypt disks. It deletes snapshots and backups with stolen admin credentials, then deletes the data. Defend against it with Vault Lock in compliance mode (after a grace period, no one, including the root user and AWS, can shorten retention or delete recovery points) and a copy in a different account, encrypted with a key that account owns. The account layout is in AR1; AWS Backup's logically air-gapped vaults are a managed version of the same idea.

clinic-infra/prod/backup/vault.tf 
resource "aws_backup_vault" "prod" {
  name        = "clinic-prod"
  kms_key_arn = aws_kms_key.backup.arn          # customer-managed key, not aws/backup
}
resource "aws_backup_vault_lock_configuration" "prod" {
  backup_vault_name   = aws_backup_vault.prod.name
  min_retention_days  = 7
  max_retention_days  = 400
  changeable_for_days = 3     # after 3 days this is PERMANENT: test in dev first
}
# proof for an auditor or a client security review
aws backup describe-backup-vault --backup-vault-name clinic-prod \
  --query '{Locked:Locked,Min:MinRetentionDays,Max:MaxRetentionDays,LockDate:LockDate}'
{ "Locked": true, "Min": 7, "Max": 400, "LockDate": "2026-09-17T08:00:00+07:00" }

Restore testing, automatically, every week

AWS Backup restore testing picks a recent recovery point on a schedule, restores it into a scratch resource, lets you validate it, records how long it took, and deletes it. That gives you two things nobody has otherwise: proof that backups restore, and a measured restore time to compare with your RTO. The clinic's 180 GB database restores in about 38 minutes, which is the number the 2-hour corruption RTO is built on.

clinic-infra/prod/backup/restore-testing.tf 
resource "aws_backup_restore_testing_plan" "weekly" {
  name                = "clinic_weekly_restore"
  schedule_expression = "cron(0 20 ? * SAT *)"     # Sunday 03:00 ICT
  recovery_point_selection {
    algorithm             = "LATEST_WITHIN_WINDOW"
    include_vaults        = [aws_backup_vault.prod.arn]
    recovery_point_types  = ["SNAPSHOT", "CONTINUOUS"]
    selection_window_days = 7
  }
}
resource "aws_backup_restore_testing_selection" "rds" {
  name                      = "clinic_db"
  restore_testing_plan_name = aws_backup_restore_testing_plan.weekly.name
  protected_resource_type   = "RDS"
  iam_role_arn              = aws_iam_role.restore_test.arn
  protected_resource_arns   = [aws_db_instance.clinic.arn]
  validation_window_hours   = 2                       # time for the check below to report
  restore_metadata_overrides = { dbsubnetgroupname = "clinic-restore-test" }
}
# validation Lambda (triggered by the restore job event) runs these and reports back
psql "$RESTORED_DSN" -Atc "select count(*), max(created_at) from bookings"
1842213|2026-11-01 02:57:41+07
# max(created_at) within 5 min of the recovery point = RPO met; then:
aws backup put-restore-validation-result --restore-job-id "$JOB_ID" \
  --validation-status SUCCESSFUL --validation-status-message "rows=1842213 lag=3m19s"

Part IV · Game days & chaos engineering

Resilience is a claim until you have broken it on purpose

Every design in Parts I–III is a hypothesis: "if the RDS writer fails, patients see under two minutes of errors". Chaos engineering tests those hypotheses with controlled, observable failures, starting small and in staging and moving to production once you trust your stop conditions. AWS Fault Injection Service (FIS) runs the experiments; the SLO from SR1 tells you whether the hypothesis held.

Write the hypothesis before the experiment

An experiment without a written hypothesis is just breaking things. Each one names the steady state (an SLI and its normal value), the fault, the expected effect with a number, the stop condition, and the blast radius. If you cannot predict the outcome, you are not ready to run it in production.

clinic-infra/chaos/experiments/rds-failover.md 
Steady state:   booking-api availability SLI ≥ 99.9% (5 min), p99 < 300 ms, 4 req/s
Fault:          force Multi-AZ failover of clinic-db (reboot with failover)
Hypothesis:     5xx for < 120 s, < 500 failed requests (< 5% of 30-day budget),
                no duplicate bookings, worker resumes without manual action
Stop condition: CloudWatch alarm booking-api-5xx-gt-5pct (1 min) → FIS stops
Blast radius:   prod, all patients; run Tue 14:00 ICT (low traffic), IC on standby
Abort plan:     none needed for failover; if the new writer is unhealthy, fail back

AWS FIS experiment templates as code

Templates live in Terraform next to the infrastructure they break, with an IAM role that can only perform the listed actions on tagged resources. The stop condition is the most important line: FIS halts the experiment the moment the alarm fires.

clinic-infra/prod/chaos/fis.tf 
resource "aws_fis_experiment_template" "rds_failover" {
  description = "Force clinic-db Multi-AZ failover; expect < 120 s of 5xx"
  role_arn    = aws_iam_role.fis.arn               # AWSFaultInjectionSimulatorRDSAccess only
  stop_condition {
    source = "aws:cloudwatch:alarm"
    value  = aws_cloudwatch_metric_alarm.api_5xx_gt_5pct.arn
  }
  action {
    name      = "failover-writer"
    action_id = "aws:rds:reboot-db-instances"
    parameter {
      key   = "forceFailover"
      value = "true"
    }
    target {
      key   = "DBInstances"
      value = "clinic-db"
    }
  }
  target {
    name           = "clinic-db"
    resource_type  = "aws:rds:db"
    selection_mode = "ALL"
    resource_arns  = [aws_db_instance.clinic.arn]
  }
  tags = { Name = "rds-failover", Owner = "devops" }
}

This is the closest you can get to a real zonal event, and it tests static stability, health-check timing and zonal shift together. Run it in staging first; in production, only after the RDS and task-kill experiments pass.

No stop condition, no experiment. FIS will happily run a template with an empty stop-condition list. An AZ isolation that coincides with a real deploy problem, with nothing to halt it, is an outage you caused. Our CI policy (SE2 checks) rejects any aws_fis_experiment_template in a prod workspace whose stop_condition is missing or points to "none".

Running a game day

A game day is a scheduled session where the team runs several experiments and practises the incident process from SR2 at the same time. The on-call engineer should not be told which fault is coming: you are testing detection and response as well as the architecture.

  • Roles: a game master who runs FIS, the on-call responder, an IC, and one observer who writes down every surprise.
  • Order: smallest blast radius first (kill one task), then dependencies (Valkey, RDS), then the AZ. Stop the day after the first unexpected result and fix it.
  • Cadence: quarterly in production for task, RDS and Valkey faults; AZ isolation twice a year; a full regional DR drill once a year.
  • Output: a results table, action items tracked like postmortem items, and updated runbooks with a new "last tested" date.

If that is too long for your SLO, shorten the interval or enable zonal autoshift; both have costs, and now you can measure them.

The yearly regional DR drill

Once a year, fail the whole stack over to Sydney for real, using the pilot-light runbook, during an agreed low-traffic window with the clinic informed. Time every step against the sequence in Part II. The only result that counts is the measured RTO and RPO, compared with the targets the client signed.

  • Backup copies go to another region and are readable there (KMS)
  • RTO/RPO numbers come from drills, not from documentation
Red flags in AI-written resilience code: a cross-region read replica described as "the backup"; skip_final_snapshot = true and deletion_protection = false on a production database; backups encrypted with the default aws/backup key and then "copied cross-account" (they cannot be shared); delete_marker_replication enabled on a DR bucket; a single NAT gateway; FIS templates with selection_mode = "ALL" on untagged resources; and health checks that query the database.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Backups in the same blast radius

Snapshots in the same account, same region, same KMS key as the data protect against nothing an attacker or a bad Terraform destroy can do. Copy out, and lock.

✗ Counting on autoscaling during an AZ event

Everyone in the region scales at once, APIs throttle and capacity runs short. Pre-provision for the loss of one AZ; let autoscaling handle growth, not failure.

✗ A DR region that has never served traffic

Missing images, an expired certificate, a quota of 0 Fargate vCPUs: you find these in the first real drill, so make sure the first real drill is not the disaster.

✓ Check DR-region quotas yearly

Request the Fargate vCPU and RDS quotas you need in Sydney before the drill. Quotas and limits are covered in SR4.

✓ Publish measured RTO and RPO

Put the last drill's numbers and date in the service README. "RTO 52 min, measured 2026-08-23" ends arguments that "should be fine" never will.
