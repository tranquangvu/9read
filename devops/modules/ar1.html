Well-Architected Design & Multi-account Landing Zones

Up to now you have built services inside accounts someone else designed. This module is about designing the accounts, and the frame you use to judge any AWS architecture. It covers the AWS Well-Architected Framework and how to run a review that produces real work rather than a score, the multi-account strategy behind our eight gol-* accounts, OU design and guardrails, Control Tower and account vending from code, centralised networking with Transit Gateway and RAM, centralised logging, and the architecture decision records that keep all of it explainable two years later.

Why it matters in production: account structure is the hardest thing in AWS to change later. A client that started with "everything in one account" eventually finds that a developer's test script deleted a production queue, that one runaway Lambda hit the account's concurrency quota and throttled the API, and that nobody can say what the staging environment costs. Splitting a live account takes months of migration. Getting the landing zone right in week one takes days, and it is the decision a senior engineer is expected to get right.
Part I · Well-Architected

A Well-Architected review is a risk register, not a scorecard

The AWS Well-Architected Framework is a set of questions, grouped into six pillars, that AWS solution architects use to review a workload. Its value is not the answers AWS considers best. It is that the questions force you to state trade-offs you were making implicitly. At L3 you are expected to run these reviews for clients, defend the trade-offs, and turn the result into a prioritised backlog.

The six pillars, translated into questions about the clinic platform

PillarThe question that mattersRisk we found on the clinic platformWhere it's taught
Operational excellenceCan we change it safely and know when it is unhealthy?No runbook for a stuck SQS queue; alarms on CPU, not on booking errorsSR1, SR2
SecurityWho can do what, and would we notice misuse?Developers held admin in gol-prod "for debugging"SE1, AW1
ReliabilityWhat happens when an AZ, a dependency or a deploy fails?Redis was single-node; a failover would log every patient outSR3
Performance efficiencyAre we using the right resource types, and do we measure?x86 Fargate tasks where Graviton would be ~20% cheaper per requestSR4
Cost optimisationDo we know what each environment and service costs?Three NAT gateways in gol-dev costing more than its computeAR2
SustainabilityAre we running things nobody uses?Staging ran 24/7 for a team that works 08:00–19:00 ICTAR2

Notice that each risk is specific, has an owner module, and could become a ticket. "Improve reliability" is not a finding. "Redis is single-node in prod; a node replacement logs out every active patient; fix: Multi-AZ replication group, about USD 90/month" is.

Running a review with the Well-Architected Tool

Define the workload in the Well-Architected Tool (one per system and environment that matters, here clinic-booking-prod), add the lenses that apply (the core framework, plus for us the Container Build lens; the SaaS and Serverless lenses exist for other shapes), and answer the questions with the team in two 2-hour sessions. Each answer marks a high-risk issue (HRI) or medium-risk issue (MRI) where a best practice is missing. Save a milestone at the end so the next review can show progress.

Run the review with the people who will do the work. A review answered by one architect from documentation produces optimistic answers. A review with the on-call engineer in the room produces "actually, we've never restored that backup". Budget four hours of the team's time; it is cheaper than the first incident it prevents.

Trade-offs between pillars are the real content

Almost every architecture decision improves one pillar at another's expense. A senior engineer names the trade-off explicitly, with a number, and lets the client choose with their eyes open. These four come up on nearly every client platform we build.

DecisionGainsCostsOur default

Part II · Multi-account strategy & OU design

The account is the only hard boundary AWS gives you

Inside one account, every isolation mechanism is a policy someone can get wrong: an IAM condition, a resource tag, a VPC security group. Between accounts, nothing is shared unless someone explicitly allows it. That is why AWS's own guidance, and ours, is many small accounts grouped into organizational units (OUs) by how they should be governed. Organizations, SCP syntax and Identity Center basics are in AW1; this Part is the design.

What an account boundary buys you that a VPC does not

ConcernSeparate VPCs, one accountSeparate accounts
IAM blast radiusAn admin in "dev" is an admin in prodPermissions do not cross unless a role trust says so
Service quotasA dev load test can use up Lambda concurrency or the EIP limit for prodEach account has its own quotas
Cost visibilityDepends on every resource being tagged perfectlyThe bill is split by account automatically (AR2)
GuardrailsOne SCP set for everythingDifferent SCPs per OU: strict for prod, loose for sandbox
Compliance scopeAuditors scope the whole accountOnly accounts that hold regulated data are in scope (SE3)
CleanupFind and delete resources one by oneClose the account

The cost of more accounts is mostly automation: each one needs a baseline, roles for CI, and a place in the network. Once vending is automated (Part III), a new account costs about 20 minutes of pipeline time and nothing per month until it runs workloads.

The Golden Owl OU structure

Design OUs around policy, not around the company org chart. Accounts that need the same guardrails go in the same OU. Ours follows AWS's recommended layout, scaled down for a client platform: a Security OU, an Infrastructure OU for shared services, a Workloads OU split into non-prod and prod, a Sandbox OU, and a Suspended OU where accounts wait to be closed.

Keep the management account empty. SCPs never apply to the management account, and anyone with admin there can detach every guardrail in the organization. No workloads, no CI deploy roles, no "just this one Lambda". Delegate every service that supports it to gol-security or gol-shared, and alert on any console login to gol-management.

Guardrails per OU: SCPs, RCPs and what each OU gets

Service control policies cap what principals in an account can do. Resource control policies (RCPs, available since late 2024) cap what can be done to resources in an account, including by principals from outside the organization, which is how you enforce a data perimeter on S3, KMS, SQS and Secrets Manager. Attach broad policies high in the tree and specific ones low, and keep each policy to one purpose so a failed change is easy to trace.

Attached atPolicyEffect
Rootdeny-leave-org, protect-security-baseline (SE1), deny-root-userNo account escapes, nobody disables detection, root user unusable
Root (RCP)data-perimeter-org-onlyS3 and KMS reachable only by principals in o-a1b2c3d4e5 or AWS services on our behalf
Workloads OUregion-allowlistOnly ap-southeast-1, plus global services
Prod OUdeny-destructive-without-pipelineDeleting RDS, S3 buckets or KMS keys only via the apply role
Sandbox OUsandbox-limitsNo Organizations or IAM user creation, small instance types only, budget action at USD 300

The NotAction list is where region SCPs break things: ACM certificates for CloudFront must be created in us-east-1 (AW7), so acm:* and wafv2:* are exempt here, and IAM, STS and Organizations are global services that live in us-east-1. Miss one and the failure shows up weeks later as an unexplained AccessDenied in a pipeline. Control Tower's own Region deny control maintains an equivalent list for you, which is one argument for using it.

The Prod OU policy is the one that changes daily behaviour, so it is worth reading closely. It does not stop anyone deleting a database. It stops anyone except the pipeline's apply role from doing so, which turns "someone ran the wrong command in the wrong terminal" into "someone merged a reviewed PR that deletes the database", a much rarer event with a much better audit trail.

Part III · Control Tower & account vending

Vend accounts from code, never from a console

A landing zone is the pre-built, governed starting point every account inherits: the OU tree, identity, logging, guardrails and a baseline inside each account. You can build one by hand in Terraform, or let AWS Control Tower build and maintain the core of it. For client platforms we use Control Tower for the parts AWS maintains well (enrolment, the org trail, Config, Identity Center integration, its control library) and Terraform for everything specific to us.

What Control Tower sets up, and what it leaves to you

AreaControl Tower providesWe add in Terraform
Core accountsA Security OU with the Log Archive and Audit accounts (ours: gol-log-archive, gol-security)Infrastructure, Workloads, Sandbox and Suspended OUs
LoggingOrganization CloudTrail and Config delivery to the Log ArchiveObject Lock, retention, data events, Flow Logs (SE1, SE3)
IdentityIAM Identity Center wired to the org, default permission setsOur permission sets and group assignments (AW1)
ControlsA catalogue of preventive (SCP/RCP), detective (Config) and proactive (CloudFormation hook) controlsWhich optional controls are enabled on which OU; our own SCPs
DetectionNothing beyond ConfigGuardDuty, Security Hub, Inspector delegated to gol-security

Control Tower changes the rules for the OUs it governs. Accounts must be enrolled to receive its baseline; OUs must be registered; and a landing zone update (a few times a year) re-applies its resources. Never edit Control Tower-managed resources (the aws-controltower-* stacks, roles and SCPs) by hand or from Terraform. That causes drift, which Control Tower reports and which blocks enrolment until you repair the landing zone.

Account vending: a PR creates an account

New accounts arrive more often than you expect: a new client environment, a performance-test account, a sandbox per trainee cohort. Each must come out identical: enrolled, in the right OU, with the baseline applied and the right people assigned. For an organization our size we vend through Control Tower's Account Factory, driven from Terraform in clinic-infra/org. Above about 15 to 20 accounts, or with many teams requesting accounts, Account Factory for Terraform (AFT) is worth its extra moving parts (its own management account, CodePipeline and a set of repos for requests and customisations).

Part IV · Central networking, logging & decisions

Share the network and the logs, never the blast radius

Once workloads live in separate accounts, some things should be shared again on purpose: the network path to the clinic's offices, the egress to the internet, and the log archive. Shared things become dependencies for every account, so the design rule is that sharing is one-directional and explicit. VPC fundamentals and the peering vs Transit Gateway basics are in AW2; this Part is how the pieces are arranged across the organization.

A Transit Gateway hub in gol-shared, shared with RAM

The Transit Gateway lives in gol-shared and is shared to the Workloads OU with AWS Resource Access Manager (RAM). Each workload account attaches its own VPC. Isolation comes from TGW route tables, not from the attachment: workload VPCs are associated with tgw-rtb-workloads, which only knows routes to the shared VPC (10.0.0.0/16) and the clinic HQ VPN (10.100.0.0/14). Dev therefore cannot reach prod, even though both are attached to the same gateway.

clinic-infra/network/shared/tgw.tf — runs in gol-shared 
resource "aws_ec2_transit_gateway" "hub" {
  description                     = "gol hub ap-southeast-1"
  amazon_side_asn                 = 64512
  auto_accept_shared_attachments  = "disable"   # every attachment is accepted by Terraform here
  default_route_table_association = "disable"   # no implicit "everyone sees everyone"
  default_route_table_propagation = "disable"
}

resource "aws_ram_resource_share" "tgw" {
  name                      = "gol-tgw-hub"
  allow_external_principals = false             # inside o-a1b2c3d4e5 only
}
resource "aws_ram_resource_association" "tgw" {
  resource_share_arn = aws_ram_resource_share.tgw.arn
  resource_arn       = aws_ec2_transit_gateway.hub.arn
}
resource "aws_ram_principal_association" "workloads_ou" {
  resource_share_arn = aws_ram_resource_share.tgw.arn
  principal          = "arn:aws:organizations::999988887777:ou/o-a1b2c3d4e5/ou-a1b2-wrk8x2pq"
}

resource "aws_ec2_transit_gateway_route_table" "workloads" {
  transit_gateway_id = aws_ec2_transit_gateway.hub.id
  tags               = { Name = "tgw-rtb-workloads" }
}

Turning off default association and propagation is the line that matters. With the defaults on, every new attachment joins one route table that routes to every other attachment, and the first time someone attaches a sandbox VPC it can reach production. A TGW costs about USD 0.07 per attachment-hour (roughly USD 50 a month each) plus USD 0.02 per GB processed, so five attachments are about USD 250 a month before traffic.

Choosing how accounts share a network

PatternHow it worksRight whenWrong when
VPC per account + TGWEach account owns its VPC; TGW route tables decide reachabilityOur default: environments isolated, shared services and VPN reachableTwo VPCs total (peering is cheaper)
Shared VPC (RAM subnet sharing)A network account owns one VPC and shares subnets; other accounts launch into themMany small accounts of one environment that a central team networksYou need environment isolation at the network layer; SGs and NACLs become shared concerns
VPC peeringDirect, non-transitive link between two VPCsTwo or three VPCs, high bandwidth, no hub feeMore than about four VPCs: the mesh grows as n²
VPC Lattice / PrivateLinkService-to-service exposure without routing whole networksExposing one API to another account or a clientYou need general network reachability (VPN, admin access)
Centralised egress is a later optimisation. Routing every account's internet traffic through NAT gateways and a firewall in gol-shared saves NAT gateways and gives one inspection point, but adds TGW processing charges to every byte and makes gol-shared a dependency for every outbound call. Below about five workload VPCs, keep NAT local to each VPC and revisit when the NAT bill (AR2) says otherwise.

Centralised logging: one archive, many writers

Control Tower already sends CloudTrail and Config to gol-log-archive, and SE1 hardens that. Add the network and edge logs that investigations need, VPC Flow Logs and ALB access logs, written directly from each account to a bucket in the archive account, so there is never a local copy that someone in the workload account controls. Application logs and metrics go to the observability stack (OB1, OB3), not the archive.

modules/account-baseline/flow-logs.tf — every account writes to the archive 
resource "aws_flow_log" "vpc" {
  vpc_id               = var.vpc_id
  traffic_type         = "ALL"
  log_destination_type = "s3"
  log_destination      = "arn:aws:s3:::gol-log-archive-flowlogs-666677778888"   # cross-account bucket

}

Architecture decision records for infra

Every decision in this module (OU layout, TGW instead of peering, local NAT, an accepted Well-Architected risk) will be questioned by the next engineer, the next auditor or the client's new CTO. An ADR is a short Markdown file in the repo that records the context, the decision, the alternatives and the consequences, at the time it was made. It takes 20 minutes to write and saves the two-hour meeting where people reconstruct why. Number them, never edit an accepted one; supersede it with a new ADR instead. Longer design docs and RFCs are AR5.

clinic-infra/docs/adr/0007-transit-gateway-hub.md 
# ADR-0007: Transit Gateway hub in gol-shared instead of VPC peering

Status: Accepted (2026-03-12) · Deciders: platform lead, security owner

## Context
Four VPCs (shared, dev, staging, prod) need to reach CI runners and the clinic HQ VPN
(10.100.0.0/14). Prod must not be reachable from dev or staging. A fifth (perf) is planned.

## Decision
One TGW in gol-shared, shared to the Workloads OU via RAM. Default association and
propagation disabled. Workload VPCs use tgw-rtb-workloads (shared + HQ routes only).

## Alternatives considered
- Peering: 6 links today, 10 with perf; no transitive VPN routing. Rejected.
- Shared VPC: removes network isolation between environments. Rejected.

## Consequences
+ Adding a VPC is one attachment and one association.
- About USD 250/month fixed cost; gol-shared becomes a dependency for HQ access.
Revisit if: more than 2 regions, or monthly TGW processing exceeds USD 300.
Score-4 move: end every Well-Architected review and every landing zone change with the ADRs it produced, linked from the PR. After a year the docs/adr/ folder is the most useful onboarding document the platform has: our clinic-infra repo has 23, and new engineers read them in their first week.

  • TGW default association/propagation stay disabled
  • Nothing edits Control Tower-managed stacks, roles or SCPs
Red flags in AI-written landing zone code: workloads or CI roles in the management account; an SCP attached to the root that has never been tested on a sandbox OU; a region-deny SCP without exemptions for IAM, STS, CloudFront or ACM; TGW with default route table association enabled; RAM shares with allow_external_principals = true; Terraform that modifies aws-controltower-* resources; account emails on a personal mailbox; and close_on_deletion = true on production accounts.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ OUs that mirror the org chart

Teams reorganise; guardrails don't. An OU per team means prod and dev of the same team share policies. Group by the policy an account needs.

✗ Testing SCPs on the root

A bad deny on the root breaks every account at once, including the pipelines you need to roll it back. Attach new SCPs to a test OU with one sandbox account first.

✗ A review that ends as a PDF

Well-Architected findings that never become tickets or ADRs are forgotten within a sprint. Close the review only when every HRI has an owner or an accepted-risk record.

✓ Plus-addressed account emails on a team mailbox

Every account's root email is aws+<account>@ a shared, monitored mailbox. When the engineer who created the account leaves, you can still recover root.

✓ Plan the CIDRs for accounts you don't have yet

Reserve ranges for future environments and regions now (AW2). Overlapping CIDRs are the one thing a TGW cannot route around.
