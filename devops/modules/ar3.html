Platform Engineering & Internal Developer Platforms

By L3 you have built the clinic platform yourself: VPCs, EKS, Argo CD, pipelines, dashboards. The next problem is that you are the only person who can use it. This module is about turning what you built into a product that other teams use without you: team topologies, the platform as a product, golden paths, Backstage as the front door, self-service infrastructure through templates and versioned modules, and the metrics that tell you whether any of it is working. Half of the module is about what to decline to build.

Why it matters in production: a DevOps team that hand-builds every new service becomes the queue every release waits in. At Golden Owl the third clinic client asked for a new notify-worker and waited eleven working days: four tickets, three Terraform PRs, one Argo CD Application written by hand and a missing alarm that was found during the first incident. A golden path turns that into one form and one review, with the alarms, the cost tags and the SLOs already in place. A badly scoped platform does the opposite: two engineers spend a quarter building a portal nobody opens.
Part I · Team topologies

A platform team exists to reduce other teams' cognitive load, or it shouldn't exist

"Platform engineering" is a reorganisation before it is a technology. The question is not "should we install Backstage?" but "which work should product teams stop having to think about, and who owns it instead?" Get the team shape wrong and no tool will save you. Get it right and a wiki page plus a Terraform module can be a perfectly good first platform.

Four team types, three interaction modes

The vocabulary from Team Topologies is the one your clients and your managers will use, so use it precisely. The useful part is not the four boxes but the interaction modes: a platform team that is in permanent "collaboration" with every product team is a shared services team with a new name, and it will not scale.

Team typeClinic exampleDefault interaction with others
Stream-alignedBooking team (booking-api, booking-web), notifications teamConsumes the platform X-as-a-Service: self-service, no ticket
PlatformTwo to four DevOps engineers owning clinic-infra modules, EKS, Argo CD, pipelinesX-as-a-Service by default; collaboration only for a time-boxed discovery (2–6 weeks)
EnablingA senior who pairs with the booking team for a sprint on SLOs or KarpenterFacilitating: teaches, then leaves
Complicated-subsystemThe team owning the HL7/FHIR integration with hospital systemsX-as-a-Service through a narrow API

Write the interaction mode down for every pair of teams and put an end date on every "collaboration". Our platform team's collaboration with the booking team on the first EKS migration ran for five weeks. After that the booking team consumed the platform through templates, and the platform team's Slack mentions from booking dropped from about 40 a week to 6.

What the platform takes away from product teams

Cognitive load is the budget. A stream-aligned team of six can hold its domain, its code and a small slice of infrastructure in their heads. Every layer the platform owns end to end is one the team can stop learning. Every layer the platform "provides" but the team still has to debug is still on their bill.

A practical test: list the last ten production incidents for a product team and mark each one by the layer that caused it. If more than three sit in the platform's box and the product team had to fix them, the platform is leaking load back to its users. That number is a better roadmap input than any feature request.

When you need a platform team, and when you don't

A platform team is overhead until there is enough repeated work to amortise it. For a single client with two services, the honest answer is a shared Terraform module and a reusable workflow, owned by whoever wrote them. Use these thresholds as a starting argument, not a law:

SituationWhat to buildWhat to decline
1–2 stream teams, under 10 servicesVersioned modules (IA2), reusable workflows (CI1), a README "how to add a service"Backstage, a dedicated platform team, any custom CLI
3–6 stream teams, 10–40 servicesA platform team of 2–4, one golden path, a service catalog, a template for the most common service shapeMultiple runtimes on the golden path, a self-built portal UI
7+ teams or several clients on shared infraBackstage with catalog, templates, TechDocs and scorecards; account vending (AR1); a platform roadmap with fundingBuilding your own orchestrator or Kubernetes abstraction layer
The cheapest platform is a convention. Before you write any tooling, write the one-page "how a service is shaped here" document: one repo per app, chart in clinic-gitops/apps/<service>, tags service/team/env/cost-center, /healthz on 8080. If teams can't agree on that page, a template will only automate the disagreement.
Part II · Platform as a product

Pave the road people already walk, and decline the rest

A product has users, a roadmap, a support model and the freedom to say no. Treat the platform the same way. The most common failure is building the platform the DevOps team finds interesting (a multi-cloud abstraction, a custom portal) instead of the one the product teams need (a new service in a day, a staging database on request).

User research: read the ticket queue first

Your users have already told you what they need. They told you in tickets and Slack threads. Export the last quarter of platform requests, group them, and count. The top three groups are your first roadmap. Here is the clinic platform queue for Q2 2026:

platform-requests-q2.sql — Jira export loaded into Athena 
SELECT category,
       count(*)                                        AS tickets,
       round(avg(date_diff('hour', created, resolved)) / 24.0, 1) AS avg_days,
       sum(engineer_hours)                              AS platform_hours
FROM   platform.requests
WHERE  created BETWEEN DATE '2026-04-01' AND DATE '2026-06-30'
GROUP BY category ORDER BY platform_hours DESC LIMIT 6;

Rank by platform hours and wait time, not by ticket count. 41 secret changes look like the biggest pain, but they take 0.6 days each and are better fixed by giving teams scoped access to their own Secrets Manager path (CI3). The 9 new-service requests cost 131 hours and an 8-day wait, which is where a template pays for itself in one quarter.

The golden path: one opinionated way, fully wired

A golden path is the supported, documented, pre-wired way to do one common thing. It is opinionated on purpose: one language template per stack, one runtime, one pipeline. Teams may leave it, but then they own what they built. The clinic golden path for "a new HTTP service on EKS" produces every artifact a service needs before its first line of business code:

Measured on the first four services built this way, time from "form submitted" to "first deploy in staging" went from 8.4 days to 5.5 hours, most of which was waiting for PR review.

Saying no: the decline list

The senior skill in platform work is choosing what not to build. Keep a written decline list in the platform README, with the reason and the alternative. A request you decline with a reason and a workaround builds trust. One you accept and deliver half-done costs more than the ticket queue it replaced.

RequestDecisionWhy, and the alternative
"Abstract away Kubernetes completely"DeclineLeaky abstractions fail during incidents. Offer a small Helm values contract and read-only kubectl access instead
"Support Go, Rust and Elixir templates"Decline for nowEvery template is a product to maintain. Support the two stacks with 90% of services (Node.js, Python); others use the generic container path
"A custom web portal for creating RDS"DeclineA Backstage template that opens a Terraform PR gives review, plan and audit for free
"Ephemeral preview environment per PR"Accept, scoped14 tickets and 36 hours last quarter. Build for booking-web only, against shared dev data, auto-deleted after 48 h
"Run our own Vault cluster"DeclineSecrets Manager plus External Secrets covers every current need with no cluster to operate
Thinnest viable platform: the smallest set of APIs, documentation and tools that speeds up the teams using it. For the clinic platform at three teams that was a repo of Terraform modules, one reusable workflow, one Helm chart and a 2-page README. Backstage came nine months later, when the README stopped being enough to find who owned what.

Escape hatches: how to leave the path without breaking it

Every golden path needs a documented way off it, or teams will find an undocumented one. The rule is that a team may override any choice but no guardrail. Choices are things like replica counts, instance families or an extra sidecar. Guardrails are encryption, tagging, OIDC-only credentials, and the review gate on clinic-infra. Write down which is which.

Escape levelHowWho supports it
1 · TuneOverride Helm values or module inputs that are exposedPlatform, fully
2 · ExtendAdd your own Terraform next to the module call, or an extra manifest in the app's gitops folderPlatform supports the module; the team owns the extra
3 · Fork the stepReplace one job of the reusable workflow with your ownTeam; still must pass the SE2 security gates
4 · Off pathOwn runtime, own pipeline, registered in the catalog as golden-path: noneTeam entirely, including on-call; reviewed in the quarterly platform review

At the clinic, 5 of 22 production services are at level 4. Two are legacy and scheduled for migration, three are the HL7 integration that genuinely doesn't fit an HTTP service shape. That is a healthy ratio: an off-path count of zero usually means the path is mandated, not that it fits everyone.

Support model and service levels for the platform itself

If product teams depend on the platform, the platform needs promises: how fast a broken pipeline is looked at, how long a module version is supported, where to ask. Publish them. Unwritten promises become "the DevOps team is slow" in every retro.

Part III · Backstage and self-service infra

The catalog is the foundation; templates are the payoff

Backstage is an open-source framework for building a developer portal. It is a framework, not a product: you run a Node.js app, a Postgres database and a set of plugins, and you own its upgrades. Adopt it for two things first, the software catalog (who owns what, and how it connects) and software templates (self-service scaffolding). Everything else, from TechDocs to scorecards, comes after those two are trusted.

The catalog: ownership as data

Every component describes itself in a catalog-info.yaml next to its code. Backstage discovers these files across the golden-owl org and builds a graph: components, the APIs they provide, the resources they depend on, and the group that owns them. Annotations connect the entity to the plugins that show its Argo CD status, Kubernetes pods and Datadog dashboards.

clinic-app/services/booking-api/catalog-info.yaml 
apiVersion: backstage.io/v1alpha1
kind: Component
metadata:
  name: booking-api
  description: Appointment booking HTTP API
  tags: [nodejs, eks, tier-1]
  annotations:
    github.com/project-slug: golden-owl/clinic-app
    backstage.io/techdocs-ref: dir:.
    argocd/app-name: booking-api-prod                  # links the Argo CD plugin (KS10)
    backstage.io/kubernetes-id: booking-api            # matches the label on the Pods
    datadoghq.com/dashboard-url: https://app.datadoghq.com/dashboard/abc-123
spec:
  type: service
  lifecycle: production
  owner: group:booking-team                            # the one field you must never leave empty
  system: clinic-booking
  providesApis: [booking-api-v2]
  dependsOn:
    - resource:clinic-prod-postgres
    - resource:booking-events-queue
Entity kindClinic examplesWho creates it
Group / Userbooking-team, platformSynced from IAM Identity Center or GitHub teams, never typed by hand
Systemclinic-bookingPlatform team, once per client product
Componentbooking-api, booking-web, booking-workerThe template, at creation time

Measure catalog health before you build anything on top of it. If 30% of components have no owner, every scorecard and on-call lookup built on the catalog will be 30% wrong. A CI check that fails a PR adding a service without a valid catalog-info.yaml moved the clinic catalog from 62% to 100% owned in two weeks.

Software templates: the form is an API

A template is a form (JSON Schema parameters) plus steps (actions that fetch skeleton files, render them and open pull requests). Design the form like a public API: every field you add is one you must support for years. The clinic HTTP service template asks for exactly four things.

platform-templates/http-service/template.yaml — parameters 
apiVersion: scaffolder.backstage.io/v1beta3
kind: Template
metadata:
  name: clinic-http-service
  title: HTTP service on EKS (golden path v3)
spec:
  owner: group:platform
  type: service
  parameters:
    - title: Service
      required: [name, owner, tier]
      properties:
        name:
          type: string
          pattern: '^[a-z][a-z0-9-]{2,24}$'           # becomes a DNS label, an IAM role and a namespace
        owner:
          type: string
          ui:field: OwnerPicker
          ui:options: { catalogFilter: { kind: Group } }
        tier:
          type: string
          enum: [tier-1, tier-2, tier-3]               # drives SLO, replicas, alert routing
        needs:
          type: array
          uniqueItems: true
          items: { type: string, enum: [postgres, sqs, s3-bucket] }
          ui:widget: checkboxes                        # no free text: every option is a supported module
platform-templates/http-service/template.yaml — steps 
  steps:
    - id: render-infra
      name: Render Terraform
      action: fetch:template
      input:
        url: ./skeleton/infra
        targetPath: ./infra
        values: { name: ${{ parameters.name }}, tier: ${{ parameters.tier }}, needs: ${{ parameters.needs }} }
    - id: infra-pr
      name: Open clinic-infra PR
      action: publish:github:pull-request
      input:
        repoUrl: github.com?owner=golden-owl&repo=clinic-infra
        branchName: new-service/${{ parameters.name }}
        title: "feat(${{ parameters.name }}): add service via golden path v3"
        sourcePath: ./infra
        targetPath: services/${{ parameters.name }}   # a PR, never a direct push: review and plan still apply
    # render-app / app-pr and render-gitops / gitops-pr follow the same pattern
  output:
    links:
      - { title: Infra PR,  url: "${{ steps['infra-pr'].output.remoteUrl }}" }
      - { title: GitOps PR, url: "${{ steps['gitops-pr'].output.remoteUrl }}" }
Templates rot the day after you ship them. A scaffolded repo is a fork of the template at that moment. When the golden path moves to v4, the 15 services created from v3 don't move with it. Keep the scaffolded code thin and push everything that must evolve (CI logic, Terraform, Helm defaults) behind a versioned reference: a module ?ref=, a reusable workflow @v3, a chart version. Renovate (CI4) then upgrades them like any other dependency.

The Terraform service module is the real platform API

The Backstage form is the friendly face. The contract that matters is the service module the template renders a call to. Its variables are what product teams may choose; everything else (encryption, tags, alarms, IAM boundaries, log retention) is decided by the platform. The point is the narrowing: four form fields become about a dozen module inputs and roughly 60 AWS resources the team never has to write.

platform-modules/service/variables.tf — the contract 
variable "name" {
  type = string
  validation {
    condition     = can(regex("^[a-z][a-z0-9-]{2,24}$", var.name))
    error_message = "name must be a lowercase DNS label, 3-25 chars."
  }
}
variable "owner" { type = string }                  # Backstage group; becomes the team tag
variable "tier" {
  type = string
  validation {
    condition     = contains(["tier-1", "tier-2", "tier-3"], var.tier)
    error_message = "tier must be tier-1, tier-2 or tier-3."
  }
}
variable "postgres" {
  type    = object({ enabled = bool, schema = optional(string) })
  default = { enabled = false }                    # a schema in the shared cluster, not a new RDS
}
variable "queues"         { type = list(string), default = [] }
variable "monthly_budget" { type = number, default = 150 }  # USD; feeds the AR2 budget alert
# NOT exposed: kms key, log retention, deletion protection, tags, IAM boundary
clinic-infra/services/notify-worker/main.tf — what the template renders

The golden-path pipeline as a reusable workflow

The scaffolded repo gets a ten-line caller. The real pipeline lives in one reusable workflow that the platform team versions and improves for every service at once. Reusable workflow mechanics are in CI1; what matters here is the split.

Budget for it like a service you own: about one engineer-day a month on upgrades and plugin breakage, more in the first quarter. Part IV · Measuring and evolving the platform

Measure lead time and adoption, not portal page views

A platform without numbers loses its funding at the first budget review. The numbers that keep it are the ones a product owner understands: services ship faster, fail less, and cost the platform team fewer hours. Collect them from systems that already exist (GitHub, Argo CD, PagerDuty, Backstage) rather than from a new tracking tool.

Developer experience metrics that survive a budget review

Combine the DORA keys (FN1) with two platform-specific signals, adoption and time to first deploy, plus one perception measure from a short quarterly survey. Compare services on the golden path with those off it; the difference is the platform's value.

Never turn these into per-team targets. The day "lead time" becomes a team OKR, teams split PRs into trivial pieces and the number improves while delivery doesn't. Publish the metrics as a comparison of paths, not a league table of teams, and use them to choose the next platform investment.

Computing them from data you already have

The clinic platform lands GitHub deployment events and Argo CD sync notifications in S3, which Athena reads in the same account used for the CUR tables in AR2. One query gives the golden-path comparison every quarterly review starts with:

dx-lead-time.sql — Athena, platform_metrics database 
WITH changes AS (
  SELECT m.service, m.merged_at, min(s.synced_at) AS live_at
  FROM   github_merges m
  JOIN   argocd_syncs  s ON s.service = m.service
         AND s.env = 'prod' AND s.revision_contains = m.sha   -- first prod sync carrying the commit
  WHERE  m.merged_at >= DATE '2026-07-01'
  GROUP BY m.service, m.merged_at
)
SELECT c.golden_path_version IS NOT NULL                        AS on_path,
       count(*)                                                AS changes,
       approx_percentile(date_diff('minute', merged_at, live_at), 0.5) AS p50_min,
       approx_percentile(date_diff('minute', merged_at, live_at), 0.9) AS p90_min
FROM   changes JOIN catalog_components c ON c.name = changes.service
GROUP BY 1;
on_path  changes  p50_min  p90_min
true     612      42       118
false    97       186      1440

Versioning and deprecating the platform API

Templates, modules, reusable workflows and charts are all public APIs to your users. Apply the same rules as any API: semantic versions, a changelog, a deprecation window you publish in advance, and migration help. Breaking changes ship only in a major version, and the previous major stays supported for six months (SERVICE-LEVELS.md above).

Make deprecation visible where the team already looks. A Terraform check block in the old major version prints a warning on every plan without breaking anything:

platform-modules/service/deprecation.tf — on the v3 branch 
check "golden_path_v3_deprecated" {

The platform roadmap is a product roadmap

Run the platform with the habits of a product team: a named owner (usually the senior DevOps engineer reading this), a quarterly roadmap built from the request data and incident layers in Parts I and II, and a short demo to the product teams at the end of each cycle. Write each roadmap item as an outcome with a number, then argue for it the way AR5 teaches you to argue for an RFC.

  • Q4 outcome: preview environments for booking-web, cutting "preview-environment" tickets from 14 a quarter to 0 and saving about 36 platform hours.
  • Q4 outcome: move the three v2 services to v3 before January, taking adoption of a supported path from 77% to 91%.
  • Declined this quarter: a second runtime (ECS) on the golden path. Two services want it; the cost is a second template, a second chart and double the support. Revisit if a fourth asks.
Score-4 move: run the platform's quarterly review with product managers in the room, show the on-path vs off-path numbers, and let them vote on two of the next three roadmap items. A platform whose users chose its roadmap does not get cut in the next budget round.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Portal first, platform later

Installing Backstage before there is a golden path gives teams a catalog of things they still can't self-serve. Build the module, workflow and chart first; the portal is the front door to them.

✗ Mandating the golden path

A mandated path hides its own flaws because nobody can leave it. Keep it optional, make it the easiest choice, and treat every team that leaves as user research.

✗ Fat templates

Copying 400 lines of pipeline and Terraform into every new repo creates 20 forks that never get upgrades. Scaffold references to versioned building blocks, not copies of them.

✓ Migrate the first services yourselves

The platform team moves the first three services onto a new path or major version. You find the rough edges before your users do, and the migration guide is written from experience.

✓ Put a number on every decline

"Two services want ECS; supporting it costs a template, a chart and about 6 hours a month" is a decision people accept. "We don't support that" is a fight.
