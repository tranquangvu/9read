Product Thinking, Trade-offs & Growth

PP1 was about owning your work. This module is about the judgement around it: why a feature exists, which trade-off you are making, what to work on next, when to say no to a shortcut, a tool or a harmful request, and how to keep growing while an agent writes most of the code.

Why it matters when AI writes the code: an agent will build whatever you point it at, quickly and confidently. It will not ask whether the feature is worth building, whether a simpler version would do, or whether it leaks patient data. When output is nearly free, the scarce skill is choosing the right thing, at the right cost, in the right order, and refusing the wrong thing.
Part I · Product & business thinking

You are paid for outcomes, not for merged PRs

A feature is a bet: the client pays for it because they expect something in their business to change. Knowing what that is lets you build less, build better, and spot when the bet did not pay off.

Know the user, the job, and the number

Before you build, you should be able to fill in three lines. If you can't, the ticket isn't ready, and that is a question for the PM or BA, not a guess for the agent. It takes two minutes and changes what you build.

ticket comment — the three lines, CLINIC-210 
Who:    patients with a booking tomorrow (mostly 30–60, on mobile, Vietnamese)
Job:    "remind me in time, and let me cancel if I can't come"
Number: no-show rate (18% today) · target from the client: ≤ 10% in 2 months
# the "cancel" in the job changes the design: the SMS needs a link, not just text

Ask "what problem are we solving?" without being difficult

The question is only annoying when it sounds like a challenge. Show you read the ticket, offer your understanding, and ask for confirmation. One message, easy to answer.

Sounds difficultSounds like a partner
"Why do we need this?""I think the goal is fewer no-shows. Is that right, or is it also about reception workload?"
"This is a bad idea.""If the goal is X, could we try Y first? It's 1 day instead of 5."
"The ticket is unclear.""Two readings: A or B. I'll build A unless you say otherwise by 3pm."

Every feature has a price tag after launch

The agent makes building cheap, so the build is the smallest part of the cost now. What stays is the maintenance: every screen needs testing on each release, every integration can break, every setting is a support question. When you judge a feature, count all of it:

  • Run cost: SMS fees per message, API quotas, servers, monitoring.
  • Change cost: more code to read and regression-test in every future ticket.
  • Support cost: the receptionist who calls because "the reminder went to the wrong number".
  • Removal cost: once patients rely on it, turning it off is a client conversation.

Offer the simpler version that meets the goal

Once you know the number, you can often hit it with a fraction of the scope. Propose it, don't impose it: the PM owns scope, you own the options and their costs.

Slack to the PM — a cheaper first version 
Ticket asks for a 2-way SMS bot (reply C to cancel, R to reschedule): ~6 days,
plus a provider that supports inbound SMS (~3x the cost per message).

Option: 1-way SMS with a cancel link to our existing cancel page. ~1.5 days.
Covers the goal (fewer no-shows, early cancellations free the slot).
We can add replies later if data shows people want them.

Which do you prefer? Happy to go with the full version if the client asked for it.
# options + cost + link to the goal + the PM decides

Understand how the client makes money

A clinic earns per visit. An empty slot is revenue lost forever; a receptionist on the phone is paid time not spent on patients. Knowing that tells you which bugs are urgent and which features matter.

Business leverWhat it means for your work
Filled slotsBooking and cancel flows are the money path. A bug there is urgent on a Sunday.
Staff timeAnything that saves reception phone calls is valued, even if it looks boring.
Patient trustWrong reminders or leaked records cost more than any feature earns.
DepositsPayment and refund logic needs the most care (SD14).

Close the loop: read the data after release

PP1 checks that your feature works in production. This check asks whether it worked: did the number move? Look two to four weeks later, in analytics, the database or client feedback, and share one line.

ticket comment — 4 weeks after CLINIC-210 
No-show rate: 18% → 12% (4 weeks, 2,140 visits). 9% of SMS led to an early cancel,
and 60% of those slots were rebooked. 31 SMS failed: numbers without +84 prefix.
Suggest: validate phone format at booking (small ticket, CLINIC-233).
Part II · Trade-offs & decision making

There are no best solutions, only chosen costs

"What's the best way?" has no answer. "What are we giving up, and is that OK here?" does. Name the trade-off out loud and most technical arguments get shorter.

The trade-offs you will meet every week

None of these sides is right in general. The context decides: deadline, risk, how long the code will live, and how bad the failure would be for the clinic.

Trade-offLean left whenLean right when
Speed vs qualityDemo prototype, easy to throw awayPayments, auth, patient data
Build vs buyIt's the client's core value, or vendors don't fitCommodity: SMS, email, auth, payments
Always correct vs always upBooking a slot: refuse rather than double-bookClinic homepage: slightly stale is fine
Flexibility vs simplicityThe client already has 3 real variations"Might need it someday" (YAGNI, CO6)
Consistency vs local bestFollow the codebase's pattern, even if you'd pick anotherThe pattern is causing real bugs: propose a change

One-way doors deserve analysis, two-way doors deserve speed

Ask: if this is wrong, how expensive is it to undo? A button label or a library behind your own interface is a two-way door: decide in minutes and move on. A database schema with production data, a public API the mobile app depends on, or a vendor contract is a one-way door: slow down, write it up, involve your lead.

"Good enough" is a target, not an excuse

Perfection is waste when nobody would notice the difference: polishing an admin report used twice a month, or abstracting code that has one caller. It is not waste on the money path, security or data integrity. Good enough means the acceptance criteria, the Definition of Done (QA3) and no known risk left unsaid.

When you don't know, run a time-boxed spike

A spike is a short, throwaway experiment to answer one question, with a fixed time limit. With an agent you can try two approaches in an afternoon. The product of a spike is not code, it is a written answer.

CLINIC-215 — spike outcome (time-box: 4h, used 3.5h) 
Question: can we send Zalo messages instead of SMS for reminders?
Tried:    Zalo ZNS API in a scratch branch, 1 approved template, test OA
Answer:   yes, ~40% cheaper per message, BUT each template needs Zalo approval
          (2–5 working days) and the clinic needs a verified Official Account
Recommend: keep SMS for launch; client starts OA verification now; revisit in 1 month
Code:     branch spike/zalo-zns, not for merge
# question · tried · answer · recommendation — readable by a PM in 30 seconds

Disagree, then commit

Say your concern once, clearly, with evidence, to the person who decides. If the decision goes the other way, commit fully: build it well, don't re-argue it in every stand-up, and don't build your version in secret. If your concern later turns out true, bring the data calmly. That is how your next concern gets heard.

Write the decision down in ten lines

For one-way doors, a short decision record saves the team from re-debating it in six months. The full ADR format is in SD2. This is the minimum.

docs/decisions/007-reminder-provider.md 
## Decision  Use SMS via eSMS for appointment reminders at launch
## Context   Client wants no-shows ≤ 10%; launch in 3 weeks; Zalo OA not verified
## Options   SMS (eSMS) · Zalo ZNS · email only
## Why       only option live in 3 weeks; email open rate too low for this age group
## Cost      ~700 VND/SMS; migrate to Zalo later = new adapter, ~2 days
## Revisit   when the Zalo OA is verified, or SMS cost > 5M VND/month
## Decided   2026-09-12 · PM, tech lead, @you

Explain tech debt in business terms

A PM can't prioritise "the booking service is messy". They can prioritise risk, speed and cost. Translate the debt (CO6 covers what it is) into those words and give a concrete, small ask.

Engineer saysPM hears it better as
"Slot logic is duplicated in 3 places"Risk: 2 of the last 5 booking bugs came from this. A double-booking on a busy Monday is likely.
"We have no tests on payments"Speed: every payment change needs a full day of manual QC. Tests cut it to an hour.
"The ORM version is ancient"Cost: security patches stop in March; upgrading later means 2 weeks, now 3 days.
Score-3 move: end every trade-off message with a recommendation and what you need ("I suggest B. Need your OK by Thursday to keep the release date"). Options without a recommendation hand the thinking back to the PM.
Part III · Prioritisation & focus

Finish the right thing before starting the next

By week 9 your day looks like this: a ticket in progress, a QC bug, two PRs waiting for your review, a BA question and a DevOps ping about an env variable. Busy is easy. Finishing what matters most is the skill.

Sort by impact and urgency, then ask what the PM would choose

Place each item on the grid below. When two items compete and you're unsure, ask yourself "if the PM saw both, which would they pick?" If you still can't tell, ask them in one line. That is not weakness, it is their job.

Protect deep work, batch the rest

Each switch between tasks costs you 10–20 minutes to get back into the problem. Reviewing agent output needs your full attention, so plan for it. A simple day shape that works at an agency:

TimeWhatRule
9:00–9:30Stand-up, messages, reviews that block othersUnblock people first
9:30–11:30Focus block 1: your main ticketSlack on "busy", notifications off
11:30 · 14:00 · 16:30Message batchesAnswer everything that waited
14:00–16:00Focus block 2Production issues still break in
17:30End-of-day noteWritten next step (below)

Limit your work in progress, and say "not now" with a trade-off

Keep at most one main ticket and one small item in progress. Three half-done tickets look busy and deliver nothing. When a new request arrives, don't just say yes (and slip silently) or no (and look unhelpful). Show the cost and let the requester, or the PM, choose.

Slack — "not now", politely 
BA: can you add the "preferred doctor" field to the booking form today?

✗ "Sure!"                       # and CLINIC-210 slips without anyone knowing
✗ "I'm busy."                    # true, and unhelpful
✓ "Happy to. I'm on CLINIC-210 (due Thu). I can do the field today if 210
   moves to Friday, or first thing Thursday otherwise. @pm which do you prefer?"

End every day with a written next step

Before you close the laptop, write three lines in the ticket or your work log. Tomorrow morning you start in two minutes instead of thirty, and if you are sick, a teammate can pick it up.

CLINIC-210 — end of day, Tue 
Done:    SMS job + template, tests green, cancel link works on staging
Open:    phone numbers without +84 fail at provider → asked BA which format we store
Next:    normalise numbers in the job, then PR (branch feat/clinic-210-sms)

Run agents in parallel without losing track

Agents let you start three things at once; your review attention does not scale the same way. Give each agent its own branch or worktree (AG5), keep a tiny tracker, and cap it at two or three sessions. A finished session that nobody reviews is just unreviewed code.

  • One task per session, named after the ticket, in its own worktree.
  • Only parallelise independent work: two agents editing the booking service will conflict.
  • Review in order of priority, not order of finishing.
Part IV · Responsible AI, ethics & deliberate learning

The agent types; you answer for what ships and for what you learn

Clients trust Golden Owl with their code, their data and their patients, and that trust rests on each engineer's choices. The technical rules live in SD9 (security) and SD17 (personal data). This Part is about the judgement calls, and about staying good enough to make them.

Know when not to use AI

The agent is the default, not a rule. In these cases you switch it off or keep it on a short leash.

SituationWhyInstead
Learning a fundamentalYou skip the understanding you'll need to reviewWrite it by hand first, then compare with the agent
Auth, payments, access rulesPlausible code with one missing check is a breachAgent may draft; you read every line and get a senior review
Data the project forbidsContract or NDA says it can't leave approved systemsFake or anonymised data (CO1 has the list)
Client policy restricts AISome clients limit tools or require approvalCheck the project policy with your lead first

Licences, IP and token cost

The code you write usually belongs to the client, and rules on AI-generated code are still evolving, so follow the project policy and ask your lead when unsure. Be wary of a long block that looks copied from a known library; add the real, vetted dependency instead (CO5). Sessions are billed by usage: keep tasks scoped and contexts fresh (AG2), and judge a new tool on a real task, not a demo.

Never ship a known hole quietly

Sometimes a client accepts a risk to hit a deadline. That is their decision to make with full information, not yours to make by staying silent. Report it privately, the same hour, even if it is your own mistake.

Slack DM to the tech lead — reporting a vulnerability 
Found a security issue, not public yet. GET /api/bookings/:id returns any
booking if you change the ID (no ownership check). On production since v1.4.
Fix is ~1h (ownership check + 403 test). Can we hotfix today?
I can also check the other /api/* endpoints for the same gap.
# private channel · what · since when · fix size · next step — no exploit details in public
The line you don't cross: shipping a security or data problem you know about without telling your lead: another patient's records behind a changed ID, a secret committed to git, a backup of real patient data on your laptop.

Guard confidentiality, and push back on harmful requests

NDAs cover screenshots in your portfolio, client names on LinkedIn and staging URLs in public forums too. And occasionally someone asks for something that harms users: pre-ticked marketing consent, a hidden cancel button, fake reviews. You don't argue with the client yourself: name the problem, suggest an alternative, involve your lead or PM.

A useful script: "I'm not comfortable building this as written: it could break consumer or data-protection rules and hurt the clinic's reputation. Could we do X instead? I've flagged it to @lead."

Learn deliberately while the agent types

The risk of AI-assisted work is shipping for months and learning nothing, as CO1 warned. If you only accept diffs, you lose the ability to judge them, and reviewing is now your main job. Three habits keep you sharp:

  • Explain every line before accepting it; if you can't, ask the agent "why this and not X?"
  • Rebuild one small piece by hand each week, then compare with what the agent wrote.
  • Debug one problem a week without the agent first, for at least 30 minutes.

Keep a work log: shipped, broke, learned

A work log (also called a brag document) is a few lines per week. It doubles as your learning log, feeds your status updates, and is the evidence you bring to every review. Write it Friday afternoon, while you still remember.

docs/work-log.md — one week 
## Week 9
Shipped:  CLINIC-142 cancel booking (web + API). 3 AC proposed, 1 changed by PM.
Found myself before QC: double-tap created two cancellations → idempotency key
Broke:    MoMo refunds (postmortem 2026-10-02). Added provider tests + alert.
Learned:  SELECT ... FOR UPDATE vs a conditional UPDATE; why refunds need an adapter
Scores:   QA3 2 → 3 (evidence: PR #88 verification table) · SD14 1 → 2
Next:     ask to own the reschedule ticket end to end

Score yourself on the 0–4 scale, then check it with your mentor

Every module has the same ladder. Before each mentor review, pick your score per module and attach one piece of evidence from the log. The mentor confirms or adjusts it; the gap between your score and theirs is the most useful part of the conversation.

ScoreEvidence that proves it

The agent is a good devil's advocate: it lists options, costs and risks fast. The choice, the priority and the ethical call stay with you and the people who own them.

Let the agent do
  • List options and trade-offs, including ones you didn't think of
  • Draft spike write-ups, decision records and "not now" messages
  • Build a throwaway spike on a scratch branch
  • Scan a diff for privacy and security smells
  • Summarise your week's commits into a work-log draft
You must check
  • Effort and cost numbers against your own experience and real pricing
  • Which decision is actually yours, and which belongs to the PM or lead

Red flags in agent output: one option presented as "the best practice" with no downside · effort estimates in hours for a one-way door · a "simple fix" that disables a check or a test · code that looks copied from a library, licence unknown · confident claims about law, licences or pricing with no source · a plan that quietly widens the ticket's scope.

0
No exposure. Builds tickets as written. Works on whatever arrived last. Uses AI for everything, or not at all, and can't say what they learned last month.
1

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Gold-plating a two-way door

A day of debate over a label or a helper name. Decide, note it, move on; save the analysis for schemas, APIs and money.

✗ Five things in progress

Three tickets and two agent sessions half-done. Nothing ships, and nobody knows which one to wait for.

✗ Silent compliance

Building a dark pattern or shipping a known hole "because the client asked". Raise it; the decision belongs to people with full information.

✗ Scoring on feelings

"I think I'm a 3 now" with no PR to point at. Without a work log, reviews turn into guesses and your growth goes unseen.

✓ Options with a recommendation

Two or three options, the cost of each, and "I suggest B because…". PMs love engineers who make decisions easy.
