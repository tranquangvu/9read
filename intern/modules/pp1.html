Ownership, Verification & Follow-through

This module has no new framework. It covers the habits around one ticket, from the day it arrives to the week after it ships: you question the ticket before you build it, you verify your own work, you try to break it and check what else it touched, and you watch it in production and own it when it breaks. None of these habits needs talent. They need you to decide that the feature working for the user is your job, not only the code compiling.

Why it matters when AI writes the code: the agent removed the slow part of the job, which was typing. What is left is the part that decides whether a client trusts you: understanding what the business actually needs, proving the result is right, and owning it when it isn't. Two interns with the same agent produce the same amount of code. One of them gets the next interesting ticket. This module is the difference.
Part I · Own the outcome & question the ticket

Find the missing cases before the first prompt

CO1 described ownership in week 1, before you had shipped anything. Nine weeks later you have seen the whole road: ticket, plan, PR, review, staging, QC, release. This Part is about the moments on that road where a professional acts before being asked.

A closed ticket is not a working feature

CLINIC-142 ("patients can cancel a booking") was merged, QC passed it on staging, and the ticket moved to Done. Two days after release, the clinic calls: cancelled slots never reappear for other patients. The code did what the ticket said. Nobody asked what happens to the slot. The task was finished. The outcome, a clinic that can refill cancelled appointments, was not.

Owning the outcome means your question is never only "did I do what the ticket says?" It is "does this work for the patient, the receptionist and the clinic owner, in production, with their data?" The table shows what that looks like in daily behaviour.

Raise blockers early, and ask questions people can answer

Interns wait too long to ask, because asking feels like admitting failure. Seniors see it the other way round: an intern who is silent for a day is the risk. Use a time-box. Try on your own, with the agent, for about 30 minutes on a small problem (a couple of hours on a big one). If you haven't made progress, ask. If your estimate is slipping, say so the moment you know, not on the due date. Re-estimating and status updates are covered in PP3.

A good question can be answered in one message, because it already contains the context. The status update format from CO1 covers the daily report. This one is for when you are stuck.

Slack — a question someone can answer in one reply 
Goal:     cancel a booking and free the slot (CLINIC-142)
Tried:    set slot.available = true in the same transaction as the status change
Expected: slot shows as free for other patients
Actual:   still booked in the slot picker until I restart the API
My guess: slot list is cached in Redis and the cancel doesn't invalidate it
Ask:      is there a helper for slot cache invalidation, or should I add one?
# 6 lines. The reader knows what you want, what you did, and what to answer.

The same rule applies to improvements you notice along the way. Seeing that the reminder job has no retry is valuable. Silently rewriting it inside your cancel PR is not: it makes the PR harder to review and the change harder to roll back. Note it, ticket it, ask. Let the lead decide when it gets done.

Read the ticket like QA before you read it like a developer

Most bugs that reach QC were decided before the first line of code: a case nobody thought about. Tickets are written by busy people and describe the happy path. Your job is to find the rest, and the agent is good at helping (see the prompts at the end). Walk this list for every ticket that changes behaviour:

Hunt forQuestion for "patients can cancel a booking"
Roles & permissionsCan receptionists cancel for a patient? Can a patient cancel someone else's booking by changing the ID?
States & transitionsWhat about a booking that is already completed, no-show or cancelled?
Time"24 hours before" in whose time zone? What if the clinic is abroad?
MoneyWas a deposit paid? Full refund, partial, or none? Who triggers it?
Side effectsDoes the slot free up? Is the reminder email cancelled? Is the doctor notified?
ConcurrencyPatient cancels while the receptionist edits the same booking. Two taps on "Cancel".
Data volume & emptiesA patient with 0 bookings, or 300. A booking whose doctor was deleted.
Language & channelsVietnamese and English messages? Web and mobile both?

Write the missing acceptance criteria, then get them confirmed

Turn your answers into acceptance criteria in Given / When / Then form and post them in the ticket. Each one becomes a test case for you and for QC (QA3 shares test cases the same way). When you don't know the answer, propose a default and ask. That way you are never blocked: if nobody replies by the time you reach that step, you build the default and say so in the PR.

CLINIC-142 — comment with proposed acceptance criteria 
AC1 Given a confirmed booking more than 24h ahead (clinic's local time)
    When the patient taps Cancel
    Then the booking is cancelled, the slot is free for others,
    and the reminder is not sent
AC2 Given a booking less than 24h ahead
    When the patient taps Cancel
    Then they see "Please call the clinic to cancel" and nothing changes
AC3 Given a paid deposit
    When a cancellation succeeds
    Then ??? — proposed default: full refund, triggered automatically

@pm AC3 isn't in the ticket. I'll build the default unless you say otherwise
by Wed noon. AC1 and AC2 contradict the FAQ page ("cancel any time") — which wins?
# a default + a deadline + one precise question: nobody is blocked
Score-3 move: when you find a contradiction (the ticket says one thing, the existing app or the FAQ says another), quote both and ask which wins. Don't pick one quietly. A silent guess is how two departments end up with different stories for the same client.
Part II · Verify your own work

Test your feature like someone who doesn't trust you

The Definition of Done in QA3 is the minimum a PR must prove. This Part is the attitude behind it: you are the first tester of your own feature, and a suspicious one. The goal is that QC finds nothing you could have found yourself.

Be every user, not just the developer

Developers test with one admin account, a clean database and a fast laptop. Users have none of those. Before you hand anything to QC, use the feature the way real people will:

  • Every role the ticket touches: patient, receptionist, doctor, clinic admin. Log in as each one. Try the actions each one should not be able to do.
  • Realistic data: long Vietnamese names with diacritics, a clinic with 40 doctors, a patient with 300 old bookings, an empty account.
  • Real conditions: a phone-sized screen, slow 3G in DevTools, the Back button, refreshing mid-flow, double-tapping the submit button, two tabs open at once.
  • The unhappy paths: the network drops during submit, the payment is declined, the slot was taken a second ago.

An agent's "done" is a claim, not evidence

"All tests pass, the feature is complete" is what the agent says, not what you saw. Never forward it to your lead or QC as if it were yours. Run the tests yourself, open the screen yourself, and pass on what you saw (AG3 has the verification ladder).

"Done, but not really"The check that catches it
Works on your machine onlyTest on staging after merging to develop, with staging data and config
Works for admin onlyLog in as each role, including one that must be refused
Screen says success, data is wrongSQL spot check before and after
New feature works, old screen brokeGrep the callers of what you changed, open each one
Works for new rows, crashes on old onesTest against realistic or anonymised existing data
Tests pass, but test nothingBreak the code on purpose and watch a test fail (QA1)
Agent says complete, one AC missingWalk the acceptance criteria one by one yourself
"Should work" is a red flag in your own messages too. If you catch yourself writing "should work", "I think it's fine" or "tested locally" in a hand-off, stop. Either you checked it, and you can say what you saw, or you didn't, and you should say that instead. QC and clients forgive "I haven't tested the refund path yet". They don't forget "done" that wasn't.
Part III · Bug-bash & regression

Break it on purpose, and prove nothing else broke

Part II checked that your feature does what the ticket says. This Part checks the two things QC catches most often: the feature breaking under odd input, and other features breaking because of your change.

Map the blast radius: who else calls this?

The riskiest bugs are not in the screen you built. They are in the three other screens that call the function you changed. Agents make this worse, because they happily edit a shared helper to make your feature pass. Before you ask for review, list every caller and check each one:

terminal — who else depends on what I changed? 
git diff --stat main...HEAD                   # every file you (and the agent) touched
git diff main...HEAD -- src/shared/           # shared code changed? that's the risk
grep -rn "freeSlot\|releaseSlot" src/         # every caller of the function you changed
grep -rn "bookings.status" src/ jobs/ reports/ # jobs and reports read this column too
# For each caller: open the screen or run the job once. Note what you checked in the PR.

Pay special attention to data that already exists. Your migration and code work on the fresh rows you created while testing. Production has three years of bookings created by older versions of the app, some with nulls where your code expects values. Ask your mentor for an anonymised copy or a staging database with realistic data, and run your change against it.

Run your personal bug-bash

Once the feature works, switch sides. Spend 20–30 minutes trying to break it on purpose, and keep a short log. Every bug you find here costs you ten minutes. The same bug found by QC costs a round trip, and found by the client, it costs trust. A personal bug-bash checklist that grows over time is one of the most valuable files you will own.

docs/my-bug-bash.md — before every hand-off to QC 
[ ] Each role in the ticket, including one that must be refused (expect 403)
[ ] Empty, one, and many (0 / 1 / 300 bookings)
[ ] Double submit, Back button, refresh mid-flow, two tabs
[ ] Slow network + request failure: is there an error message, is data consistent?
[ ] Boundaries: exactly 24h, midnight, last day of month, other time zone
[ ] Vietnamese text, very long text, emoji in free-text fields
[ ] Money: rounding, zero, refund path
[ ] Existing data: an old row created before this change
[ ] Mobile width + keyboard-only for the main action
[ ] Console and server logs clean while doing all of the above
# add a line every time QC or a client finds something this list missed

Spot-check the data with SQL

A green toast proves the request returned 200. It doesn't prove the right rows changed. For anything that writes data, look at the database before and after, on staging as well as locally.

staging SQL — spot-check CLINIC-142 after testing 
-- 1. every cancelled booking released its slot
SELECT b.id, s.id AS slot_id, s.available
FROM bookings b JOIN slots s ON s.id = b.slot_id
WHERE b.status = 'cancelled' AND s.available = false;   -- expect 0 rows

-- 2. no reminder queued for a cancelled booking
SELECT count(*) FROM reminders r JOIN bookings b ON b.id = r.booking_id
WHERE b.status = 'cancelled' AND r.sent_at IS NULL;       -- expect 0

-- 3. nothing outside the feature moved: compare with the count before testing
SELECT status, count(*) FROM bookings GROUP BY status;

Re-check on staging after merge to develop

Your branch passed locally, but staging runs the merged develop: your change plus everyone else's, with staging config and data. Before you tell QC it is ready, repeat the main flow as one allowed and one refused role, and re-run the SQL spot checks there. Any difference from local is a bug you found first (the branch flow is in CO2).

ticket comment — hand-off to QC 
Staging (develop @ 4be21a0), 14:20:
[x] AC1–AC3 as patient; receptionist cancel OK; other patient's ID → 403
[x] Callers: reschedule, slot editor, no-show job (ran once), report
[x] SQL spot checks 1–3: 0 / 0 / counts unchanged outside test rows
[ ] Not tested: MoMo refund (no sandbox key on staging) → asked @lead
# what you saw, and what you didn't test: QC starts from facts
Part IV · After release & incidents

Your feature isn't done until it has survived real users

In our workflow (CO2) a change is verified three times: by you before the PR, by QC on staging, and in production after release. The third one is the one interns skip, because by then the ticket is closed and a new one is waiting. It is also the only check that uses real users, real data and real traffic.

Watch the first hour and the first day

When your change reaches production, keep an eye on it for the first hour, and look again the next morning. You are not on call. You are checking that the thing you built behaves the way you believe it does. Where these signals live depends on the stack: error tracking and Web Vitals for the web (FE6), crash reporting for mobile (MB5), CloudWatch alarms on AWS (OP5).

post-release check — CLINIC-142, released Thu 15:00 
[x] Error tracker: no new issue mentioning cancel / booking since 15:00
[x] API logs: POST /bookings/:id/cancel → 41 calls, 38 × 200, 3 × 422 (inside 24h, expected)
[x] Data: 38 cancelled bookings, 38 slots released (SQL spot check #1)
[x] Reminders: 0 queued for cancelled bookings
[ ] Refunds: 2 pending > 1h in the provider dashboard → asked @lead at 16:10
# post this in the ticket. It takes 10 minutes and it is what "owning it" looks like.

When the bug is yours: tell, contain, fix, explain

Everyone ships a bug to production eventually. What people remember is not the bug, it is what you did in the first thirty minutes. CO1 set the rule: tell people early. Here is the whole sequence.

Rollback decisions and anything touching production data are made with your lead, not alone. Your job is to bring the facts fast. The update to the PM or client is plain words, not a stack trace: what happened, who was affected, what is fixed, what happens next.

message to the PM — 40 minutes after the report 
Since Thursday's release, cancelling a booking didn't send the refund for
deposits paid by MoMo (Stripe was fine). 7 patients are affected, total 1,050,000 VND.

Now:  fixed and released at 11:20. New cancellations refund correctly.
Next: I've queued the 7 refunds manually with @lead; they'll arrive within 24h.
      I'll send you the list of patients by 14:00 if the clinic wants to call them.
Why:  our test used Stripe only. We're adding MoMo to the test suite today.
# what · who · now · next · why — no blame, no jargon, no "should be ok"

A blameless postmortem turns one bug into a better system

For anything a client noticed, write a short postmortem. "Blameless" doesn't mean nobody was involved. It means you ask why the process let it through, because "be more careful" never prevents the next one. Then make every lesson durable: a regression test, a line in your bug-bash list, an alert, or a CLAUDE.md rule, as in the Reflect step of AG3.

docs/postmortems/2026-10-02-momo-refunds.md 
## Impact     7 patients, MoMo deposits not refunded for ~44h
## Timeline   Thu 15:00 release · Sat 09:10 clinic reports · 09:15 team told
              · 11:20 fix released · 12:30 manual refunds queued
## Root cause refund call used the Stripe client for every provider;
              MoMo branch never ran in tests (only Stripe fixtures existed)
## Why it got through
              - AC3 mentioned "refund", not "refund per provider"
              - bug-bash list had "money: refund path", tested with Stripe only
## Changes    [x] test per provider   [x] bug-bash: "each payment provider"
              [x] alert: refund pending > 2h   [ ] adapter review (SD14) — ticket CLINIC-201

Turn every bug into a test, a checklist line or a rule

A bug fixed only in the code will come back in a different file. Before you close it, make it impossible to repeat silently: a regression test that fails without the fix (QA1), a line in your bug-bash list, and, if the agent wrote the bug, a rule in CLAUDE.md so it doesn't write it again. Growing these habits over the internship, and scoring yourself with evidence, is in PP2.

CLAUDE.md — rule added after the MoMo postmortem 
## Payments
- Every refund/charge change must have a test per provider (Stripe, MoMo).
- Never call a provider client directly; use PaymentAdapter.forProvider().
- In the PR summary, list which providers you tested and how.
# one bug → one test, one checklist line, one rule the agent reads every session

Working with Claude Code

The agent is an excellent sparring partner for the habits in this module. It is tireless at listing edge cases, planning a bug-bash and drafting status messages. It is not the one who owns the result. Use it to think wider, then decide and verify yourself.

prompt — read the ticket like QA 
Act as a senior QA engineer. Here is ticket CLINIC-142 and the relevant code
(BookingService, the bookings/slots/reminders/payments schema).
List edge cases by category: roles, states, time, money, side effects,
concurrency, data volume, language. For each, the question to ask the PM.
Then draft acceptance criteria as Given/When/Then. Mark every AC that is
your assumption, not something the ticket says.
Try to break my feature. Based on the diff on this branch, write a 20-minute
bug-bash plan: the exact steps, which role/data to use, and what I should
see. Include callers of any shared function I changed. Don't fix anything.
From these notes, draft (1) a plain-words update for the PM: what, who, now,
next, why, no jargon; and (2) a blameless postmortem with impact, timeline,
root cause, why it got through, and changes. Notes: ...
# then edit both yourself: facts and promises must be yours
Let the agent do
  • List edge cases and draft acceptance criteria
  • Find every caller of the code you changed
  • Write the bug-bash plan and the SQL spot checks
  • Draft status updates, postmortems and hand-over notes
  • Turn a fixed bug into a regression test and a CLAUDE.md rule draft
You must check
  • Which AC are assumptions, and get them confirmed by a human
  • Run the bug-bash yourself, as each role, on staging
  • Every number and promise in a message before you send it
  • That "done" in any hand-off means something you saw
  • That the PR contains only what the ticket asked for
Red flags in agent output (and in your own messages): "should work", "all tests pass" with no output · acceptance criteria that silently invent business rules · a status update that promises dates you didn't agree to · a postmortem that blames a person · a "small cleanup" of files outside the ticket · a bug-bash plan that only covers the happy path and the admin role.

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0
No exposure. Does what the ticket literally says and hands it over. Hears about problems from others.
1
Awareness. Knows they should ask questions and test more, but does it after QC finds the bugs. Reports blockers at stand-up the next day.
2
Works with support. Lists edge cases and runs a bug-bash when reminded. Checks staging, sometimes forgets production. Owns bugs when asked.
3
Works independently (target). Posts proposed AC on day one, raises blockers within the time-box, finds most of their own bugs before QC, checks every release in production, and tells the team first when something they shipped breaks.
4
Leads & coaches. Improves the team's templates (AC, bug-bash, postmortem), runs blameless postmortems, and is the person PMs trust with vague tickets.
Self-check before claiming 3: on your last three tickets, did you post questions or AC before coding? How many bugs did QC find that your bug-bash should have caught? Did you check each one in production after release, and can you show where you wrote that down?

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Using QC as your test suite

Every bug QC finds that you could have found costs a round trip and some trust. QC should find the surprises, not the obvious.

✗ Silent guessing

Building your own interpretation of an unclear rule without saying so. Propose a default and ask, in writing.

✗ Hero mode

Fixing a production problem alone and quietly. Tell the team first: they may know it's already a known issue, or that a rollback is safer.

✓ A personal bug-bash list

Add a line every time someone finds a bug you missed. After a few months, it is the best checklist on the team.

✓ Post-release note in the ticket

Ten minutes after each release: what you checked in production and what you saw. It closes the loop and builds a record of reliability.

✓ Re-check after merge to develop

Your branch is not what QC tests. Repeat the main flow and the SQL checks on staging, and write down what you saw before you hand it over.
