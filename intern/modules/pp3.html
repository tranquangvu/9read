Communication & Collaboration

At an agency, most of your working day is spent talking to people: a question in Slack, a status update, a demo for a client in Sydney, a reply to a review comment. This module is about doing that well, short, clear and in English: status and estimates people can plan on, demos and handovers without surprises, feedback taken and given like a professional, and making the people around you better at their jobs too.

Why it matters when AI writes the code: the agent can produce a feature in an hour, but it can't find out what the client meant, agree a scope change, or tell QC what changed. When code is cheap, the slow part of every project is the conversation around it. The engineer who communicates clearly becomes the one the team can't ship without.
Part I · Written & async communication

Write for a busy reader: point first, context included, one message

Your PM reads 200 messages a day, often on a phone between meetings. Every message you send competes for that attention. CO1 covered the daily status format and PP1 the "I'm stuck" question. This Part is the general rule behind both.

Lead with the point, and never send a bare "hi"

Use BLUF (bottom line up front): the first line says what you need or what happened. Details come after, for whoever wants them. Put everything in one message. "Hi, are you there?" followed by silence costs the reader a context switch and gives them nothing to answer.

Slack — the same question, two ways 
✗ 09:02  hi anh
  09:02  are you free?
  09:40  (no reply yet, you are still waiting)

✓ Hi anh, quick decision needed on CLINIC-156 (reschedule booking):
  can a patient reschedule to another doctor, or only the same one?
  The ticket doesn't say. I'll build "same doctor only" unless you say
  otherwise by 14:00. Screenshot of the current flow attached.
# point in line 1 · context · a default · a deadline · nobody waits on you

Status and estimates: give ranges, and bad news early

A good update answers the reader's next question before they ask it: what is done, what is left, what you need. Words like "almost" and "should" hide exactly what the PM needs to plan. Give estimates as a range (CO1 covers how), and if it is slipping, say so the moment you know, with a new range and an option: a PM can re-plan a week ahead, not an hour before a client demo.

Slack — status update and an early "I'm late" 
✗ Reschedule is almost done, just some small issues left.

✓ CLINIC-156 reschedule: on track for Thu QC.
  Done:    API + web flow, tested as patient and receptionist
  Left:    email to the doctor about the change (~3h)
  Risk:    SMS provider sandbox is down, can't test SMS until they fix it
  Need:    nothing today

✓ (Tuesday) CLINIC-156 heads-up: Thu QC → now Fri–Mon.
  Why:     doctor email needs the new template service (~1.5 days)
  Option:  ship Thu without the email, add it Mon. Your call, Hương.
# the same news on Thursday afternoon is a crisis; on Tuesday it's a plan

The right place, and the right speed

Reply in threads so a channel stays readable. Ask in the project channel, not in a DM, unless the topic is personal. Other people learn from the answer, and you get help sooner. Decisions belong wherever people will look for them later, which is almost never the chat.

SituationReasonable reply timeIf you can't answer yet
Teammate question in a threadWithin a couple of hours"Seen, will check after my current task (~15:00)"
PM or QC blocked on youWithin the hourAcknowledge now, give a time
Production issue, @mentionNow"Looking" is a valid first reply
Client emailSame business day (their time zone)Ask the PM who replies; don't leave it unanswered

Turn every meeting into decisions, owners and dates

A meeting without written notes will be remembered three different ways. If nobody else posts a summary within the hour, you do it. Write so a designer or the client can read it: no internal jargon, no ticket numbers without a title.

Slack thread — after the Tuesday sync with the clinic 
Decided
  1. Patients can reschedule up to 24h before, same doctor only (for now)
  2. Reminder SMS moves from 24h to 2h before the appointment
Actions

  - Lan (design): screen for "no slots left"        → Wed

# decisions · owner · date · open questions — then copy the decisions into the ticket

Remote and hybrid: make your work visible

When people can't see you at a desk, they judge by what they can see online. Make that picture accurate.

  • Progress is visible: draft PRs early, ticket status that matches reality, a short update at the end of each day.
  • Your calendar is true: block focus time, mark leave and appointments, set Slack status when you're away.
  • You are reachable during agreed hours, and people know the backup when you aren't.
  • Camera on in client calls unless the team agreed otherwise. Faces build trust across time zones; a black square with a name doesn't.
Part II · Clients, demos & English

Protect the relationship: no surprises in scope, demos or handovers

Clients rarely leave an agency because of one bug. They leave because they were surprised: by a date, a cost, or something they thought was included. Your job in client conversations, demos and handovers is to prevent surprises, and to know which answers are yours to give. For more depth, see FD7 Embedded Working & Client Trust.

Say "not in this scope" with options, not just "no"

A flat "no" sounds like a closed door. "Yes, and here is what it costs" sounds like a partner. Name what is in scope, offer two or three options, and let the PM and client choose. Never agree to the extra work on the spot, even if it looks small with the agent.

reply in the client channel — PM copied 
Hi Chris, thanks, a waiting list makes sense for busy doctors.
It isn't part of the current booking scope, so I'd like to check with
Hương (our PM) before we commit. The options I can see:
  A. Add it after launch as a new ticket (no change to the launch date)
  B. Simple version now: "notify me if a slot opens", ~2 days, launch +2 days
  C. Skip it and let receptionists call patients manually for now
Hương will come back to you by Thursday with the estimate.
# acknowledge · name the scope · options with impact · who decides · when

Every change request goes through the PM

"Can you just change the button colour" in a call and "can you just add Zalo login" look the same to a client. One takes five minutes, the other changes the contract. You can estimate the impact; the PM decides and tells the client. Small changes that are clearly in scope still get written in the ticket, so QC tests them.

Explain a technical problem without the technical words

The client needs to know what users see, what you are doing about it, and whether they must do anything. They don't need the stack trace. Translate the cause into the business effect.

email — the same incident, two ways 
✗ The Redis cache TTL wasn't invalidated on the slot update event, so the
  GET /slots endpoint returned stale data until expiry.

✓ Summary: between 9:00 and 9:40 this morning, some patients saw time slots
  that were already taken. 4 of them tried to book one and got an error.
  No double bookings were created.
  Now: fixed at 9:40. Slots now update immediately.
  Next: we added a test for this case. No action needed from your team.

Demo on staging, and say what isn't included

Show the real flow on staging with realistic data, not a rehearsed happy path on your laptop. Say out loud what is not included and any known limitation. A limitation the client hears from you is a detail; the same limitation found by their users is a complaint.

demo notes — 5 lines before you share your screen 
Goal:        patient reschedules a booking (CLINIC-156)
Where:       staging, patient 0907… with 3 real-looking bookings
Show:        reschedule → doctor gets email → receptionist view updates
Not included: other-doctor reschedule (after launch, agreed Tue)
Known limit:  SMS reminder not live until the provider sandbox is back
# say the last two lines before the client asks, not after

Hand over so nobody has to ask

Decisions made in chat or a demo disappear, so copy them into the ticket. When work moves to QC, another dev, or you on Monday after leave, the next person should find what was built, what was decided, what is left, and how to verify it. Use the same short template every time.

handover template — ticket comment 
What:        reschedule booking, patient + receptionist (CLINIC-156)
Where:       PR #97 · staging build 2026-10-06 14:20
How to test: patient 0907… / booking #812; receptionist account in 1Password
Not done:    other-doctor reschedule (decided: after launch)
Known issue: SMS not testable until provider sandbox is back
Ask:         me until Fri, then @minh

Meeting English that keeps you safe

You don't need perfect English. You need a few phrases ready for the moments that matter, so you don't nod along to something you didn't understand. FD12 goes deeper on business English.

Clarifying"When you say 'cancel', do you mean the patient, or the clinic too?""OK, understood" when you aren't sure

Directness, silence, and what to escalate

Clients differ in how directly they say things and in what silence means. One client says "this is wrong", another says "maybe we could consider…" and means the same. Don't rely on stereotypes about a country; learn this client's style from your PM, and when in doubt, confirm in writing. Many Vietnamese engineers stay quiet when unsure, which Western clients can read as agreement. Say it when you're unsure. Then know which questions are yours:

TopicAnswer yourselfEscalate to PM / lead
How a feature works todayYes—
A bug's cause and fix statusFacts you've verifiedAnything with data loss or security
Dates, cost, scopeNeverAlways
Complaints about the teamThank them, don't argueAlways, the same day
Access, credentials, contractsNeverAlways
The most expensive sentence in agency life is "sure, no problem". Said in a call to be polite, it becomes a promise the client repeats to their boss. Replace it with "good idea, let me check the impact and come back to you by Thursday".
Part III · Cross-functional work & feedback

Talk to the other role's worry, and treat feedback as information

CO1 listed who does what on the road from ticket to deploy. Collaboration is the next step: knowing what each person is afraid of, handing them what they need before they ask, and taking their feedback without a fight. Each of those is a small moment; together they decide how much people trust you.

What each role worries about

When a conversation feels difficult, it's usually because you are defending your worry and they are defending theirs. Name their worry first and most arguments disappear.

RoleWorries aboutGive them
PMDates, scope, the client's moodEarly risks, honest ranges, no surprises
BAThe business rule being built as meantPrecise questions, proposed AC, contradictions quoted
DesignerThe experience and the brandMissing states found early, alternatives instead of "can't"
QCBugs escaping to the clientWhat changed, how to test it, which data to use
DevOpsA deploy that breaks production at nightEnv vars, migrations and rollback notes before release
Other devsCode they'll have to maintainSmall PRs, clear names, a heads-up on shared code

Designers: clarify the states, push back with alternatives

Designs usually show the happy path with perfect data. Before you build, ask about the rest, and build the intent rather than every pixel (AG4 covers design-to-code with the agent). When something is expensive, offer a cheaper option instead of refusing.

  • States: loading, empty ("no slots this week"), error, disabled, and success for every screen.
  • Edge data: a 40-character Vietnamese doctor name, 0 or 50 slots in a day, a small phone.
  • Push back with options: "A custom calendar is ~3 days. The shadcn date picker with your colours is half a day and covers 90%. Which do you prefer?"

QC: share test cases early, reproduce their bugs fast

QC is not the opponent who finds your mistakes; they are the last safety net before the client. Share your acceptance criteria and test notes when you start (QA3). When they report a bug, reproduce it first. "Works on my machine" is never a reply; it's the start of an investigation.

Jira — replying to a QC bug 
✗ Can't reproduce, works on my machine. Closing.

✓ Thanks Trang, reproduced on staging with patient 0907… (Safari, iOS 17).
  Cause: date picker sends local time, API expects UTC. Fix in PR #97.
  Also checked the reschedule screen: same bug, fixed there too.
  Ready for retest on staging after 16:00.

PM, BA and DevOps: risks early, deploy notes always

With PM and BA, the rule from PP1 holds: clarify acceptance criteria on day one, and raise risks the hour you see them. With DevOps, never make them discover what your change needs. Put a deploy note in the PR.

PR #97 — deploy notes 
## Deploy notes
Env vars:    NEW  SMS_PROVIDER_KEY (staging + prod, ask @devops, in 1Password)
Migration:   20261006_add_rescheduled_at.sql (adds nullable column, safe online)
Order:       migrate → deploy API → deploy web
Rollback:    redeploy previous tag; column can stay (nullable, unused)
Watch:       error rate on POST /bookings/:id/reschedule for 1h
# five lines that save DevOps a 23:00 phone call

Take feedback without defending, give it without hurting

A review comment or a QC bug report is information about the code, not a verdict on you. Reply in three moves: thank briefly, clarify if unsure, then fix it or explain once why not. Still disagreeing after one exchange? Move to a call. When you review others (CO2), point at the line, name the risk, suggest a fix, and label what is optional.

replying to review — the same comment, two ways 
Reviewer: "cancel() doesn't check the booking belongs to the user."

✗ "It works in my testing, and the frontend only shows the user's own bookings."
✓ "Good catch, thanks. Anyone could call the API with another ID. Added the
   ownership check + a 403 test (a3f9c1). Also grepped the other booking
   endpoints: reschedule had the same gap, fixed in the same commit."
# the ✓ reply fixes the bug, and the pattern, and makes the reviewer's time worth it

Trust is built in small, repeated moments

Leads and clients decide which projects you get next from dozens of undramatic moments: how you reply to a review, whether your estimates hold, what you say in a demo.

Part IV · Mentoring & knowledge sharing

Be a multiplier: what you learned once, nobody should learn again

A good engineer finishes their own tickets. A great one makes the whole team faster. You don't need to be senior to start: you only need to share what you just figured out while it's still fresh.

Answer in public, write the doc you wished existed

Every question you answer in a DM helps one person once. The same answer in the project channel helps everyone who searches later. When you spent an hour on something undocumented, spend ten more minutes writing it down.

  • README: the setup steps that actually worked, including the one error everyone hits.
  • Decision records: why we chose X over Y, in a short ADR (SD2), so nobody reopens it without new facts.
  • Troubleshooting notes: "staging login fails after DB reset → re-run seed script".

Pairing: switch between driver and navigator

Pairing is the fastest way to pass on judgement, especially with a newer intern. With an agent in the loop, the "driver" is often the person prompting and the navigator reviews the plan and the diff. Swap every 30 minutes.

RoleDoesWith a junior, you should
DriverTypes or prompts, thinks aloudLet them drive most of the time
NavigatorWatches direction, spots risks, reads the diffAsk questions ("what happens if…?") instead of giving answers
BothAgree the goal for the session firstEnd with "what did you learn?" in one sentence

Give a 10-minute talk or demo

Internal talks are where interns get noticed. Keep them short and concrete: one problem you faced, what you did, and what others can reuse. Show real code or a real screen, not slides full of text.

talk outline — 10 minutes 
1 min  The problem   "Slots showed as free after they were booked"
2 min  Why it's hard  cache + two services writing the same data
4 min  What we did   live demo on staging, the 10 lines that matter
2 min  What to reuse  the checklist / helper / prompt, and where it lives
1 min  Questions     + link to the doc in the channel afterwards

Onboard the next intern, and share what worked with the agent

You remember what confused you in week 1. The next intern will be confused by the same things. Turn that into a checklist, and share the prompts, skills and CLAUDE.md rules that saved you time (AG5). Code review is teaching too: explain the why, not only the fix (CO2).

docs/onboarding.md — written by the last intern 
[ ] Day 1: accounts (Slack, Jira, GitHub, 1Password), join #clinic-dev
[ ] Day 1: run the app locally (README), book a slot as a test patient
[ ] Day 2: read the last 5 merged PRs and 2 ADRs
[ ] Day 2: install our Claude Code skills from .claude/skills/, read CLAUDE.md
[ ] Week 1: pair with a dev on one small ticket, you drive
[ ] Week 1: know who to ask: PM Hương, lead Minh, QC Trang, DevOps Khoa
# each intern fixes one thing that was wrong in this file
Score-4 move: on the 0–4 scale, score 4 is "leads & coaches". That rarely means a title. It means other people are faster because of something you wrote, shared or taught: a doc they use, a prompt they copy, an intern you onboarded.

Working with Claude Code

The agent is a good editor for your messages: it shortens, restructures and fixes English. It doesn't know the relationship, the promises already made, or what the client is sensitive about. Remove client data it shouldn't see (CO1) and own every word you send. 

Rewrite this Slack message BLUF-style for a busy PM: the decision I need
in line 1, then context, then my proposed default and a deadline.
Max 6 lines, plain English, no jargon. Don't add facts. Draft: ...
Here is a technical incident summary. Rewrite it as an email for a
non-technical clinic owner: what users saw, how many, what is fixed,
what happens next, whether they need to act. No component names.
Mark any sentence where you had to guess.
From this meeting transcript, list: decisions, action items (owner +
date), and open questions. If an owner or date is missing, write
"UNASSIGNED" instead of inventing one.
Let the agent do
  • Shorten and restructure drafts, fix grammar
  • Translate technical causes into plain words
  • Summarise meeting notes into decisions and actions
  • Draft PR deploy notes, demo notes, handovers and READMEs
  • Suggest polite phrasing for a "no" with options
You must check
  • Every date, estimate and promise before it is sent
  • That the tone fits this client and this moment
  • Owners and decisions match what was actually agreed
  • Nothing confidential went into the prompt
  • It still sounds like you, not a press release
Red flags in AI-drafted messages: invented dates or owners · "we will definitely" promises nobody approved · over-apologising ("we deeply regret…") for a small bug · three paragraphs where two lines would do · a summary that drops the open questions · corporate filler ("circle back", "synergy") the client will spot as machine-written.

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0
No exposure. Sends "hi" and waits. Reports a late ticket on the due date. Quiet in client calls, even when unsure. Argues with review comments.
1
Awareness. Knows the BLUF and handover formats but uses them only when reminded. Agrees to client requests to be polite.
2
Works with support. Writes clear updates and replies to QC and review well. Needs the PM present to demo, or to handle scope or disagreement with a client.
3
Works independently (target). Messages need no follow-up. Estimates in ranges and flags a slip the day they know. Summarises meetings unprompted, routes change requests through the PM with options, demos on staging with limitations stated, takes and gives feedback without friction, and hands work over so nobody has to ask.
4
Leads & coaches. Improves the team's templates and docs, onboards and pairs with juniors, gives internal talks, and is trusted to join client calls alone.
Self-check before claiming 3: pick your last five Slack messages to the PM. Could each be answered in one reply? Did your last late ticket get flagged before the due date? Did your last demo name what wasn't included? Can someone pick up your current ticket tomorrow from your handover note alone?

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Silent nodding

Agreeing in a call to something you didn't understand. The client hears "yes"; you build a guess.

✗ Scope by side door

Quietly building a "small" client request outside the ticket. It skips QC, the estimate and the invoice.

✗ Defending instead of fixing

"It works in my testing" in reply to a review. The reviewer stops bothering, and the next bug ships.

✓ Bad news early, with a plan

"I'm behind, here is the new range and what I'd cut" on day two is a normal project event. The same news on the due date is a crisis.

✓ Options, not refusals

Every "not in scope" or design push-back comes with two or three options and their cost.

✓ Leave it better for the next intern

Fix one thing in the README or onboarding doc every time it confused you.
