The Developer Role in the AI Era

Coding agents now type most of the code. That changes what a developer spends the day on, but not what a developer is responsible for. This module explains what you are actually paid for, how a change moves through a software agency from ticket to production, why you own every line you ship, how to use AI tools without leaking a client's secrets, and how to learn fast with an agent without skipping the understanding.

Why it matters when AI writes the code: when typing code was the slow part, a junior developer's output was limited by typing speed, and mistakes were small because the changes were small. Now an intern can produce a senior-sized diff in an afternoon. The bottleneck has moved to understanding the problem, choosing a design, checking the result and explaining it to other people. Those are the skills this program builds, and this first module tells you why.
Part I · What changed

Typing got cheap. Judgement did not.

A coding agent such as Claude Code can read a repository, edit many files, run the tests and open a pull request. It does this in minutes. What it can't do on its own is decide what the client actually needs, notice that the ticket contradicts last month's decision, or take responsibility when the release breaks on Friday evening. Those parts of the job didn't get cheaper. They became most of the job.

What got cheap, and what stayed expensive

Use this table to decide where your attention goes. The left column is work you should now hand to the agent almost every time. The right column is work where the agent can help, but the decision and the responsibility stay with you.

Notice the pattern: every "still expensive" row is about deciding or checking. An intern who only prompts and pastes is competing with the agent. An intern who decides and checks is the person the agent works for.

The five things you are paid for

Every module in this program trains one or more of these. When you score yourself each week, ask which of the five you got better at.

  • Understand: restate the ticket in your own words, find the unstated rules, ask the client-side questions before any code exists.
  • Design: choose the data model, where the logic lives and what you will not build. The whole System & Data Design track (SD1–SD12) trains this.
  • Verify: prove the change works with evidence, not a feeling. See AG3 and QA3.
  • Own: stay with the change after merge, through QA, deploy and the first bug report.
  • Communicate: a clear PR, a short daily update, a clear "I'm blocked" early instead of a surprise late.

Part II · How an agency ships

Every change travels the same road, from ticket to deploy

At a software agency like Golden Owl you build products for clients. The client pays for working features, delivered on time, that they can trust. The team's workflow exists so that every change is understood before it is built, reviewed before it is merged, and tested before the client sees it. Learn the road once and every project will feel familiar.

The road, drawn

Read the ticket before you prompt anything

A ticket is a small contract. Before you open Claude Code, find the three things every workable ticket needs: what should change, why (the user problem) and how we'll know it's done (acceptance criteria). If one is missing, ask. An agent will happily build a feature from a ticket that has no acceptance criteria, and you will find out in QA what it should have done.

ticket — CLINIC-142, as written vs as clarified 
As written:  "Patients should be able to cancel bookings."

After 10 minutes of questions:
Why:    Patients call reception to cancel; staff want fewer calls.
What:   A "Cancel" button on the booking detail page (web only).
Rules:  - Only the patient who made the booking, or clinic staff
        - Not later than 24h before the slot          # asked: who decided 24h? → client, confirmed
        - Cancelled slot becomes bookable again
Out of scope: refunds (no payments yet), mobile app     # the non-goals save a week
Acceptance:
  1. Patient cancels own booking 2 days ahead → status "Cancelled"
  2. Same patient, 3h ahead → error message, booking unchanged
  3. Another patient tries by URL → 403

The clarified version is also a near-perfect prompt for the agent. That is not a coincidence: a ticket you understand well enough to test is a ticket an agent can build correctly.

Communicate status before anyone asks

A short written update, posted at the same time every day, is the cheapest trust-building tool you have. It turns "the intern disappeared for two days" into "the intern hit a problem on Tuesday and said so".

          Ticket doesn't say. Asked @pm in the ticket at 10:05.

# facts, links, one explicit question. No "almost done".
Part III · Accountability

"The agent wrote it" is never an excuse

When you open a PR, your name is on it. The reviewer, the QA engineer and the client don't care which tool typed the code, just as nobody cares which keyboard you used. If a bug reaches production, the question is "why didn't our process catch it?", and the first step of that process was you.

What ownership looks like in practice

Ownership is not a feeling. It is a set of habits a reviewer can see. Compare the two columns.

Operator (passes output along)
  • "Claude says it works."
  • Can't answer "why is this line here?" in review
  • Opens a 40-file PR and lets the reviewer find the problems
  • Forwards review comments to the agent without reading them
  • Moves on after merge
Owner (signs off on the result)
  • "Here is the test output and the screenshot."
  • Can explain every line, or has removed the lines they couldn't
  • Keeps PRs small enough to review in one sitting
  • Replies to each review comment with a decision and a reason
  • Checks the feature on staging, and again after deploy

The explain-every-line rule

Before you mark a PR ready for review, you should be able to explain what every changed line does and why it's needed. You don't have to know how you'd have written it from scratch. If you can't explain a line, you have three honest options: ask the agent until you can (CO3 shows how), remove it and check the tests still pass, or flag it in the PR as something you're unsure about.

PR #88 description — the "reviewer notes" section 
## Reviewer notes
- The 24h rule lives in BookingService.cancel(), not the controller,
  so the future mobile API gets it for free.
- Unsure: the agent used SELECT ... FOR UPDATE when loading the booking.
  I think it prevents two cancels racing, but I haven't tested concurrency.
  # saying what you DON'T know is ownership too
- No refunds: out of scope per CLINIC-142.

## Evidence
- npm test → 51 passed    - E2E booking-cancel.spec.ts → 3 passed
- Screenshot: cancel button + error for <24h (attached)

When something you shipped breaks

It will happen. How you respond matters more than the bug. The sequence below is what a senior does, and it is what your mentor wants to see from you.

    participant Y as You

    participant T as Team
    QA->>Y: "Cancelled slots stay unbookable"
    Y->>T: "Mine, from PR #88. Looking now."
    Y->>C: reproduce it with a failing test first

nobody asks "who typed it"
Never hide or quietly patch your own bug. Say "that's mine" in the first message, before you know the fix. A bug the team hears about from you costs a little. A bug the team discovers you knew about costs trust, and trust is what gets you better work.
Part IV · Safety and learning

Protect the client's data, and protect your understanding

Two risks come with AI tools that didn't exist when you studied. The first is leaking something that isn't yours to share. The second is quieter: getting working code every day while learning nothing, so that by week 12 you still can't design or debug on your own.

What never goes into an untrusted AI tool

Client source code and data belong to the client, and our contracts say how they may be handled. The rule is simple: only use the AI tools and accounts the company has approved for that project. A personal account on a free chatbot, a browser extension you found yesterday or a random "AI code formatter" website is not approved, even if it would be convenient. When in doubt, ask your mentor before pasting.

WhatApproved company toolPersonal or unknown tool
Secrets: API keys, passwords, tokens, .env files, private keysNever. Block the agent from reading them (below).Never. If one leaks, tell your mentor at once so it can be rotated.
Real personal data: patient names, emails, phone numbers, production DB dumpsOnly if the project explicitly allows it. Use seeded fake data instead.Never
Client source codeYes, within the project's rulesNever
Error messages and stack tracesYes, after checking they contain no tokens or personal dataOnly generic ones, with names removed
Your own practice projectsYesYour choice

Claude Code runs in your terminal with access to the files in your project, so tell it what it may not touch. Deny rules in the settings file stop it from reading secret files, even if a prompt asks it to. Permissions are covered in full in AG2; secrets management in general is in SD10.

.claude/settings.json — keep secrets out of the agent's reach 
{
  "permissions": {
    "deny": [
      "Read(./.env)",
      "Read(./.env.*)",
      "Read(./secrets/**)"
    ]
  }
}
// the first two rules come straight from the Claude Code settings docs
// settings files are strict JSON: delete these comment lines in a real file
Secrets leak through side doors. Deny rules cover the files, not everything else. Don't paste a token into the chat "just for a quick test". Don't let the agent print environment variables into a log you then share. Don't screenshot a terminal that shows a key. And never commit one: CO2 covers .gitignore and what to do if a secret does reach git history.

Learn with the agent without skipping the understanding

Reading a correct answer feels like understanding it. It isn't. You find out the difference the first time you have to debug the code without help. Two habits close that gap, and they cost a few minutes each.

    A["Predict
what will this do?

test · script · request"]
    B --> C{"Matched?"}
    C -- "yes" --> D["Explain back

    C -- "no" --> E["Find the gap

    D --> F["Agent checks
your explanation"]
    F -- "gaps found" --> E
  • Predict before you run. Before running a test, a query or a curl command, write one line: "I expect 403 because Bob doesn't own the booking." A wrong prediction is the most useful thing that can happen to you all day, because it shows you exactly what you misunderstood.
  • Explain back. After the agent explains something, close the explanation and write your own version. Then ask the agent to check it and point out what you got wrong. This is how you turn "I read it" into "I know it".
prompt — explain-back check 
Here is my understanding of how booking cancellation works in this repo:
"The controller checks the user is logged in, then BookingService.cancel()
checks ownership and the 24h rule, then sets status to cancelled."

Check it against the code. For each claim say correct / wrong / incomplete,
cite file:line, and tell me the one thing I missed that matters most.
# Don't rewrite my explanation for me. Just grade it.
notes/learning-log.md — one entry per day, 5 minutes 
## Tue, week 1
Predicted: POST /bookings/12/cancel as Bob → 403
Actual:    404
Why:       the query is scoped to the current user, so Bob can't even
           "see" booking 12. 404 hides that it exists. Deliberate.
New term:  "scoping queries to the owner" → read SD10 on IDOR
# your mentor reads this in the weekly review
The one-week test: if the agent disappeared for a week, could you still make small changes to the code you shipped? If not, you shipped code you didn't learn from. Slow down on the next ticket and use predict and explain-back on every step.

Working with Claude Code

In week 1 use the agent as a patient senior colleague who answers questions, not as a code generator. These prompts help you understand a ticket and a codebase, and turn the agent into a tutor that makes you think.

prompt — understand the ticket before the code 
Here is ticket CLINIC-142: <paste the ticket text>
Don't write code. Instead:
1. Restate it as goal, rules and acceptance criteria.
2. List every question the ticket leaves open (who, when, edge cases, errors).
3. Tell me which of those questions the existing code already answers — cite file:line.
Act as my tutor for this repo. When I ask how something works, ask me to predict
the answer first, then show me the code that proves or disproves my guess.
# turns "give me the answer" into predict → run → explain-back
Before I open this PR, quiz me: ask 5 questions a reviewer would ask about
this diff. Wait for my answers, then tell me which ones were weak.
Let the agent do
  • Turn a vague ticket into a list of open questions
  • Explain unfamiliar code, with file:line citations
  • Draft PR descriptions and daily updates from your notes
  • Quiz you and grade your explanations
  • Suggest what to test for a given acceptance criterion
You must check
  • That the open questions go to a human (PM or mentor), not get answered by the agent's guess
  • At least two cited lines, opened yourself
  • That nothing you paste contains secrets or real personal data
  • That every claim in your PR description is true, and every line explainable
  • Your learning log: write it yourself, don't generate it
Red flags in agent output: it answers a business question the ticket left open ("I assumed staff can cancel any time") instead of asking · it invents a convention the team doesn't use · it reads or prints .env or credentials · it says "works" with no evidence · it produces a PR description that claims tests or checks that were never run.

Self-check before claiming 3: take your latest PR. Could you walk your mentor through every changed line without the agent open? Did you post a status update every working day this week? Is there a deny rule for .env in your project settings?

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Letting the agent answer business questions

"Can staff cancel inside 24h?" is the client's decision. An agent will pick an answer to keep going. Take the question to the PM instead.

✗ Going silent when stuck

Two hours stuck without saying so is normal. Two days is a problem. Post what you tried, what you expected and what happened, then ask.

✗ Measuring yourself by lines shipped

The agent can produce lines without limit. Your mentor measures clarity, correctness and whether you can explain the work.

✓ Write "Unsure:" in your PR

Naming what you haven't verified points the reviewer at the risky part. Reviewers trust interns who do this.

✓ Ask the tester first

Asking QA "how will you test this?" before you build gives you the acceptance tests for free and avoids a round trip.

✓ Predict before every run

One written prediction per command, every day, is the fastest way to build a real mental model of a codebase.
