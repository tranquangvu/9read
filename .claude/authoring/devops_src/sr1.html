SLIs, SLOs, Error Budgets & Alerting

You can already collect metrics in CloudWatch, Prometheus and Datadog. This module decides which of those numbers matter to a patient trying to book an appointment, how reliable they need to be, and when a human should be woken up. You will pick SLIs for each clinic booking service, do the SLO and error-budget maths with real traffic numbers, write an error budget policy the product owner signs, and replace threshold alerts with multi-window burn-rate alerts in PromQL, Datadog and CloudWatch.

Why it matters in production: most teams have two failure modes at once. They page on CPU at 80% at 3 a.m. while nothing is wrong for users, and they miss the slow leak of 2% failed bookings that lasts a whole afternoon because no single threshold crossed. SLOs fix both. They give engineering and product one shared number for "reliable enough", they tell you when to stop shipping features and fix things, and they make every page mean "users are hurting and the budget is burning". Without them, reliability is an argument about opinions.
Part I · SRE principles

100% is the wrong target, and the right one is a number you agree on

Site Reliability Engineering is what you get when you treat operations as a software problem with a measurable goal. The goal is never "never fail". It is "fail rarely enough that users don't care, and spend everything above that on shipping". The vocabulary is small. Learn it precisely, because people mix these words up in every meeting and the mix-up causes bad decisions.

SLI, SLO, SLA: three different things

An SLI is a measurement. An SLO is a target for that measurement over a window. An SLA is a contract with a penalty. Your SLO must be stricter than any SLA you sign, so you get warned internally long before the client gets a refund. Most of our client contracts have no SLA at all, and that is fine: the SLO is still the tool you run the service with.

TermWhat it isClinic booking exampleWho owns it
SLIRatio of good events to valid events, 0–100%Share of POST /bookings requests that did not return 5xxEngineering
SLOTarget for an SLI over a window99.9% over a rolling 30 daysEngineering + product, signed together
Error budget1 − SLO: the failure you are allowed0.1% = 43.2 minutes, or about 10,400 failed requests per 30 daysSpent by whoever ships changes
SLAContract with a financial penalty"99.5% monthly or 10% service credit"Account management, legal

Note the order of strictness: the SLA (99.5%) is looser than the SLO (99.9%), which is looser than what you usually achieve. If those three ever invert, someone signed a contract engineering cannot keep.

Each extra nine costs roughly ten times more

Users reach the booking platform through home Wi-Fi and mobile networks that are themselves around 99–99.9% available. Above a certain point, extra reliability on your side is invisible to them, but each nine still demands more redundancy, slower releases and more on-call effort. Memorise this table. You will use it in every SLO conversation.

Dependencies multiply, and AWS SLAs are not predictions

When booking-api needs the ALB, Fargate, RDS and Valkey on every request, a failure in any one fails the request. Serial availabilities multiply. Using the published AWS SLA figures as a floor: 

# ALB 99.99% × ECS/Fargate 99.99% × ElastiCache Multi-AZ 99.99% × RDS Multi-AZ 99.95%
python3 -c "print(0.9999 * 0.9999 * 0.9999 * 0.9995)"
0.99920018...
# ≈ 99.92%: on paper, a 99.9% SLO leaves only 0.02% for your own bugs and deploys

In practice those services run far above their SLA, which is a refund threshold, not a forecast. The useful lessons are different. First, every hard dependency on the request path lowers your ceiling, so make the ones you can soft (the booking flow should still work if Valkey is down, just slower). Second, you cannot promise 99.99% on top of a single-region RDS instance. The infra side of this, multi-AZ and failover, is SR3. The application side (timeouts, retries, fallbacks) is intern SD10.

Toil and the 50% rule: SRE teams cap operational work at about half their time and spend the rest removing the causes of that work. The definition of toil and how to measure it is in FN1; reducing it through on-call is in SR2. Here it matters for one reason: an SLO that is too strict produces toil, because every small blip becomes urgent.
Part II · Choosing SLIs

Measure what the patient feels, as close to the patient as you can

A good SLI correlates with user happiness. CPU, memory, queue depth and pod restarts do not: a pod can restart every hour behind a load balancer and no patient notices. Choose a small number of SLIs per service (two or three), each expressed as a ratio of good events to valid events, and say exactly where you measure them.

The SLI menu by service type

Different services fail in different ways, so they need different SLIs. Start from this menu and pick the one or two that would make a user complain. For the clinic platform the answer is three services and five SLIs.

Service typeSLI kindsGood event meansClinic service
Request/response APIAvailability, latencyNon-5xx response; response faster than a thresholdbooking-api
Async worker / pipelineFreshness, correctnessJob finished within N minutes of being enqueued; output validbooking-worker (reminder SMS and emails)
Web frontendPage load, interaction latency, JS errorsLCP under 2.5 s from real browsers (RUM)booking-web
StorageDurability, availabilityObject written is readable laterS3 uploads: we rely on AWS here and do not set our own SLO

Valid events matters as much as good events. Exclude health checks, requests from your own synthetic monitors, and 4xx caused by the client (a 400 for a malformed date is not your failure; a 429 from your own rate limiter usually is). Write the exclusions down, because they decide whether the SLI is honest.

Where you measure changes what you see

The same request can be measured at four points. The further from the user, the more failures you miss: an app metric never sees the request that the ALB rejected because all targets were unhealthy. We default to the load balancer for availability, because it sees every request, and to the application histogram for latency per route, because it has the route label.

The clinic booking SLO sheet

This is the document you agree with the product owner. It names the SLI, the exact measurement, the target and the window. It lives in clinic-infra/slo/ next to the rules generated from it, so a change to a target is a reviewed PR like any other.

Traffic assumption for all maths in this module: booking-api averages 4 requests per second on those routes, so 4 × 86,400 × 30 = 10,368,000 requests per 30 days, and 0.1% of that is 10,368 failed requests. Notice latency is measured only over non-5xx requests: a fast error should not count as a good latency event.

Turning the sheet into SLI recording rules

In Prometheus (OB2) an SLI is a recording rule that computes the error ratio over a window. OB2 already records the 5m, 30m, 1h and 6h windows that the two paging burn-rate alerts in Part IV need; the ticket tier adds 2h, 1d and 3d. The metric is the OpenTelemetry HTTP server histogram (OB4), so the labels follow semantic conventions.

clinic-infra/slo/prometheus/booking-api-sli.yaml 
groups:
  - name: slo-booking-api-sli
    interval: 30s
    rules:
      - record: slo:sli_error:ratio_rate5m
        labels: { slo: booking-api-availability }
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_count{job="booking-api",
                http_route=~"/bookings|/slots", http_response_status_code=~"5.."}[5m]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_count{job="booking-api",
                http_route=~"/bookings|/slots"}[5m]))
      # repeat with [30m] [1h] [6h] (OB2's set), plus [2h] [1d] [3d] for the ticket tier
      - record: slo:sli_error:ratio_rate5m
        labels: { slo: booking-api-latency }
        expr: |
          1 - (
            sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="booking-api",
                  http_route=~"/bookings|/slots", http_response_status_code!~"5..", le="0.3"}[5m]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_count{job="booking-api",
                  http_route=~"/bookings|/slots", http_response_status_code!~"5.."}[5m]))
          )                                      # le="0.3" must be a real bucket boundary
The bucket boundary trap: a latency SLI of "under 300 ms" only works if the histogram has a bucket at exactly 0.3 s. The OTel default HTTP buckets include 0.25 and 0.5 but not 0.3, so the query above silently returns nothing. Either set explicit bucket boundaries in the SDK view, or pick an SLO threshold that matches an existing bucket (250 ms). Check with count by (le) (http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="booking-api"}) before you publish the SLO.
Part III · SLO maths & error budgets

The error budget turns reliability arguments into arithmetic

An SLO by itself is a dashboard. The error budget is what makes it a management tool: a quantity of failure that the team may spend on releases, experiments and migrations, and that product and engineering agree to protect when it runs low. The maths is simple. Getting the window, the policy and the burn rate right is where judgement comes in.

Choosing the window

The window decides how long a bad day is remembered. Short windows forgive quickly and make the budget jumpy. Long windows are stable but let a team coast on a good quarter. We use a rolling 30 days for operations and report calendar months to clients only where a contract demands it.

WindowRight whenCatch
Rolling 30 dDay-to-day decisions, alerting, release gatingA big outage stays in the number for exactly 30 days, then drops out at once
Rolling 28 dTraffic with a strong weekly pattern: always four MondaysBudget minutes differ from the "30 days" people expect (99.9% = 40.3 min)
Calendar monthSLA reporting, client monthly reportsBudget resets on the 1st, so the team takes risks at month end
QuarterPlanning conversations, headcount for reliability workToo slow to drive any weekly decision
clinic-infra/slo/budget.py — the numbers in a weekly review 
def budget_report(slo: float, total: int, bad: int, window_days: int = 30):
    allowed = (1 - slo) * total                # bad events you may have
    remaining = 1 - bad / allowed
    minutes = (1 - slo) * window_days * 1440
    return f"allowed={allowed:,.0f} bad={bad:,} remaining={remaining:.0%} ({minutes*remaining:.1f} of {minutes:.1f} min)"

print(budget_report(0.999, total=10_368_000, bad=6_220))
allowed=10,368 bad=6,220 remaining=40% (17.3 of 43.2 min)

"60% spent, 40% left, 17 minutes of full outage to go" is a sentence a product owner understands. That sentence, once a week in the team channel, does more for reliability than any dashboard.

The "it wasn't our fault" exclusion destroys the policy. If an RDS failover or a third-party SMS outage is excluded from the budget, the SLO stops measuring what patients felt, and the team never invests in the Multi-AZ or fallback design that would have hidden it. Count everything. Then argue, with the number in hand, about whether the fix is worth it.

Burn rate: how fast you are spending

Burn rate is the current error ratio divided by the ratio the SLO allows. A burn rate of 1 spends exactly the whole budget over the window. A burn rate of 14.4 spends it in 30 × 24 / 14.4 = 50 hours. The burn rate is the number you alert on, because it is independent of traffic volume and of the SLO target. 

burn_rate      = error_ratio / (1 − SLO)
budget_spent   = burn_rate × elapsed / window
time_to_empty  = window / burn_rate

# booking-api, SLO 99.9% → allowed ratio 0.001
1.44% errors   → burn 14.4 → 2% of budget per hour  → empty in 50 h
0.6% errors    → burn 6    → 5% of budget per 6 h   → empty in 5 days
0.1% errors    → burn 1    → 10% of budget per 3 d  → empty in exactly 30 days

SLOs as code in Datadog and CloudWatch

Prometheus rules are one implementation. Datadog has first-class SLO objects with built-in budget and burn-rate widgets (OB3), and CloudWatch Application Signals offers SLOs on its service metrics. Whichever you use, define it in Terraform in clinic-infra, never by clicking, so the target in the tool always matches the signed sheet.

clinic-infra/slo/datadog.tf 
resource "datadog_service_level_objective" "booking_api_availability" {
  name        = "booking-api availability"
  type        = "metric"
  description = "Non-5xx share of ALB requests to booking-api. Sheet: slo/README.md"
  query {
    numerator   = "sum:aws.applicationelb.request_count{env:prod,service:booking-api}.as_count() - sum:aws.applicationelb.httpcode_target_5xx{env:prod,service:booking-api}.as_count() - sum:aws.applicationelb.httpcode_elb_5xx{env:prod,service:booking-api}.as_count()"
    denominator = "sum:aws.applicationelb.request_count{env:prod,service:booking-api}.as_count()"
  }
  thresholds {
    timeframe = "30d"
    target    = 99.9
    warning   = 99.95     # dashboard turns yellow before the budget is gone
  }
  tags = ["service:booking-api", "env:prod", "team:clinic"]
}

The ALB-level SLI counts httpcode_elb_5xx too: those are the 502s and 503s the load balancer itself returns when no target is healthy, which is exactly the failure an app metric cannot see. The service tag on ALB metrics comes from the AWS resource tags imported by the Datadog AWS integration, so tag the target group and load balancer in Terraform. OB3 shows the same object with an illustrative 99.5% target; the clinic's signed booking-api target is 99.9%.

Part IV · Alerting

Page on symptoms that burn budget; ticket everything else

An alert is a request for a human's attention. A page is a request at 3 a.m. Both should be rare, and both should mean the same thing every time: users are hurting, or will be soon, and someone has to act. Burn-rate alerts on SLOs give you that property almost for free. Everything else becomes a dashboard panel or a ticket.

Why threshold alerts fail both ways

Judge an alert on four properties: precision (how often a firing is real), recall (how many real problems fire it), detection time and reset time (how long it keeps firing after the fix). At 4 requests per second, a 5-minute window holds 1,200 requests, so "0.1% errors" is 1.2 failed requests. That is noise, not a page.

Alert rulePrecisionDetectionVerdict
Error ratio > 0.1% over 5 minVery low: two failed requests fire itFastPages every night. The team learns to ignore it.
Error ratio > 0.1% over 30 daysHighHours to days, and never resets in timeA report, not an alert
Burn > 14.4 over 1 h onlyGoodFull outage: about 5 minKeeps firing up to an hour after the fix
Burn > 14.4 over 1 h and 5 minGoodFull outage: about 5 minResets within 5 min of the fix. Our default.
CPU > 80% on booking-apiUnrelated to usersn/aDelete it. Autoscaling (CN2, KS8) handles it.

Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts

The standard from the Google SRE Workbook uses three or four pairs. Each pair has a long window that proves the burn is significant and a short window, one twelfth of the long one, that proves it is still happening. We use two paging pairs, fast burn (1 h + 5 min) and slow burn (6 h + 30 min), on the windows OB2 already records, plus an optional ticket tier for very slow leaks. This replaces the static "5xx > 2%" placeholder alarm from the OB modules: at a 99.9% SLO, 2% is a burn rate of 20, so the placeholder both misses slow leaks and pages on short blips.

Low traffic breaks burn-rate maths. Between midnight and 6 a.m. the clinic API sees a few requests a minute. Two failures out of 30 in a 5-minute window is a 6.7% error ratio and a burn rate of 67. The same alerts in Datadog and CloudWatch

Datadog has burn-rate monitors built into its SLO objects: you give it the SLO, the long and short windows and the threshold. CloudWatch has no burn-rate primitive, so you build it from two metric-math alarms and a composite alarm (OB1). Where the SLO alert lives follows our signal routing: app metrics go to CloudWatch for ECS services and to Prometheus (AMP) for EKS services, and to Datadog when the client pays for it (our Datadog site is ap1.datadoghq.com). AWS-resource alarms stay in CloudWatch regardless. Pick one place to page from per service. Paging from both is how one outage wakes two people with two different alerts.

clinic-infra/slo/datadog.tf — burn-rate monitor 
resource "datadog_monitor" "booking_api_burn_fast" {
  name    = "[clinic] booking-api burning error budget fast"
  type    = "slo alert"
  query   = "burn_rate(\"${datadog_service_level_objective.booking_api_availability.id}\").over(\"30d\").long_window(\"1h\").short_window(\"5m\") > 14.4"

    booking-api is spending its 30-day budget 14.4x faster than allowed.
    Runbook: https://github.com/golden-owl/clinic-infra/blob/main/runbooks/booking-api-5xx.md
    @pagerduty-clinic-prod
  EOT
  monitor_thresholds { critical = 14.4 }
  tags = ["service:booking-api", "env:prod", "severity:page"]
}

Alert design: symptoms, actionability, and a page budget

Page on symptoms (users see errors or slowness), not causes (a pod restarted, CPU is high). Causes belong on the dashboard you open after the page. The one exception is a cause that will certainly become an outage with no symptom first: disk filling, a certificate expiring, RDS storage running out. Alert on those predictively, as tickets, days before they hit.

Instead of paging on…Do this
Pod restarted / task stoppedDashboard panel. If restarts cause errors, the burn-rate alert fires anyway.
RDS CPU > 90%Latency SLO burn pages; RDS CPU is the first panel in the runbook
SQS queue depth > 1,000Reminder freshness SLO; queue age is the cause you check
Disk 85% fullTicket on predict_linear(node_filesystem_avail_bytes[6h], 4*3600) < 0: full within 4 h
ACM certificate expiryTicket at 30 days via DaysToExpiry; page only at 7 days

Before any new page goes live, it must pass all five checks:

  • Urgent: waiting until 9 a.m. would hurt users or the budget measurably.
  • Actionable: the responder can do something now, and the runbook says what (SR2 owns runbooks).
  • Unique: no other alert fires for the same failure. Group by service, not by pod.
  • Owned: it routes to one team's rotation through Alertmanager or the Datadog handle (OB2).
  • Linked: the notification carries the runbook URL and the SLO dashboard.
Set a page budget, too: no more than two paging incidents per 12-hour on-call shift, averaged over a month. Review every page in the weekly ops sync and tag it actionable or noise. If fewer than 80% are actionable, fixing the alerts takes priority over new alerts. On the clinic platform this review deleted 23 of 31 legacy CloudWatch alarms in the first month.

Golden signals, RED and USE: for dashboards, not pages

Once the page fires, you need to find the cause fast. The three classic signal sets tell you what to put on the dashboard so that the first screen answers "what is broken". Use RED for services and USE for resources, and build the top row of every service dashboard from the four golden signals.

Red flags in AI-written SLOs and alerts: an SLI built from CPU, memory or pod restarts; avg() of per-pod error ratios instead of sum(errors) / sum(requests); histogram_quantile used as an SLI (a p99 is not a ratio of good events and cannot be budgeted); rate() over a window shorter than four scrape intervals; a for: 1h on a burn-rate page; a 99.99% target with no multi-region design; and health-check endpoints left in the denominator, which inflate the SLI.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Setting the SLO from current performance

"We did 99.97% last month, so the SLO is 99.97%" leaves zero budget for releases. Set it from what users need, then check that history clears it with room to spare.

✗ Averaging percentiles

The mean of three pods' p99s is not the service's p99. Aggregate histogram buckets with sum by (le) first, or better, use a good-events ratio that sums cleanly.

✗ An SLO nobody acts on

A Datadog SLO widget with no signed policy is decoration. If an exhausted budget does not change what ships next week, you have a metric, not an SLO.

✓ Start with one SLO per service

Availability for booking-api, freshness for the worker. Run them for a month, review the pages, then add latency. Ten SLOs on day one means nobody reads any of them.

✓ Annotate deploys on the budget graph

Push a Grafana or Datadog event from the deploy job (CI2). When the budget drops, the first question is "what changed?", and the answer should be on the chart.
