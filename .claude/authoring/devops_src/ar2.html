FinOps: Cost Visibility & Optimisation

At L2 you knew what your own resources cost. At L3 you own the bill for the whole clinic platform: five accounts, ECS and EKS, RDS, Redis, S3, CloudFront and a lot of NAT traffic nobody asked for. This module runs the FinOps lifecycle (inform, optimise, operate) the way a senior engineer does it. You make every dollar attributable to a service and a team, query the Cost and Usage Report with Athena, choose commitments and Spot with numbers rather than vibes, find the data-transfer traps, and turn it into a unit cost that the client's CFO and your engineers both understand: dollars per 1,000 bookings.

Why it matters in production: cost incidents are real incidents, they just page finance instead of you. A debug log level left on in prod pushes CloudWatch Logs ingestion to USD 2,400 in a week. A worker that downloads uploads through a NAT gateway turns into a four-figure line item. A Savings Plan bought on a spiky month sits 40% unused for three years. None of these show up in a latency graph. The engineer who can explain the bill line by line, and cut it without touching reliability, is the one a client trusts with the platform.
Part I · The FinOps lifecycle

Cost is an engineering metric that finance happens to read

FinOps is not a quarterly cost-cutting sprint. It is a loop, run by engineers, with finance and product as stakeholders. The FinOps Foundation calls the phases Inform (see and attribute spend), Optimise (change rates and usage) and Operate (make it continuous: budgets, alerts, reviews, ownership). Teams fail when they jump straight to optimise. Without attribution you cut the wrong thing, and without operate the savings drift back within two quarters.

Inform, optimise, operate: a loop, not a project

Each phase produces an artifact the next one depends on. Inform produces an allocated bill: every dollar has a service and an env. Optimise produces a ranked list of changes with expected savings. Operate produces the guardrails that keep the savings: budgets, anomaly alerts, a monthly review and a unit-cost trend line. You go round the loop monthly, and each turn should be cheaper to run than the last.

PhaseCrawlWalkRun (our target)
InformCost Explorer by accountTags activated, 70% of spend allocatedCUR 2.0 in Athena, ≥ 95% allocated, shared costs split by rule
OptimiseDelete obvious idle resourcesRightsizing from Compute Optimizer, first Savings PlanCommitment coverage 70–80% of baseline, Spot for every interruptible workload

Be honest about where a client is. Most clients we inherit are at crawl in all three rows. Moving Inform to walk is usually a week of work and pays for everything else.

Who owns which dollar

The senior job is not to approve every spend. It is to make sure every dollar has an owner who can see it and change it. Centralised cost-cutting by the DevOps team does not scale: you will never know whether booking-worker really needs 4 vCPU. The team that runs the service does. Your job is the system around them.

Use amortised cost, not unblended, for decisions. Unblended cost shows a one-year upfront Savings Plan as a spike in the month you bought it and zero afterwards. Amortised (and net amortised, after credits and discounts) spreads the commitment over its term, so a service's cost does not jump just because finance paid an invoice.

What you decline to build

A senior engineer protects the team from FinOps theatre. Every one of these has been proposed to us by a client, and every one cost more in engineering time than it saved:

  • A custom cost dashboard app. Athena plus a Grafana or QuickSight dashboard covers it. AWS's own Cloud Intelligence Dashboards are free templates. Build the queries, not a product.
  • Per-request cost attribution in application code. Allocate by service and divide by business volume. Accuracy beyond ±10% does not change any decision you will make.
  • A lambda that stops dev every night when dev costs USD 600 a month. Two engineer-days to build and maintain is worth more than the saving. Do it with an ECS scheduled scaling action or Karpenter limits instead, or not at all.
  • Chargeback invoices between internal teams. Showback (visible, not billed) gets 90% of the behaviour change with none of the accounting disputes.
Part II · Inform — visibility and allocation

If a dollar has no owner tag, it has no owner

Allocation is built from three layers: accounts (the cheapest, most reliable split, from AR1), tags (split inside an account by service and team) and rules for what cannot be tagged (support fees, shared NAT, the EKS control plane). Get the first two right in Terraform and the third becomes a short SQL view.

A tag schema small enough to enforce

Every client wants fifteen tags. Enforce four. A tag that is optional is a tag that is missing on 40% of resources, and a 60%-tagged bill is not much better than an untagged one. These four answer every allocation question we have been asked. Exact casing is enforced by the tag policy below.

Tag keyAllowed valuesAnswersSet by
servicebooking-api, booking-web, booking-worker, platformWhat does each service cost?Module input, required
envdev, staging, prod, sharedHow much is non-prod overhead?Provider default_tags
teamclinic, platformWhose budget is it?Provider default_tags
cost-centerThe client's finance code, e.g. CC-4410Which invoice line?Provider default_tags

Put three of them in the provider so nobody can forget them, and make service a required variable of every internal module (module design is in IA2):

clinic-infra/envs/prod/providers.tf

default_tags do not reach everything. Resources created by AWS on your behalf are not tagged by Terraform: EBS volumes and ENIs that Karpenter or ECS create, Fargate tasks, Auto Scaling instances unless propagate_at_launch is set. Set propagateTags = SERVICE on ECS services, tags in the Karpenter EC2NodeClass, and tag_specifications in launch templates. Then check: the untagged-spend query below must trend towards zero.

Enforce in the organisation, then activate for billing

Two steps people forget. An Organizations tag policy standardises keys and values (so you never get Service, service and svc) and can block non-compliant tagging for listed resource types. Then the tag must be activated as a cost allocation tag in the management account, or it never appears in Cost Explorer or the CUR. Activation is not retroactive, so do it on day one.

Five queries that explain any bill

You do not need a hundred queries. You need a handful you trust, saved as Athena named queries in Terraform. The first one gives you allocated cost by service with amortised commitments, which is the number every other report builds on. Always filter on the billing_period partition: a query without it scans every month and costs real money on a large CUR.

finops/queries/01_cost_by_service.sql 
SELECT
  coalesce(nullif(resource_tags['user_service'], ''), 'UNTAGGED') AS service,
  round(sum(CASE line_item_line_item_type
    WHEN 'SavingsPlanCoveredUsage' THEN savings_plan_savings_plan_effective_cost
    WHEN 'DiscountedUsage'         THEN reservation_effective_cost
    WHEN 'SavingsPlanNegation'     THEN 0       -- already counted above
    ELSE line_item_unblended_cost END), 2) AS amortised_usd
FROM cur2.clinic
WHERE billing_period = '2026-09'              -- partition filter: never omit
  AND line_item_line_item_type NOT IN ('Tax', 'Credit', 'Refund')
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 2 DESC;
service          amortised_usd
platform               3790.12
booking-api            3355.40
booking-worker         2398.77
UNTAGGED               1921.04
booking-web             711.25
(Run time: 2.8 sec, Data scanned: 41.2 MB)

The other four you save next to it: 02 untagged spend by line_item_resource_id (fix list for tagging), 03 month-on-month delta by usage type (what moved), 04 data transfer and NAT bytes (Part III), and 05 unit cost (Part IV). Share costs such as platform are then split back to services in a view, using a rule the client agrees to in writing, for example proportional to each service's vCPU-hours.

Part III · Optimise — usage first, then rate

Shrink what you run, then commit to what is left

There are two levers. Usage levers change how much you run: rightsizing, scheduling, deleting idle resources, fixing traffic paths. Rate levers change what you pay per unit: Savings Plans, Reserved Instances, Spot, Graviton. The order matters. Commit first and you lock in a discount on capacity you were about to delete. Rightsize first, measure the new floor for two to four weeks, then commit to it.

Rightsizing with Compute Optimizer, read sceptically

Compute Optimizer looks at 14 days of CloudWatch metrics (up to 93 with enhanced infrastructure metrics, worth enabling for prod) and recommends sizes for EC2, Auto Scaling groups, EBS, Lambda, ECS on Fargate and RDS. It is a good first pass and a bad final answer: it does not see memory unless the CloudWatch agent publishes it, it does not know about your Black Monday booking spike, and it cannot see that a task is CPU-bound only during startup. 

aws compute-optimizer get-ecs-service-recommendations --profile gol-prod --region ap-southeast-1 \
  --query 'ecsServiceRecommendations[].[finding,
           currentServiceConfiguration.cpu, currentServiceConfiguration.memory,
           serviceRecommendationOptions[0].cpu, serviceRecommendationOptions[0].memory,
           serviceRecommendationOptions[0].savingsOpportunity.estimatedMonthlySavings.value, serviceArn]' --output text

For EKS, rightsizing means requests, not instances: Karpenter picks instances to fit the requests you declare, so over-requested pods are the waste (method in KS8). For RDS, the biggest wins are usually a stale db.r6g.2xlarge from the launch week, and gp2 to gp3 storage (about 20% cheaper per GB with 3,000 baseline IOPS included). A senior review of the clinic platform typically finds 15–25% in usage savings before any commitment is bought.

Savings Plans vs Reserved Instances: buy the floor, not the average

A commitment is a bet that you will use at least this much for one or three years. The safe size is the floor of your steady hourly spend after rightsizing (the lowest hour of the last month, roughly), not the average. Buying to the average means part of the commitment goes unused in every quiet hour, and unused commitment is pure loss.

A commitment is a three-year decision someone else pays for. Never buy one from a single month of data, never buy three-year terms for a client whose contract with us is one year, and never buy before a migration (ECS→EKS, x86→Graviton) unless it is a Compute Savings Plan. Write the purchase as a short RFC with the coverage model, the break-even, and the client's written approval.

Spot: an architecture decision, not a checkbox

Spot is 60–90% cheaper and can be taken back with two minutes' notice. Whether a workload belongs on Spot depends on how it behaves when interrupted, not on how much you want the saving. Diversify across many instance types and all three AZs so one capacity pool running dry does not take the service down.

WorkloadSpot?Why
booking-worker (SQS consumer)Yes, 100%Idempotent jobs, visibility timeout returns an interrupted message to the queue
CI runners, dev and staging nodesYesA retried job costs minutes, not patients
booking-apiPartly: on-demand base, Spot above itKeep enough on-demand to serve the SLO if all Spot vanishes; ECS capacity provider base or a Karpenter on-demand NodePool
Postgres, Redis, Argo CD, Karpenter itselfNoStateful or control plane: an interruption is an outage

Moving booking-worker to Fargate Spot (CN2) saved USD 640 a month on the clinic bill. Interruptions in the first month: 11, every one absorbed by the queue with zero failed bookings. That last number is the one to put in the report.

The NAT gateway trap and other data-transfer surprises

Data transfer is the line that grows without anyone deploying anything. The worst offender is the NAT gateway: in ap-southeast-1 it costs about USD 0.059 per hour plus USD 0.059 per GB processed, and every byte from a private subnet to S3, ECR or any AWS public endpoint goes through it unless a VPC endpoint gives it a shorter path (endpoint design is in AW2).

finops/queries/04_nat_and_transfer.sql 
SELECT line_item_usage_account_id AS account,
       line_item_usage_type          AS usage_type,
       line_item_resource_id         AS resource,
       round(sum(line_item_usage_amount), 0)   AS gb,
       round(sum(line_item_unblended_cost), 2) AS usd
FROM cur2.clinic
WHERE billing_period = '2026-09'
  AND (line_item_usage_type LIKE '%NatGateway-Bytes'             -- NAT processing
       OR line_item_usage_type LIKE '%DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes'  -- cross-AZ, both directions
       OR line_item_usage_type LIKE '%DataTransfer-Out-Bytes')      -- to the internet
GROUP BY 1, 2, 3 ORDER BY usd DESC LIMIT 5;

Part IV · Operate — make it stick

Savings you do not guard are savings you lose by next quarter

Every optimisation decays. New services launch untagged, a load test leaves 20 tasks running, someone turns on debug logging "for an hour". Operate is the set of cheap, automatic checks that catch this within a day instead of on next month's invoice, plus the one number that tells the client whether the platform is getting more efficient.

Budgets in Terraform, alerting on forecast

A budget that alerts on actual spend tells you on the 28th that the month went wrong. Alert on the forecast at 80% and on actual at 100%, send it to the team that owns the service, and keep the budgets in clinic-infra next to the services they watch. Budgets without actions cost nothing; budgets with actions are free for the first two and about USD 0.10 a day each after that.

clinic-infra/finops/budgets.tf — gol-management 
locals {
  service_budgets = { "booking-api" = 3500, "booking-worker" = 2000, "platform" = 3900 }  # USD/month, agreed with the client
}

resource "aws_budgets_budget" "service" {
  for_each     = local.service_budgets
  name         = "clinic-${each.key}-monthly"
  budget_type  = "COST"
  limit_amount = each.value
  limit_unit   = "USD"
  time_unit    = "MONTHLY"
  cost_filter {
    name   = "TagKeyValue"
    values = [format("user:service$%s", each.key)]  # API wants "user:<key>$<value>"
  }
  notification {
    comparison_operator       = "GREATER_THAN"
    threshold                 = 80
    threshold_type            = "PERCENTAGE"
    notification_type         = "FORECASTED"   # early warning, not a post-mortem
    subscriber_sns_topic_arns = [aws_sns_topic.finops_alerts.arn]
  }
  notification {
    comparison_operator       = "GREATER_THAN"
    threshold                 = 100
    threshold_type            = "PERCENTAGE"
    notification_type         = "ACTUAL"
    subscriber_sns_topic_arns = [aws_sns_topic.finops_alerts.arn]
  }
}

Route the SNS topic to Slack through AWS Chatbot (now Amazon Q Developer in chat applications), into the #clinic-cost channel the service team reads, not a DevOps inbox. Dev and staging get one account-level budget each, with a budget action that applies a deny-launch SCP at 120% in gol-dev only. Never attach automatic actions to prod.

Anomaly detection for what budgets miss

Budgets catch a month going over. Cost Anomaly Detection catches a rate change: CloudWatch Logs jumping from USD 40 to USD 340 a day is well within a monthly budget for the first week, and an anomaly on day one. It is free and uses machine-learned baselines per service, so give it a sensible absolute threshold or it will alert on USD 3 wobbles and get muted.

resource "aws_ce_anomaly_monitor" "by_service" {

  monitor_type      = "DIMENSIONAL"

}

resource "aws_ce_anomaly_subscription" "slack" {
  name             = "clinic-anomalies-immediate"
  frequency        = "IMMEDIATE"              # IMMEDIATE requires an SNS subscriber
  monitor_arn_list = [aws_ce_anomaly_monitor.by_service.arn]
  subscriber {
    type    = "SNS"

Unit economics: dollars per 1,000 bookings

A total bill that goes up is not bad news if bookings went up faster. A unit cost turns the bill into a metric the client's product owner can reason about and your engineers can move. Pick a unit that tracks value (confirmed bookings, not HTTP requests), take the allocated cost from CUR, and divide by the count from the application database or analytics, exported daily to S3.

finops/queries/05_unit_cost.sql 
WITH cost AS (
  SELECT billing_period, sum(amortised_usd) AS usd
  FROM finops.allocated_cost                  -- view from query 01 + shared-cost split
  WHERE env = 'prod' AND billing_period BETWEEN '2026-04' AND '2026-09'
  GROUP BY 1
), volume AS (
  SELECT date_format(booked_at, '%Y-%m') AS billing_period, count(*) AS bookings
  FROM analytics.confirmed_bookings           -- daily export from booking-api, no PII
  GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT c.billing_period, c.usd, v.bookings,
       round(1000 * c.usd / v.bookings, 2) AS usd_per_1k_bookings
FROM cost c JOIN volume v USING (billing_period) ORDER BY 1;
billing_period   usd       bookings   usd_per_1k_bookings

The monthly FinOps report and review

The L3 capstone asks for a FinOps report that cuts the bill by a measured amount. "Measured" means before and after, from CUR, with the reliability impact stated. One page, the same structure every month, reviewed in a 30-minute meeting with the client's product owner and the service leads:

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Buying commitments before rightsizing

You lock a discount onto waste for a year. Shrink first, wait two to four weeks for the new floor, then commit to about 70–80% of it.

✗ Reporting unblended cost

Upfront payments and SP negation lines make unblended numbers swing wildly. Use net amortised for every trend and every "we saved X" claim.

✗ Cutting cost by cutting redundancy

Dropping RDS Multi-AZ or going to one NAT gateway saves a few hundred dollars and spends your error budget. Cost changes that touch availability go through the same RFC as any reliability change (SR3).

✓ Fix the tag at the source

Never patch tags by hand in the console. Fix the module, the Karpenter EC2NodeClass or the ECS propagate_tags, and the untagged query will stay clean.

✓ Start with Flow Logs on the NAT gateway

On any inherited account, the NAT and data-transfer query is the highest-return hour you will spend. It routinely finds four figures a month that one endpoint removes.
