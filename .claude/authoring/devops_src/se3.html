Compliance & Audit Readiness

Sooner or later a client hands you a security questionnaire, an auditor's request list or a regulator's checklist. This module teaches you to answer it from the platform you already run rather than from a weekend of screenshots. It covers the frameworks Golden Owl clients actually meet (SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI DSS and Vietnam's personal data rules), how one technical control maps onto all of them, AWS Audit Manager and AWS Artifact, automated evidence collection, audit-grade logging and retention, and the access review that every framework asks for. The clinic booking platform holds patient health data, which makes it a realistic and demanding example.

Why it matters in production: compliance failures rarely look like breaches. They look like a sales deal that stalls for three months because nobody can produce a SOC 2 report, or an auditor's finding that CloudTrail logs only go back 90 days when the framework requires a year. The engineering work is modest if it is designed in: retention settings, an evidence bucket, a quarterly review script. Retrofitting it under audit pressure costs weeks, and some evidence, such as last year's logs, cannot be recreated at any price.
Part I · The frameworks we meet

Frameworks differ in wording more than in the controls you build

A DevOps engineer does not need to be a compliance officer. You need to know what each framework is, who asks for it, what the auditor will want to see from the infrastructure, and where the legal questions start, so you can hand those to the right person. The good news: about 80% of what auditors ask the platform team for is the same across frameworks. Logging, access control, encryption, change management, vulnerability management, backups. You built most of it in SE1 and SE2.

Two differences shape the engineering work. SOC 2 Type II and ISO surveillance audits judge controls over time, so the auditor samples, say, 25 production changes from the last six months and asks for the PR, approval and deploy log for each. One good screenshot is not enough; you need a system that produced the evidence every time. PCI DSS is about scope: the fewer systems touch card data, the fewer requirements apply.

SOC 2 and ISO 27001 from the platform team's chair

Both frameworks ask the company to define its own controls and then prove it follows them. Neither says "use GuardDuty". The auditor checks that you wrote down a control ("production changes are peer-reviewed and deployed by the pipeline"), that it is suitably designed, and, for Type II, that it operated every time across the period. That makes your Git history, rulesets and pipeline logs the most valuable evidence you own.

When a client's spreadsheet cites "A.12.4.1", it is using the old numbering. Map to the current IDs and say so in your answer rather than mixing both.

PCI DSS: shrink the scope before you build anything

The clinic platform takes deposits for some bookings. If card numbers ever pass through booking-api, the whole cardholder data environment, which in practice means the VPC, the cluster, the CI system that deploys to it and every admin's laptop, falls under roughly 300 PCI DSS requirements. If the card form is hosted entirely by the payment provider, the platform never sees a card number and the merchant usually qualifies for SAQ A, about 30 requirements. That is a design decision worth more than any amount of hardening, and it has to be made before the first line of payment code.

Option B still leaves requirements for us. Under PCI DSS v4 the page that redirects to or embeds the payment form must be protected against script tampering (requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1, mandatory since 31 March 2025), which in practice means a strict Content Security Policy on app.clinic.example.com and monitoring for changes to the scripts on that page. The ZAP CSP rule from SE2 is now a compliance control. Which SAQ applies is decided by the client's acquirer, not by us; our job is to present the architecture clearly.

Vietnam's personal data rules: Decree 13 and the PDP Law

Vietnam regulated personal data first through Decree 13/2023/ND-CP (in force from 1 July 2023) and then through the Law on Personal Data Protection (Law No. 91/2025/QH15), passed in June 2025 and in force from 1 January 2026, which takes over from the decree, together with implementing decrees. The headline obligations below have been consistent across both, but thresholds, deadlines, exemptions for small companies and penalty amounts are set in detail by the law and its implementing rules and may have changed since this was written. Treat this table as an engineer's orientation, not legal advice.

The most useful thing the platform team can hand the client's legal team is a data map: every store and every processor that holds personal data, where it is physically located, and how it is protected. The impact assessments are built from it, and it answers the cross-border question with facts rather than guesses. Keep it in the repo next to the control matrix and update it in the same PR that adds a store or a vendor.

Check with legal before you promise anything. The PDP Law carries much larger penalties than Decree 13 (reported as up to 5% of the previous year's revenue for some cross-border transfer violations), and the implementing decrees fill in deadlines, dossier formats and exemptions. Do not tell a client "we are compliant" or "Singapore hosting is fine". Say what the platform does (where data is stored, how it is encrypted, who can access it, how fast you detect a breach) and let the client's legal counsel decide what the law requires of them. Also ask whether sector rules for healthcare or the cybersecurity and data laws add localisation requirements for this client.
Part II · Mapping controls to AWS

Build each control once and map it to every framework

The worst way to handle compliance is framework by framework: a SOC 2 project, then an ISO project, then a questionnaire, each producing its own spreadsheet and its own screenshots. Instead keep one control matrix: a list of the controls the platform actually implements, each mapped to the framework clauses it satisfies, the AWS service that implements it, and where its evidence comes from. A new framework then becomes a mapping exercise, not new engineering.

One control, many frameworks

Keep the matrix in the infra repo next to the code it describes, as YAML or CSV, so a PR that changes a control can update its row at the same time. A spreadsheet on a shared drive goes stale within a quarter.

AWS Artifact: the half of the audit AWS has already passed

Every framework covers the physical data centre, the hypervisor and the managed services' internals. You cannot audit those, and you don't need to: AWS publishes its own SOC 1/2/3 reports, ISO certificates and PCI DSS Attestation of Compliance in AWS Artifact. Your auditor treats AWS as a sub-service organization and reads its report, so your controls only start where the shared responsibility line does (SE1). Download reports from the gol-management or gol-security account, accept the report's terms once, and hand them to the auditor under the NDA they come with. 

aws artifact list-reports --profile gol-security --region us-east-1 \
  --query 'reports[?contains(name, `SOC 2`) || contains(name, `PCI`)].[name,periodStart,periodEnd]' --output table

"In scope" is per service. AWS's compliance reports list which services are covered. Before you introduce a newly launched service into a PCI or SOC 2 scoped system, check the AWS Services in Scope by Compliance Program page. A service that is not yet listed can block the audit or force a scope exclusion.

Tag where regulated data lives

Auditors and the personal data rules both start with "where is the sensitive data?" Answer it in metadata, not memory. Every data store carries a data-class tag (health, personal, internal, public), set in Terraform and enforced by a Config rule. Then "list every store with health data and show it is encrypted and logged" is one query instead of a week of interviews.

Part III · Evidence collection

Evidence is a pipeline, not a scramble before the auditor arrives

For a Type II audit the auditor will ask for the state of a control at dates in the past: "show me the IAM configuration on 14 March". If the only way to answer is today's console, you cannot. So collect evidence on a schedule, store it where it cannot be edited, and label it with the control ID. Two tools do most of the work: AWS Audit Manager for continuous collection mapped to framework controls, and a small scheduled job for everything it does not cover.

AWS Audit Manager: continuous collection, mapped to a framework

Audit Manager ships prebuilt frameworks (SOC 2, PCI DSS v4, ISO 27001, CIS and others). You create an assessment for a framework, a set of accounts and a set of services. It then gathers evidence automatically from Config rule results, Security Hub checks, CloudTrail activity and periodic API snapshots, and files each item under the matching control. Run it from gol-security as delegated administrator. Its limit: it collects and organises, it does not judge. A folder of 40,000 evidence items is not a passed audit. Someone still reviews the controls that matter and adds manual evidence for process controls such as access reviews.

clinic-infra/security/audit-manager/main.tf — runs in gol-security 
resource "aws_auditmanager_assessment" "soc2_2026" {
  name         = "clinic-soc2-type2-2026"
  framework_id = var.soc2_framework_id          # from: aws auditmanager list-assessment-frameworks
  roles {

A monthly evidence job for everything else

A scheduled GitHub Actions workflow in clinic-infra assumes a read-only evidence role (OIDC, as in CI1), exports a fixed set of JSON snapshots, and writes them to an Object Lock bucket under evidence/<control-id>/<yyyy-mm-dd>/. Raw JSON, not screenshots: it is searchable, diffable, and an auditor can trust a file with a timestamp and a hash more than a picture of a console.

clinic-infra/compliance/collect_evidence.py — excerpt 
import boto3, json, hashlib, datetime
today = datetime.date.today().isoformat()
s3 = boto3.client("s3")
BUCKET = "gol-security-audit-evidence"

def put(control, name, data):
    body = json.dumps(data, default=str, indent=2).encode()
    key = f"evidence/{control}/{today}/{name}.json"
    s3.put_object(Bucket=BUCKET, Key=key, Body=body,
                  ChecksumAlgorithm="SHA256")             # S3 stores and verifies the checksum
    print(f"{key}  sha256={hashlib.sha256(body).hexdigest()[:16]}")

ct = boto3.client("cloudtrail")
put("GOL-LOG-01", "trails", ct.describe_trails(includeShadowTrails=False)["trailList"])

sso = boto3.client("sso-admin")
inst = sso.list_instances()["Instances"][0]["InstanceArn"]
sets = sso.list_permission_sets(InstanceArn=inst)["PermissionSets"]
assignments = [a for ps in sets
               for a in sso.list_accounts_for_provisioned_permission_set(
                   InstanceArn=inst, PermissionSetArn=ps)["AccountIds"]]
put("GOL-IAM-02", "permission-set-accounts", {"sets": sets, "accounts": assignments})

Evidence can leak data. A credential report contains user names; a CloudTrail export can contain patient IDs in request parameters; a "sample of 25 bookings to prove encryption" is personal health data handed to a third party. Export configuration and metadata, never application data, and give the auditor a time-limited, read-only role or presigned links rather than a copy of the bucket.

Change management evidence comes free if the pipeline is strict

Change management is the control auditors sample most heavily, and it is where a clean GitOps setup pays off. If production can only change through a merged PR (rulesets requiring review, apply role trusted only from main, Argo CD with no manual sync rights), then every change has an author, a reviewer, a plan and a run log, linked by commit SHA. Your evidence for a sampled change is one link. The exception is the emergency change: define it in policy (break-glass role, incident ticket, retrospective PR within one business day), because the auditor will find the one time someone used the console.

Part IV · Retention, integrity & access reviews

Prove who could do what, and keep the proof long enough

Two questions come up in every audit and every serious incident: "can you show what happened on this date?" and "who had access to this at the time?" The first is a retention and integrity problem. The second is an access review. Both are cheap to get right in advance and impossible to fix afterwards.

Retention: decide per log type, then enforce it in code

Retention is a trade-off between evidence, cost and privacy. Keep security logs long; keep logs that contain personal data only as long as you need them, because personal data rules push the other way. PCI DSS is the most explicit framework: at least 12 months of audit logs, with the last 3 months immediately available. We use 400 days for security logs to cover a 12-month audit period plus the time it takes the auditor to ask.

clinic-infra/modules/log-archive/retention.tf 
resource "aws_s3_bucket_object_lock_configuration" "trail" {
  bucket = aws_s3_bucket.trail.id               # bucket created with object_lock_enabled = true
  rule {
    default_retention {
      mode = "COMPLIANCE"                        # nobody, including root, can shorten or delete
      days = 400
    }
  }
}

resource "aws_s3_bucket_lifecycle_configuration" "trail" {
  bucket = aws_s3_bucket.trail.id
  rule {
    id     = "archive-then-expire"
    status = "Enabled"
    filter {}
    transition {
      days          = 90
      storage_class = "GLACIER_IR"              # still millisecond reads for Athena
    }
    expiration {
      days = 1095                               # 3 years, then gone: retention has an end too
    }
  }
}
Compliance mode is irreversible. An object written under a 400-day compliance-mode lock cannot be deleted by anyone, including AWS support, until the 400 days pass. Test the configuration with GOVERNANCE mode and a 1-day period in gol-sandbox first. A typo of days = 4000 on a busy bucket is an 11-year storage bill you cannot cancel.

Integrity: prove the logs were not edited

Object Lock prevents deletion. CloudTrail log file validation proves that no file was changed: every hour CloudTrail writes a digest file containing a hash of each log file, signed with an AWS private key, and chained to the previous digest. Auditors for SOC 2 and PCI like to see this run. Run it yourself first, monthly, as part of the evidence job.

Quarterly access reviews that take an hour, not a week

Every framework asks for a periodic review of who has access to production and whether they still need it. The review itself is a human judgement. Everything around it can be automated: export the current state, diff it against last quarter, send each owner only the changes and the high-risk access, record the decisions, and open PRs to remove what was rejected. Because access is managed in Terraform (permission sets and assignments from AW1), a removal is a reviewed PR, which is itself evidence.

Audit fieldwork: the request list and the auditor's access

Fieldwork starts with a request list (often called the PBC list, "provided by client"): 80 to 200 items, of which the platform team typically owns 30 to 50. Answer each from the control matrix and evidence bucket, never from memory, and keep a log of what you sent. If the auditor needs to look themselves, give them a time-limited, read-only role in gol-security that expires on its own when fieldwork ends. Never give them a user in gol-prod.

        Bool            = { "aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent" = "true" }
      }
    }]
  })
}
resource "aws_iam_role_policy_attachment" "auditor" {
  role       = aws_iam_role.auditor.name
  policy_arn = "arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/SecurityAudit"      # config metadata, no data reads
}

SecurityAudit lets the auditor read configuration across services without reading S3 objects or database contents, so patient data stays out of reach. Every call they make lands in the org trail, which is a nice touch to show them. Delete the role in the PR that closes the audit.

Working with Claude Code

Compliance work is document-heavy, which suits an agent: mapping controls, drafting questionnaire answers from the control matrix, writing evidence exporters. The danger is confident text. An agent will write "the platform is compliant with Decree 13" or cite a control number that does not exist. Ground every answer in your matrix and evidence, and treat legal statements as out of scope for the agent and for you. 

Using compliance/controls.yaml as the only source of truth, draft answers to the attached
120-question vendor security questionnaire. For each answer cite the control ID. If no control
covers a question, answer "GAP" and say what evidence would be needed. Do not invent controls.
Extend compliance/collect_evidence.py to export (1) GitHub rulesets for clinic-app and
clinic-infra via the REST API and (2) EKS access entries for clinic-prod-eks. Use read-only
calls only, write JSON with a SHA-256 checksum, and add a --dry-run flag that prints keys only.
Here is our Identity Center assignment export for this quarter and last quarter. Produce
the review sheet: added, removed and unchanged assignments per team, with every direct USER
assignment and every admin permission set on gol-prod flagged for an explicit decision.
Let the agent do
  • Draft control-to-framework mappings for you to verify
  • Answer questionnaires from the control matrix, marking gaps
  • Write read-only evidence exporters and access-review diffs
  • Summarise a framework section into the infra controls it implies
You must check
  • Every cited clause exists in the version the client is audited against
  • Answers describe what the platform does, not what a framework requires
  • Evidence exports contain no patient or personal data
  • Legal conclusions about Vietnamese law go to the client's counsel
Red flags in AI-written compliance work: "fully compliant with SOC 2 / PDPL" claims; ISO 27001:2013 control numbers presented as current; Object Lock in compliance mode with a retention period nobody asked for; evidence scripts that use admin credentials or call write APIs; questionnaire answers that describe controls not in the matrix; specific Vietnamese penalty amounts or deadlines stated as fact with no source; and retention settings that keep application logs with personal data "forever, to be safe".

0
No exposure. Thinks compliance is paperwork for another department and has never seen an audit request list.
1

2

Self-check before claiming 2: given a mentor's mock request list of ten items ("show CloudTrail retention", "show access to prod was reviewed last quarter", "show 5 sampled changes with approvals"), produce the evidence for each from the evidence bucket and the repos in under two hours, and say which framework clauses each item answers.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Screenshots as evidence

They show one moment, can't be searched and prove little. Scheduled JSON exports with checksums in an Object Lock bucket answer "what was true on that date" for any date.

✗ Discovering retention at audit time

A 90-day default on logs means a 12-month audit period has nine months missing, and nothing can recreate them. Set retention on day one of every new account.

✗ Engineers giving legal answers

"Is Singapore hosting allowed under the PDP Law?" is a legal question. Describe the facts of the platform precisely and let counsel answer.

✓ Keep the control matrix in the repo

A controls.yaml next to the Terraform it describes stays current, is reviewed in PRs, and turns every new questionnaire into a lookup.

✓ Shrink scope by design

Provider-hosted card checkout, health data in as few stores as possible, synthetic data in dev. Every system you keep out of scope is a system nobody has to audit.
