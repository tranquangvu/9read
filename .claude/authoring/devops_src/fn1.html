DevOps Culture, SDLC & DORA Metrics

This is the first module of the track, and it has no prerequisites. It explains what DevOps actually is (a way of working, not a tool or a job title), the road every change takes from a developer's laptop to a patient booking an appointment, and the four numbers that tell you whether that road is fast and safe. It ends with the ideas you will use every week as an engineer: finding where work waits, getting rid of repetitive manual work, and talking about failures without blaming people. Finally it shows how the 52 modules of this program map to the job levels from beginner to senior.

Why it matters in production: teams rarely fail because they picked the wrong tool. They fail because a change waits nine days for a manual approval, so changes get bundled into large risky releases, so releases break, so people add more approvals. Every module after this one teaches a tool (Terraform, GitHub Actions, Kubernetes). This one teaches you to tell whether the tool made delivery better, using numbers your manager and your client both understand.
Part I · What DevOps is

DevOps shortens the path from commit to customer, safely

DevOps is a set of practices and habits that lets the people who write software and the people who run it work as one team, so that changes reach users quickly and break things rarely. The word joins development (writing code) and operations (running servers, databases and networks, and getting woken up when they break). There is no DevOps product to buy. Tools matter, but only as the way a habit is enforced.

The problem DevOps was invented to fix

Before about 2009, most companies split the two jobs into separate departments with opposite goals. Developers were rewarded for shipping features, which means change. Operations was rewarded for uptime, which means avoiding change. Code was "thrown over the wall" to ops a few times a year, together with a deployment document nobody had tested. People call this the wall of confusion. Here is what it looks like for our running example, the clinic booking platform (a web app where patients book doctor appointments), before and after.

QuestionWall of confusionDevOps team
How often do we release?Once a month, on a Saturday nightSeveral times a day, during working hours
How big is a release?80 changes from 6 people at onceOne small pull request
Who deploys?An ops engineer follows a 40-step documentA pipeline, triggered by merging code
How are servers created?A ticket, then someone clicks in the AWS consoleA reviewed Terraform change (IA1)
When it breaksOps is paged; developers are asleep and out of reachThe owning team is paged and rolls back in minutes
After an outageFind who made the mistakeFind which part of the system allowed it (Part IV)

The counter-intuitive lesson of the right-hand column is that releasing more often is safer. A one-line change that breaks is easy to find and undo. An 80-change release that breaks takes hours to untangle, and the fix itself is another risky change.

CALMS: five lenses for judging a team

CALMS is a checklist for "is this team actually doing DevOps?". It stands for Culture, Automation, Lean, Measurement and Sharing. Use it when you join a client project. Ask one question per letter, and you will find the weakest area within a day. A team with excellent automation and a blame culture is still slow, because people stop admitting mistakes and every change gets extra sign-offs.

LetterMeansQuestion to askWarning sign
CultureShared ownership of the running system, safety to report problems"Who gets paged when booking-api fails at 2 am?""That's the ops team's problem"
AutomationBuilds, tests, infrastructure and deploys run without manual steps"Show me how a change reaches production"A wiki page called "Deploy steps"
LeanSmall batches, limit work in progress, remove waiting"How many open PRs are older than 3 days?"Long-lived feature branches
MeasurementDecisions based on delivery and reliability data"What was last month's change failure rate?"Nobody knows, or it's a guess

Write the answers down. A one-page CALMS assessment with one concrete improvement per letter is a useful first deliverable on any new client, and it is the starting point for the practice task at the end of this module.

The three ways: flow, feedback, learning

The Phoenix Project and The DevOps Handbook describe DevOps as three "ways". They are a useful way to classify any improvement you propose. Flow makes work move left to right faster (smaller changes, automated deploys). Feedback sends information right to left faster (tests in minutes, alerts in seconds). Continual learning turns every failure and experiment into a better system.

Practise labelling proposals. "Cache Docker layers in CI" is Flow (builds go from 14 to 5 minutes). "Alert on booking errors instead of CPU" is Feedback. "Run a quarterly database-failover drill" is Learning. If a proposal fits none of the three, ask what problem it really solves.

What a DevOps engineer actually does all week

Strictly, DevOps is a way of working for the whole team, not a job. In practice, companies (including ours) hire DevOps engineers: people who build and look after the road that changes travel on. You build the pipelines, write the infrastructure as code, run the container platform, set up monitoring and lead the response when production breaks. Similar titles you will see: Site Reliability Engineer (SRE, more focus on reliability targets, SR1) and Platform Engineer (more focus on self-service tools for developers, AR3).

Work typeTypical shareExample on the clinic project
Infrastructure changes~30%Add an SQS queue for booking-worker in clinic-infra
Pipelines and developer tooling~25%Cut the booking-web build from 12 to 4 minutes

"DevOps team" is a warning sign when it becomes a new wall. If developers file tickets to "the DevOps team" and wait, you have rebuilt the old ops department with a new name. Your goal is to make the safe path the easy path for developers, so they can ship without waiting for you.
Part II · SDLC and the deployment pipeline

Every change takes the same road, and you build the road

The software development life cycle (SDLC) is the list of stages a change goes through, from an idea to code running for users and back to the next idea. The deployment pipeline is the automated part of that cycle: the machine that turns a merged pull request into a running release. DevOps engineers spend most of their time on this machine, so you need to know every stage and what each one proves.

The SDLC as a loop, not a line

Older textbooks draw the SDLC as a straight line ending at "release" (the waterfall model). DevOps draws it as a loop, often shown as an infinity sign, because what you learn from operating the software decides what you build next. Each stage below has an owner and a tool on the clinic project.

The deployment pipeline for booking-api, end to end

Here is the real road for one change to booking-api, the clinic's backend service. Read it once now; every term in it gets its own module later. Notice two rules. First, the image is built once and the same image is promoted through every environment, so what you tested is what you ship. Second, every stage can stop the line: a failure anywhere means nothing reaches production.

In GitHub Actions that road is a YAML file in the repository. You will learn to write one properly in CI1 and CI2. For now, read it as a list of stages and find the two rules above in it.

clinic-app/.github/workflows/booking-api.yml — the skeleton 
on:
  push: { branches: [main], paths: ["services/booking-api/**"] }
permissions: { id-token: write, contents: read }   # OIDC to AWS: no stored keys
jobs:
  build:
    runs-on: ubuntu-24.04
    outputs: { digest: "${{ steps.push.outputs.digest }}" }
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: make test                                   # tests again on main, not only on the PR
      - id: push
        run: ./ci/build-and-push.sh booking-api          # builds ONCE, prints the image digest
  deploy-dev:
    needs: build
    uses: ./.github/workflows/deploy.yml
    with: { env: dev, digest: "${{ needs.build.outputs.digest }}" }
  deploy-staging:
    needs: deploy-dev
    uses: ./.github/workflows/deploy.yml
    with: { env: staging, digest: "${{ needs.build.outputs.digest }}" }
  deploy-prod:
    needs: deploy-staging
    uses: ./.github/workflows/deploy.yml                 # the prod environment requires 1 approval
    with: { env: prod, digest: "${{ needs.build.outputs.digest }}" }
Rebuilding per environment breaks the whole idea. If staging and prod each run docker build, they can pull different base images or library versions, and you ship something nobody tested. Pass the image digest (a fingerprint of the exact image) from stage to stage, as the workflow above does.

Environments: dev, staging and prod in separate AWS accounts

An environment is a complete copy of the platform where a version runs. We keep each one in its own AWS account, so a mistake in dev cannot delete anything in prod. Accounts are explained in AW1. What matters now is what each environment is for, because that decides what may happen there.

Infrastructure changes travel the same road

In a DevOps team the servers, networks and databases are also described in code (infrastructure as code, IaC) and kept in a Git repository, clinic-infra. A change to infrastructure is a pull request like any other. The pipeline runs terraform plan to show exactly what would change, a reviewer reads that plan, and the change is applied only after merge. The details are in IA1 and IA2; the habit to build now is reading the plan summary line before anything else. 

# comment posted by the pipeline on a clinic-infra pull request
Terraform will perform the following actions:
  # aws_sqs_queue.booking_reminders will be created
  + resource "aws_sqs_queue" "booking_reminders" {
      + name                       = "booking-reminders-prod"
      + visibility_timeout_seconds = 120
    }
Plan: 1 to add, 0 to change, 0 to destroy.
# the line to read first: "destroy" above 0 on a prod plan always gets a second reviewer
The console is not a shortcut. A queue someone created by clicking in the AWS console is invisible to review, is missing from the other environments, and is deleted or overwritten the next time Terraform runs. If you had to change something by hand during an incident, open the matching pull request the same day.
Part III · DORA metrics

Measure delivery with four numbers, and always read them together

DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment, now a research team at Google Cloud) has surveyed tens of thousands of engineers every year since 2014. Its central finding is that speed and stability are not a trade-off: the teams that deploy most often also break production least often and recover fastest. DORA measures this with four delivery metrics, often called the four keys. They are the shared language for "is our delivery getting better?" across this whole program.

The four keys, defined precisely

Two metrics measure throughput (how fast changes flow) and two measure stability (how often they hurt). Precise definitions matter. Teams that define "deployment" or "failure" loosely end up with numbers that look good and mean nothing.

MetricDefinitionHow we count it on the clinic project
Deployment frequencyHow often a change is deployed to productionSuccessful GitHub deployments to the prod environment, per working day
Change lead timeTime from a change being committed to it running in productionMedian of (prod deploy succeeded − first commit of the PR)
Change failure rateShare of production deployments that cause a failure needing immediate fixingProd deploys followed by a rollback, hotfix or incident within 24 h ÷ all prod deploys
Failed deployment recovery timeTime to restore service after a deployment causes a failureMedian of (incident resolved − incident started), deploy-caused incidents only

You may read "MTTR" (mean time to restore) in older material; DORA renamed it in 2023 to make clear it counts only failures caused by changes. Recent reports also track a fifth delivery metric, rework rate: the share of deployments that were unplanned and made only to fix a problem users saw. Use medians, not averages: one 9-hour outage hides the fact that every other recovery took 10 minutes.

What "good" looks like

The 2024 State of DevOps report grouped teams into four performance clusters. Treat these as a rough map, not a target to hit at any cost. The 2025 report moved away from clusters towards team profiles, but these ranges are still what clients and interviewers quote.

Cluster (share of teams)Deploy frequencyLead timeChange failure rateRecovery time
Elite (19%)On demand, several per dayUnder 1 day~5%Under 1 hour
High (22%)Daily to weekly1 day to 1 week~20%Under 1 day
Medium (35%)Weekly to monthly1 week to 1 month~10%Under 1 day
Low (25%)Monthly to every 6 months1 to 6 months~40%1 week to 1 month

The clinic platform's goal for its first year is to be "High" on every metric: at least one prod deploy per working day, lead time under 24 hours, change failure rate under 15%, recovery under one hour. Notice that the Medium cluster has a lower failure rate than High. That is why you never judge a team on one metric alone.

Computing the keys from data you already have

You do not need a product to start measuring. GitHub records a deployment object every time a workflow job targets an environment, so deployment frequency is one API call with the GitHub CLI (gh). Scripting with jq and Python is taught properly in FN5; here, just read what each line asks. 

# deployment frequency: production deployments in October 2026
gh api --paginate "repos/golden-owl/clinic-app/deployments?environment=prod&per_page=100" \
  --jq '.[] | select(.created_at >= "2026-10-01" and .created_at < "2026-11-01") | .sha' | wc -l
      46
# 46 deploys / 22 working days = 2.1 per day → "Elite" frequency
clinic-app/scripts/lead_time.py — median change lead time 
import json, statistics, subprocess
from datetime import datetime

REPO = "golden-owl/clinic-app"

def gh(path):
    out = subprocess.run(["gh", "api", path], capture_output=True, text=True, check=True)
    return json.loads(out.stdout)

def ts(s):
    return datetime.fromisoformat(s.replace("Z", "+00:00"))

hours = []
for d in gh(f"repos/{REPO}/deployments?environment=prod&per_page=50"):
    pulls = gh(f"repos/{REPO}/commits/{d['sha']}/pulls")
    if not pulls:
        continue                                  # direct push to main: count it separately
    commits = gh(f"repos/{REPO}/pulls/{pulls[0]['number']}/commits")
    first = ts(commits[0]["commit"]["author"]["date"])
    hours.append((ts(d["created_at"]) - first).total_seconds() / 3600)

print(f"deploys measured: {len(hours)}")
print(f"median lead time: {statistics.median(hours):.1f} h")   # median, never mean
print(f"slowest 10%:      {statistics.quantiles(hours, n=10)[-1]:.1f} h")
python3 scripts/lead_time.py

slowest 10%:      71.8 h
# the slow tail is where to look: which PRs waited 3 days, and for what? (Part IV)

Reliability, and how metrics get gamed

DORA adds a fifth measure next to the four keys: reliability, meaning whether the service meets the targets its users care about (for example "99.9% of booking requests succeed"). Fast, safe delivery is worth little if the platform is slow every Monday morning. Those targets are called SLOs and are the subject of SR1. Any number you report can be gamed, and the four keys are no exception. Here is how they get gamed and what stops it:

Gaming moveMetric it inflatesWhat catches it
Deploying an unchanged image to raise the countDeployment frequencyCount only deploys whose digest differs from the previous one
Not filing incidents for "small" breakagesChange failure rateCross-check rollbacks and hotfix PRs against incident records
Closing the incident before users can book againRecovery time"Resolved" means the SLI is back to normal, from the dashboard
Squashing commits so the first commit is recentLead timeAlso measure from PR opened, and watch PR age
Never use the four keys to rank people or compare teams. DORA's own guidance is that the metrics are for a team to improve itself. The day they decide bonuses, people optimise the number instead of the system (this is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure). Report them as a trend for one team over time, together, every month.
Part IV · Value streams, toil and culture

Find the waiting, automate the toil, fix the system instead of blaming people

The four keys tell you that delivery is slow or fragile. This Part gives you three tools for finding why and what to do about it: a value-stream map for slowness, a toil log for wasted engineering time, and blameless reviews for failures. It ends with how this program turns you into a senior engineer who uses all three without being asked.

Value-stream mapping: most lead time is waiting

A value stream is every step a change goes through from "someone starts work" to "a patient benefits", including the time it sits in queues. To map one, take a real recent change and write down when each step started and ended. You will nearly always find that people spent a few hours working and the change spent days waiting. Here is one real-looking booking-api change from before the clinic team adopted continuous deployment. 

gantt

The number to compute is flow efficiency: work time divided by total lead time. Here it is 5.5 ÷ 133.5 = 4%. Making the developer type faster cannot help much. Removing waits does:

Toil: the work that grows with the system and teaches you nothing

Google's SRE book defines toil as operational work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive, has no lasting value, and grows as the service grows. Restarting a stuck worker by hand every few days is toil. Designing the alert that tells you why it gets stuck is not. Google's rule of thumb is that toil should stay under 50% of an engineer's time; the rest goes to engineering work that removes future toil. You cannot reduce what you do not count, so keep a toil log.

clinic-infra/docs/toil-log.yaml — one entry per recurring manual task 
- task: Restart booking-worker when the SQS backlog stops draining
  how_often: 3 per week
  minutes_each: 15
  minutes_per_month: 195                 # 3 × 15 × ~4.3 weeks
  fix: Health check on queue age + auto-restart; then find the root cause
  fix_cost_hours: 6                      # pays back in under 2 months
- task: Create a database user for each new developer in staging
  how_often: 2 per month
  minutes_each: 25
  minutes_per_month: 50
  fix: Self-service via IAM database authentication (AW5)
  fix_cost_hours: 8
- task: Copy the release notes into the client's Slack channel
  how_often: 10 per week
  minutes_each: 5
  minutes_per_month: 215
  fix: Pipeline step posts the changelog automatically (CI2)
  fix_cost_hours: 2                      # best ratio: do this one first

Sort the log by minutes saved per hour of fixing, and work from the top. Not every repeated task is toil, and not all toil is worth removing:

TaskToil?Why

Blameless culture: people are not the root cause

When something breaks, the natural reaction is to ask who did it. A blameless team asks instead what about the system made the mistake easy to make and hard to catch. The reason is practical, not kind: if people are punished for mistakes, they hide them, and you lose the information you need to stop the next outage. Assume everyone acted reasonably given what they knew at the time. The full postmortem process is in SR2; the language habit starts now.

BlamefulBlamelessAction it leads to
"Lan deleted the staging database.""A cleanup script ran against staging because the profile name looked like dev."Distinct profile names, deletion protection on RDS, prompts that show the account
"Someone forgot to run the migration.""The deploy relied on a manual migration step that was not in the pipeline."Migrations run as a pipeline step (CI2)
"Human error.""Review did not catch it because the plan was 900 lines long."Split state so plans stay small (IA2)

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Buying tools to "do DevOps"

A new CI product does not fix a 59-hour wait for a release window. Map the value stream first; most fixes are agreements and small automations, not purchases.

✗ Adding approvals after every incident

Each sign-off feels safer, but it makes batches bigger and lead time longer, and bigger batches fail more. Add an automated check instead of a human gate.

✗ Reporting one metric in isolation

"We deploy 10 times a day" means little if a third of those deploys break production. Always show throughput and stability side by side.

✓ Measure before and after every improvement

Record median lead time before you speed up CI and again two weeks after. "Lead time 19.4 h → 6.1 h" is the sentence that gets your next improvement approved.

✓ Keep the toil log next to the code

A toil-log.yaml in clinic-infra turns "ops work never ends" into a ranked backlog with payback times that a manager can prioritise.
