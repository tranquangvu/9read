Part I · The asymmetry

Why speed is the FDE's weapon

Every consulting firm on earth can produce a deck. A deck describes a future; it asks the customer to imagine. Imagination is expensive, unevenly distributed, and easy to argue with — which is why slide reviews turn into opinion contests. A working prototype does something a deck structurally cannot: it removes the argument. The thing either extracts the fields from their real invoice or it doesn't. Twenty seconds of a running screen ends a debate that three meetings could not.

This is the asymmetry the FDE role is built on. You are not a better consultant than the Big Four partner in the room — you are a consultant who can build the thing during the engagement. Discovery (FD1) tells you what to build; rapid prototyping is how you convert that finding into belief, budget and momentum before anyone's attention moves on.

The "touch it" moment

There is a specific instant in a good demo where the room changes. It is not when you say "and as you can see, the model extracts the fields". It is when you slide the laptop across the table, or paste the share link in chat, and say: "upload one of yours". The skeptic — the person who got burned by the 2024 chatbot project and has been quiet for forty minutes — drags in a file they chose themselves, one you have never seen. It works, or it works mostly, and their face changes.

Design every prototype for that moment. Ask yourself while building: "what will they try first when I hand it over?" Then make that path work. A prototype that only survives your own scripted click sequence is a video, not a demo — and everyone in the room can tell the difference within about four seconds.

The compounding effect

Speed does not just win the meeting; it changes the quality of everything downstream. A demo is the highest-bandwidth discovery instrument you will ever own — better than any interview, because people cannot politely agree with software. Watch the loop:

  • Early demo → honest feedback. "That's not how we'd phrase it", "you're missing the sub-contractor case", "why is it asking me to log in twice?" — reactions you would never have extracted from a requirements workshop.
  • Honest feedback → better scope. Half of what you planned turns out to be unnecessary; one thing you never considered turns out to be mandatory. Finding that in week one instead of month three is worth more than any amount of upfront design.
  • Better scope → credible estimate. You now size the real build from a thing that exists (FD3) and a value model grounded in observed behaviour (FD4), not from a wish.
  • Credible estimate → trust → more access. The team that saw something real in four days gives you the production database credentials that were "impossible" in week one. Access follows demonstrated competence, not requests.
  • And discovery continues. Every demo is another FD1 probe: who leaned in, who went quiet, which feature nobody mentioned again. Note it all.

The honest cost of slow

Enterprises have a metabolic rate, and it is not yours. Sponsors move teams. Budgets get re-cut at quarter end. A reorg lands and the champion who invited you now reports to someone who has never heard of the project. A six-week PoC does not lose to a better competitor — it loses to a calendar. The half-life of executive attention on a new initiative is measured in weeks, and the clock starts at kickoff, not when you feel ready to show something.

Prototype ≠ product — and say so out loud

The speed only exists because you deliberately skip things. This is a professional choice, not a dirty secret, and the single most trust-building sentence in a demo is the one where you name what you skipped before anyone asks. The failure mode is not skipping — it is skipping silently, letting a stakeholder believe they are two weeks from production, and getting caught in month two.

Deliberately skipped in a 1–3 day PoC
  • Auth, SSO, roles & permissions
  • Multi-tenancy, audit logs, retention
  • Production error handling & retries at scale
  • Automated tests, CI/CD, IaC
  • Real integrations (mock the write-back)
  • Cost/latency optimisation, caching tiers
  • Systematic evals — a golden set of 10 is enough for now (AI9)
Never skipped, even in 1 day
  • Real or realistic customer data — the whole trick (Part III)
  • The actual hard step of the workflow (don't demo the easy 20%)
  • Honest output — never fake a result you cannot reproduce
  • Basic data hygiene: no customer PII on your laptop (PR5)
  • A visible confidence / uncertainty signal where the model can be wrong
  • Something the customer can click themselves
The sentence to memorise: "This is a three-day prototype. It runs on my laptop with a copy of forty of your documents, there's no login, and it only handles the standard case — I skipped all of that on purpose so we could look at the part that actually decides whether this is worth building." Nobody has ever reacted badly to that sentence. Silence about it, on the other hand, is how you end up demoing to a CIO who thinks you shipped.
Part II · The playbook

The 1-3 day prototype playbook

Speed is not typing fast. Almost all of the time saved in a rapid prototype is saved by decisions made before the first line of code: what you build, what you refuse to build, what you reuse, and what data you build it on. Engineers who "can't prototype in three days" are almost never slow coders — they are scoping a product.

Rule 1 — Scope discipline: ONE workflow, ONE happy path

Take the single use case your FD1 problem statement selected, and cut it further. Not "invoice processing" — "take one supplier PDF invoice, extract eight fields, show them next to the matching PO, and let the operator accept or correct". One entry point. One user role. One document type. One outcome on the screen.

TemptationWhat it costs youDo this instead
"Let's support all three document types"3× the prompt/eval work, 0× the persuasionOne type; say "the others are the same pattern"
"It needs login before I show anyone"Half a day, plus a password to forget on stageNo auth; run it on a private URL or share your screen

The scope test: write the demo script first (Part IV) — the literal ten sentences you will say. Anything you cannot point at while saying one of those sentences does not get built. This one habit is the difference between a three-day prototype and a three-week one.

There is no "best" prototyping stack; there is the stack that reaches a demoable state fastest for this audience. Backend logic is Python-first — it is where the AI ecosystem lives and where you will be fastest. The UI layer is the variable.

Rule 3 — Never start from an empty directory

The single biggest lever on prototype speed is a pre-built kit you keep and grow. An FDE two years into the job starts a prototype at roughly hour six of the average engineer's day, because six components already exist and already work. Lab 1 builds yours.

1 · Auth-free app skeleton

Runs with one command, has a title, a sidebar for settings, and a place to drop a customer logo.

2 · LLM wrapper

One function: retries, timeout, token/cost log, structured-output helper, on-disk response cache. Straight from AI2.

3 · File ingest

Drag-and-drop for PDF / DOCX / CSV / images → text, with page markers. Ninety percent of PoCs start with a file.

4 · Chat + streaming view

Message history, streaming tokens, a "show the prompt" expander that makes technical audiences trust you.

5 · Results table + diff view

Editable dataframe, confidence column with colour, and side-by-side "AI said / human corrected" — the shot that sells human-in-the-loop.

6 · One-command deploy

A script that produces a URL you can paste in chat. A shareable link converts 3× better than "let me screen-share again".

Grow it after every engagement. Budget ninety minutes in the retro to pull back into the kit whatever you wrote twice: an Outlook .msg parser, a Vietnamese-invoice date normaliser, a confidence-threshold slider component. The kit is your compounding asset — protect it from client-specific code, and keep it dependency-light so it still runs in eighteen months.

Rule 4 — Let the AI write the boring 70%

Prototyping is exactly the regime where AI-assisted development pays maximum dividends (FN7): throwaway code, well-trodden libraries, low correctness stakes, and a human reviewing every screen anyway. Use it hard for the scaffolding — parsers, Streamlit layouts, dataframe wrangling, sample-data generators, the deploy config. Keep your own hands on the two things that decide the demo: the prompt and what appears on the screen.

The Day-1 kill switch. If after three focused hours of prompt iteration on real samples the output is still not close, stop building. You have discovered something valuable — the task is harder than the use case assumed, or the data is worse than described. Bring that to the customer on day one as a finding with evidence ("here are 10 real cases and what the model produced"). That conversation preserves the engagement. Quietly grinding for two more days and demoing something weak does not.
Part III · Data

Realistic data is the whole trick

If you remember one thing from this module: the data in your demo matters more than the code in your demo. Two prototypes with identical logic — one running on Acme Corp / Invoice #1234 / Widget A, one running on the customer's actual supplier names, their actual weird PO format, their actual truncated Vietnamese address field — are not comparable artifacts. The first is a toy. The second is evidence.

Why fake data destroys a demo

It happens in one second and it is irreversible. The domain expert scans your screen, sees "Invoice #1234, Acme Corp, $1,000.00", and a switch flips: this person has not seen our real documents. Everything after that is filtered through the assumption that you are showing a best case constructed to work. Worse, they stop volunteering the hard cases — because in their mind you're not ready for them yet.

Real data does the opposite, and it does something better than persuade: it informs. Real data is where you discover that 30% of their invoices are scans of printouts, that the PO number sometimes lives in the email subject and not the document, that three suppliers use a comma as a decimal separator. Every one of those is a finding you would otherwise have hit in month two of the build.

The demo-day proof move: keep 3–5 samples in reserve that you have never run. Open them live. If they work, the room is convinced beyond argument. If one fails, you get to do the single most credibility-building thing available to an FDE: explain calmly why it failed and what the fix costs. Either outcome is a win. A demo with zero unseen inputs is a rehearsed performance and experienced buyers know it.

Getting real-ish data fast — the ladder

Work down this ladder and stop at the first rung you can actually reach today. Do not wait two weeks for rung 1 when rung 3 is available this afternoon.

Synthetic-from-real-shape, done properly

Rung 3 is the FDE's workhorse and it is a craft. Bad synthetic data is clean, uniform and cheerful — which makes your prototype look great and then collapse on contact with reality. Good synthetic data is generated from an explicit list of the messiness you observed. Show the model two real (redacted) examples, tell it what the ugly variants are, and ask for volume: 

# make_samples.py — generate realistic demo data from 2 redacted real examples
# Usage: uv run make_samples.py 40   →  writes ./data/synthetic/*.txt
import os, sys, json, pathlib, concurrent.futures as cf
from anthropic import Anthropic

client = Anthropic(api_key=os.environ["ANTHROPIC_API_KEY"])
MODEL  = "claude-sonnet-5"
OUT    = pathlib.Path("data/synthetic"); OUT.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)

REAL_1 = pathlib.Path("data/real/redacted_01.txt").read_text()
REAL_2 = pathlib.Path("data/real/redacted_02.txt").read_text()

# ── The messiness list. Write this from what you SAW in discovery.
#    This list is the difference between useful and useless synthetic data.
QUIRKS = """
- ~30% are forwarded email chains: 2-3 levels of "> " quoting, signatures,
  and a mobile-client footer ("Sent from my iPhone").
- Dates appear as 04/08/2026, 2026-08-04, and "4 Aug 26" — inconsistently,
  sometimes two formats in one document.
- Amounts: some use 1.234,56 (comma decimal), some 1,234.56.
- ~15% have the PO number ONLY in the subject line, not in the body.
- Vietnamese addresses are often truncated mid-word and lose diacritics.
- ~10% say "as per our call" and omit a mandatory field entirely.
- Supplier names vary: "CT TNHH ABC", "ABC Co Ltd", "ABC CO.,LTD".
"""

PROMPT = f"""Here are two REAL (redacted) examples of the documents this
customer processes:

<example_1>
{REAL_1}
</example_1>

<example_2>
{REAL_2}
</example_2>

Known messiness in the real corpus:
{QUIRKS}

Generate ONE new synthetic document of the same type. Requirements:
- Invent all names, numbers and addresses (nothing from the examples).
- Match the real structure, tone and layout — including the ugly parts.
- Randomly apply 1-3 of the quirks above so the corpus is varied.
- Output the document text ONLY, no commentary, no markdown fences."""

def one(i: int):
    r = client.messages.create(
        model=MODEL, max_tokens=1500, temperature=1.0,   # variety matters here
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT}],
    )
    (OUT / f"sample_{i:03d}.txt").write_text(r.content[0].text.strip())
    return i

if __name__ == "__main__":
    n = int(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 40
    with cf.ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=8) as ex:
        list(ex.map(one, range(n)))
    print(f"wrote {n} samples to {OUT}")

# wrote 40 samples to data/synthetic
# ~35s, ~$0.30 — cheaper and better than three days of waiting for a data extract
Two disciplines. (1) Read ten of the generated samples yourself before you build on them — if they all feel the same, your QUIRKS list was too thin and your prototype will overfit to tidy input. (2) Label them. Watermark synthetic files (a [SYNTHETIC SAMPLE] header, or a badge in the UI) and say so in the demo. Being caught presenting generated data as customer data costs more than the demo is worth.

The "we can't give you data yet" blocker

This is the most common reason prototypes slip from three days to three weeks, and it is almost always a process problem (legal review, DPA, a security questionnaire), not a refusal. Do not sit and wait. Run two tracks in parallel:

  • Track A — unblock in the background. Ask the precise question: "what is the smallest thing that could be shared today — 10 documents with names blacked out, under the existing NDA?" Offer to work on their machine, or in their tenant, or to accept a redacted subset. Name the approver and the date (PR5, PR13).
  • Track B — build on a public analogue behind an adapter. Find the nearest public corpus (public tenders, SEC/annual-report PDFs, open support-ticket datasets, their own published brochures) and build against it — but put every data access behind one swappable interface so the real corpus drops in without touching the app.
# data_source.py — the swap-in adapter. Costs 15 minutes, saves the engagement.
from typing import Protocol, Iterator
from dataclasses import dataclass
import pathlib

@dataclass
class Doc:
    id: str
    text: str
    source: str          # "public-analogue" | "synthetic" | "customer"
    meta: dict

class DataSource(Protocol):
    def docs(self) -> Iterator[Doc]: ...

class LocalFolder:
    """Works for all three phases: analogue → synthetic → real customer export."""
    def __init__(self, folder: str, source: str):
        self.folder, self.source = pathlib.Path(folder), source
    def docs(self) -> Iterator[Doc]:
        for p in sorted(self.folder.glob("*.txt")):
            yield Doc(id=p.stem, text=p.read_text(), source=self.source, meta={})

class CustomerSharePoint:
    """Written on day 1, unused until access lands. Then it is a one-line switch."""
    def docs(self) -> Iterator[Doc]:
        raise NotImplementedError("pending IT approval — ticket SEC-4471, ETA 08/18")

# app.py imports exactly one line — and the demo never mentions the plumbing:
SOURCE = LocalFolder("data/public_analogue", "public-analogue")
# SOURCE = LocalFolder("data/customer_export", "customer")   ← day the export lands

Then say it out loud in the demo: "the documents on screen are public filings with the same structure as yours, because the data request is still with security — ticket SEC-4471. The moment that lands, it's a one-line change and we re-run this same demo on your corpus." Demo data hygiene

Moving fast with customer data is exactly how FDEs create incidents. The rules are short, and none of them cost you meaningful time (full treatment in PR5):

  • Ask before you copy. Written confirmation from a named person that these files may leave their environment, and where they may go. One line in email is enough — but get the line.
  • Minimum viable corpus. Forty documents, not the 40,000-row export. Less data is faster, cheaper, and dramatically less risk.
  • Redact at ingest, not later. A ten-line scrubber for names, emails, phone numbers and IDs run on import beats good intentions. Keep the shape, kill the identity.
  • Encrypted disk, project folder, .gitignore the data dir. Never commit customer files. Never paste customer data into a personal AI account.
  • Check the vendor terms. Know whether the API you're calling trains on inputs; be able to answer that question in the room, because someone will ask it.
  • Delete on a date. Put a calendar reminder for engagement end +30 days, delete, and tell the customer you did. Nobody expects it; everybody remembers it.
Part IV · Demo craft

Running the room

A prototype is not a deliverable — the demo is the deliverable, and the prototype is a prop inside it. Engineers routinely build something genuinely impressive and then lose the room by opening with a five-minute architecture explanation. Ten minutes of structure, rehearsed twice, converts more than three extra features.

The 10-minute demo shape

00:00–00:45 — Context, in their words. "Last week we sat with Linh's team. Five people, about 140 booking emails a day, roughly ninety minutes each of re-keying." No agenda slide, no company intro, no architecture.
00:45–01:15 — The pain quote. Verbatim, from FD1: "In peak season I stay late every day just doing entry." Attribute it. The person who said it is often in the room, and they sit up.
01:15–01:45 — Frame what this is. The prototype-≠-product sentence. Thirty seconds. Buys you the entire rest of the demo.
01:45–05:00 — The live run. Their document. Their fields. Silence while it runs — do not narrate the spinner. Then the result, then the human-in-the-loop step: correct something on purpose, so they see the workflow, not the magic.
05:00–06:30 — Hand over the keyboard. "Try one of yours." This is the whole meeting. Shut up while they do it.
06:30–08:00 — Show a limit on purpose. The case you know fails, plus the honest reading: "handwritten annotations drop us to ~60% — in production those route to manual review, which is what happens today anyway."
08:00–09:00 — The "I don't know" moment. Someone asks something you can't answer. Say "I don't know — I'll find out by Thursday", write it down visibly, and move on. Every fabricated answer you avoid here is worth a month of credibility.
09:00–10:00 — What's next, as a choice. Two or three concrete options with rough shapes ("two weeks to harden this for five users" / "six weeks for the integrated version"). Then one explicit ask, and stop talking.
Never demo alone if you can avoid it. One person drives, one person watches faces and writes. The observer captures three times more than the driver, because driving consumes all available attention. If you must be solo: record the session (with permission) and re-watch it the same day.

Rehearsal and the failure plan

Assume the demo will break, because sometimes it does: the venue wifi is captive-portalled, the API has a rate-limit blip, a 40MB scan takes ninety seconds, the projector is 4:3. None of these are unlucky — they are the base rate. Professionals plan for them, and the plan takes twenty minutes.

🎥 Recorded fallback

A 3-minute screen recording of the full happy path, downloaded locally (not in the cloud). If the live run dies, you switch to it in ten seconds and keep the meeting.

🌱 Seeded happy path

A pre-loaded example that is one click away, with its result already cached. You never start a demo from a cold, empty screen.

📴 Offline mode

An env flag that serves cached responses from disk, so the app runs with zero network. Also: phone hotspot tested before the room, not during.

⏱️ Latency cover

Stream output rather than blocking; if a step takes >5s, have a sentence prepared to fill it — ideally the honest one: "this takes about eight seconds today; production would batch it overnight."

# cache.py — 20 lines that give you offline mode, fast reruns and a lower bill.
# Set FDE_OFFLINE=1 before the demo and the app never touches the network.
import os, json, hashlib, pathlib
from anthropic import Anthropic

CACHE = pathlib.Path(".cache"); CACHE.mkdir(exist_ok=True)
OFFLINE = os.environ.get("FDE_OFFLINE") == "1"
client = Anthropic(api_key=os.environ.get("ANTHROPIC_API_KEY", ""))

def complete(prompt: str, model: str = "claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens: int = 2000) -> str:
    key = hashlib.sha256(f"{model}|{max_tokens}|{prompt}".encode()).hexdigest()[:24]
    hit = CACHE / f"{key}.json"
    if hit.exists():
        return json.loads(hit.read_text())["text"]
    if OFFLINE:
        raise RuntimeError(
            "OFFLINE and not cached — warm this input before the demo: " + prompt[:60])
    r = client.messages.create(model=model, max_tokens=max_tokens,
                               messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}])
    text = r.content[0].text
    hit.write_text(json.dumps({"text": text,
                              "in": r.usage.input_tokens,
                              "out": r.usage.output_tokens}))
    return text

# Pre-demo warm-up: run every seeded sample once so the cache is hot.
#   $ python -c "import warm; warm.all()"   →  then FDE_OFFLINE=1 streamlit run app.py
# warmed 12/12 seeded inputs · cache 12 files · demo will run with wifi off

Showing limits on purpose

Junior instinct: hide the weaknesses, hope nobody probes. It never survives — the domain expert in the room knows exactly which case is hard, and if you don't raise it they conclude you either didn't find it or you're hiding it. Both readings are worse than the truth. Volunteering the limit does three things at once: it proves you tested on real variety, it makes every claim you did make more believable, and it moves the conversation to "what would it take to handle that?" — which is a scoping conversation, i.e. the one you want. This is the same expectation-management muscle as FD6.

✗ What kills trust✓ What builds it
"It handles everything" (it doesn't)"On the 50 documents we tested, 44 were clean. Here are the 6 that weren't, and what they have in common."
Quietly skipping the failing sampleRunning it deliberately: "this is the type it struggles with — let me show you"
"Accuracy is about 95%" (measured on nothing)"We haven't measured accuracy properly yet — that's the first thing the next phase does, with a labelled set from your team (AI9)."
"We can add that easily" (reflex answer)"That's a real change — probably a week. Let me put it on the list and size it properly."
Answering a security question with a guess"I don't know your policy on that. Who owns it? Let's get them in the next call."

The 24-hour follow-up that converts

The trap: "it already works, just let ten people use it for a month." Prototypes put into production are how AI projects acquire a reputation for being unreliable — the model was never the problem, the missing 80% was. If you genuinely must run a limited pilot on prototype code, make it explicit and bounded: named users, a fixed end date, a known owner, a written "this is unsupported pilot software" line, and no customer-facing exposure.

What you throw away, what you keep

Throw away without sentiment
  • The Streamlit UI (it did its job perfectly; it is not a product)
  • Ad-hoc parsing glue and one-off scripts
  • Hardcoded paths, notebook state, that 300-line file called stuff.py
  • Any dependency you added at 11pm to make one thing render
Keep — this is the real output
  • The prompts and everything you learned tuning them
  • The sample corpus + the failure cases — seed of the golden set
  • The workflow design you validated (where the human sits, what the threshold is)
  • The measured shape: latency, tokens, cost per document
  • The gap list and the feedback log — they are the plan

Judged this way, a prototype is not "code you'll rewrite" — it is a machine for producing five expensive-to-obtain assets in three days. The code being disposable is a feature, and knowing that from the start is what lets you build it fast enough to matter.

The "prototype debt" conversation

Have it once, explicitly, in the follow-up meeting — with the sponsor present, not just the champion:

"What you saw is roughly 20% of a production system — and deliberately the most uncertain 20%, because that's the part that decides whether this is worth building at all. That question is now answered: it works on your documents. What isn't built is the boring 80%: login, permissions, real accuracy measurement, the TMS integration, monitoring, and someone to call when it breaks at 6pm. Here's that list with sizes. If we ship the prototype as-is to ten people, it will be a bad experience and it will be my fault — so let's decide which of these we actually need for a first real release, and which can wait."

Delivered in that order — value proven, gaps named, decision offered — this conversation almost never goes badly. Skipped, it becomes the month-three argument about why "the thing that already worked" is taking so long.

Sizing the real build from the prototype

You now have something almost no estimator ever has: measurements. Use them rather than intuition — this is the input to FD3 (solution architecture) and FD4 (ROI & business case).

  • Volume × measured cost. "$0.011 per document × 140/day ≈ $46/month in model spend" — a number you measured, not guessed. Kills the "AI is too expensive" objection instantly.
  • Measured latency vs the workflow. 8s per document is fine in a review queue, fatal in a live phone call. You now know which one you're in.
  • The failure rate on real data. 6/50 misses, all one identifiable category → the exception-handling design writes itself, and it's a scope line, not a surprise.
  • The feedback log as scope. Requested features, ranked by how many different people asked, is a better backlog than any workshop produces.
  • Time-per-item saved, observed. You watched an operator use it: 90 seconds manual became 20 seconds reviewing. That single observation is the entire ROI model's core input — capture it with a stopwatch during the demo, not from a survey afterwards.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Building the platform, not the demo

Auth, database, config system, admin panel — all before anything is visible. Every one of these is invisible in a demo and costs a day. If it isn't in your ten demo sentences, it isn't in the prototype.

✗ Fake data

"Acme Corp / Invoice #1234" tells the domain expert you've never seen their world. Credibility drops in one second and does not come back in that meeting. Go get twenty real files first — it's usually one message.

✗ Demoing without rehearsing

You know the app, so you improvise — and spend three minutes in the settings sidebar explaining an option nobody asked about. Two rehearsals, one recorded, always. It is the cheapest quality gain available.

✗ Letting the prototype become production

"Just let ten users try it for a month" with no owner, no evals and no auth. This is how AI gets a reputation for being unreliable. Answer with the written gap list, every time.

✗ Hiding the failure cases

The expert in the room already knows which case is hard. Not raising it reads as either sloppiness or spin. Show it yourself, with numbers, and you own the narrative instead of defending it.

✓ Hand over the keyboard

The single highest-value 90 seconds of any FDE demo. Design the prototype so a stranger's first instinct works, then be quiet while they try it. Nothing you say converts like that.

✓ Keep and grow the kit

Ninety minutes after every engagement pulling reusable pieces back into your starter. Within a year you begin prototypes six hours ahead of everyone else — permanently.

✓ Follow up within 24 hours

Recording, their quotes, honest unknowns, two sized options, one ask. A demo without a same-day follow-up decays into a pleasant memory; with one, it becomes a decision.

The LLM wrapper — llm.py. Retries, a disk cache (which gives you offline mode for free), a cost log, and a structured-output helper. This one file is the heart of the kit. 
# llm.py
import os, json, time, hashlib, pathlib
from anthropic import Anthropic, APIStatusError

CACHE   = pathlib.Path(".cache"); CACHE.mkdir(exist_ok=True)
OFFLINE = os.environ.get("FDE_OFFLINE") == "1"
BIG     = "claude-sonnet-5"                   # quality / reasoning
FAST    = "claude-haiku-4-5-20251001"        # cheap bulk passes
_client = Anthropic(api_key=os.environ.get("ANTHROPIC_API_KEY", ""))
_spend  = {"in": 0, "out": 0, "calls": 0}

def ask(prompt: str, *, system: str = "", model: str = BIG,
        max_tokens: int = 2000, temperature: float = 0.0,
        prefill: str = "") -> str:
    """One call to rule them all: cached, retried, cost-logged."""
    key  = hashlib.sha256(
        json.dumps([model, system, prompt, prefill, max_tokens, temperature]).encode()
    ).hexdigest()[:24]
    hit = CACHE / f"{key}.json"
    if hit.exists():
        return json.loads(hit.read_text())["text"]
    if OFFLINE:
        raise RuntimeError("FDE_OFFLINE=1 and no cache entry — warm it first")

    msgs = [{"role": "user", "content": prompt}]
    if prefill:                       # forces the shape of the answer
        msgs.append({"role": "assistant", "content": prefill})

    for attempt in range(4):
        try:
            r = _client.messages.create(
                model=model, max_tokens=max_tokens, temperature=temperature,
                system=system, messages=msgs)
            break
        except APIStatusError as e:
            if e.status_code < 500 and e.status_code != 429: raise
            time.sleep(2 ** attempt)          # 1s, 2s, 4s, 8s
    else:
        raise RuntimeError("LLM unavailable after 4 attempts")

    text = prefill + r.content[0].text
    _spend["in"]  += r.usage.input_tokens
    _spend["out"] += r.usage.output_tokens
    _spend["calls"] += 1
    hit.write_text(json.dumps({"text": text}))
    return text

def ask_json(prompt: str, **kw) -> dict:
    """Structured output without a parser library: prefill the opening brace."""
    raw = ask(prompt + "\n\nRespond with JSON only.", prefill="{", **kw)
    return json.loads(raw[raw.index("{"): raw.rindex("}") + 1])

def spend() -> str:
    # rough Sonnet-class pricing; keep it honest, not exact
    usd = _spend["in"] / 1e6 * 3.0 + _spend["out"] / 1e6 * 15.0
    return f"{_spend['calls']} calls · {_spend['in']}in/{_spend['out']}out · ~${usd:.3f}"

# >>> ask_json("Extract name and total from: INV-9931 ACME 1.234,56 EUR")
# {'name': 'ACME', 'total': 1234.56, 'currency': 'EUR'}
The point of this file: retries, cache, offline mode and cost accounting live in exactly one place, so every prototype you ever build inherits them for free.

File ingest — ingest.py. Ninety percent of PoCs begin with someone dragging a file in. Keep page markers: they let you cite the source in the UI, which is worth a surprising amount of trust. 
# ingest.py
import io, csv, pathlib
from pypdf import PdfReader
import docx

def to_text(name: str, blob: bytes) -> str:
    ext = pathlib.Path(name).suffix.lower()
    if ext == ".pdf":
        pages = PdfReader(io.BytesIO(blob)).pages
        return "\n".join(f"[page {i+1}]\n{p.extract_text() or ''}"
                         for i, p in enumerate(pages))
    if ext == ".docx":
        return "\n".join(p.text for p in docx.Document(io.BytesIO(blob)).paragraphs)
    if ext in (".csv", ".txt", ".md", ".eml"):
        return blob.decode("utf-8", errors="replace")
    raise ValueError(f"unsupported file type: {ext}")

def scrub(text: str) -> str:
    """Minimal PII scrubber — run at ingest, keep the shape, kill the identity."""
    import re
    text = re.sub(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+", "[EMAIL]", text)
    text = re.sub(r"\+?\d[\d\s().-]{7,}\d",  "[PHONE]", text)
    return text

The app skeleton — app.py. Upload → run → editable results table with a confidence column, plus a "show the prompt" expander that technical audiences love and a spend readout that makes cost questions answerable on the spot. 
# app.py  —  streamlit run app.py
import streamlit as st, pandas as pd
from llm import ask_json, spend
from ingest import to_text, scrub

st.set_page_config(page_title="Document extraction — prototype", layout="wide")

with st.sidebar:
    st.image("assets/customer_logo.png", width=130)     # 2 min, big effect
    st.caption("Prototype · not production · no auth")  # honesty, permanently on screen
    FIELDS = st.text_area("Fields to extract",
        "booking_ref, customer, origin, destination, pickup_date, container_type, weight_kg, incoterm")
    threshold = st.slider("Flag below confidence", 0.0, 1.0, 0.80, 0.05)

st.title("Booking email → structured fields")
files = st.file_uploader("Drop booking emails or PDFs",
                         accept_multiple_files=True,
                         type=["pdf", "txt", "eml", "docx"])

PROMPT = """Extract these fields from the document below: {fields}

Return JSON: {{"fields": {{"<name>": {{"value": ..., "confidence": 0-1,
"evidence": "<the exact quoted text you took it from>"}}}}}}
Use null for anything genuinely absent. Never guess a value that is not
present — a null is correct, an invention is not.

<document>
{doc}
</document>"""

if files and st.button("Extract", type="primary"):
    rows = []
    bar  = st.progress(0.0)
    for i, f in enumerate(files):
        doc = scrub(to_text(f.name, f.read()))[:20000]
        out = ask_json(PROMPT.format(fields=FIELDS, doc=doc))
        row = {"file": f.name}
        for k, v in out["fields"].items():
            row[k] = v.get("value")
            row[f"conf_{k}"] = v.get("confidence")
        rows.append(row)
        bar.progress((i + 1) / len(files))
    st.session_state.df = pd.DataFrame(rows)

if "df" in st.session_state:
    df = st.session_state.df
    conf_cols = [c for c in df.columns if c.startswith("conf_")]
    low = (df[conf_cols] < threshold).any(axis=1).sum()
    c1, c2, c3 = st.columns(3)
    c1.metric("Documents", len(df))
    c2.metric("Need review", int(low))
    c3.metric("Model spend", spend().split("~")[-1])
    st.caption("Edit any cell — this is the human-in-the-loop step.")
    st.data_editor(df.drop(columns=conf_cols), use_container_width=True)
    with st.expander("Show the prompt"):
        st.code(PROMPT.format(fields=FIELDS, doc="<document text>"))
    st.download_button("Download CSV", df.to_csv(index=False), "extracted.csv")

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • json.decoder.JSONDecodeError — the model wrapped the JSON in prose or fences. The prefill="{" trick plus the index/rindex slice handles nearly all of it; if it persists, switch that call to proper tool-use/structured output.
  • PDF text comes back empty — it is a scan, not a text PDF. Send the page as an image to a vision-capable model instead of pypdf. Discovering this on day 1 rather than demo day is exactly why you build on real files.
  • Streamlit reruns the whole script on every widget click — that is by design. Keep results in st.session_state (as above) and never do expensive work outside a button handler.
  • Extraction is slow on 50 files — parallelise with a ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=8), and use the FAST model for the bulk pass. Warm the cache before the demo regardless.
  • Costs look scary in the log — you are probably re-sending whole documents on every rerun. The cache fixes it; check that identical inputs hit the cache.

Stop at H8 even if something is unfinished — stopping on time is the lab. An unfinished item is a data point about your scoping, and it belongs in the retro, not in an extra three hours.
8
Retro (20 min, same day). Answer in writing: Where did the hours actually go versus your plan? What was the single biggest time sink? What would have saved an hour if it had already been in the kit? Add that thing to the kit now, while it stings.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "I'm at H+5 and there's no UI yet." The prompt loop ate the day. Ship the notebook with a clean results table and demo that — an honest notebook beats a half-built app. Note the cause in the retro.
  • "The data is much messier than I thought." Excellent — that is the finding. Narrow the scope to the subset that works, and put the messy category on the gap list with a number attached ("38% of samples, needs OCR").
  • "I keep polishing." Set a 25-minute timer per demo sentence. When it fires, move on regardless. Polish is the most common way a 1-day prototype becomes a 5-day one.
  • "It's hour 9 and I'm nearly done." Stop anyway and log it. The habit you are training is the timebox; finishing late trains the opposite one.

✅ Done when

Eight hours logged hour-by-hour, a demo that runs on real or realistic data, corpus-level numbers, a written cut list of at least five items, a gap list, and one concrete improvement committed to your kit. Repeat this lab on a different use case before you claim score 3 — the second one is always dramatically faster, and that delta is the skill becoming real.
Lab 3 · ~1.5 h
Demo rehearsal

🎯 Objective

Turn a working prototype into a delivered demo. Most engineers have never watched themselves demo, and the first viewing is uncomfortable and enormously valuable — nearly everyone finds the same three problems: they open with architecture, they narrate the spinner, and they never hand over the keyboard.

📦 Context

Use the Lab 2 prototype. You need: the app running, 3 seeded examples, 3 unseen samples in reserve, and any screen recorder (QuickTime, OBS, Loom, or your meeting tool's record button).

🔬 Steps

1
Write the script (20 min). Literal sentences, with timings, in the Part IV shape. Not bullet points — the actual words, because the words you improvise under mild stress are worse than the words you wrote calmly. 
# demo-script.md — 10 minutes — audience: Linh (ops lead), Minh (IT), Trang (senior operator)

00:00 "Last week we sat with your team for two days. Five people, about
       140 booking emails a day, roughly 90 minutes each of re-keying."
00:45 "Trang said something that stuck with me: 'in peak season I stay
       late every day just doing entry.'"                    [look at Trang]
01:15 "What I'm about to show is a two-day prototype. No login, running on
       my laptop, 31 of your real emails, standard case only. I skipped
       all of that on purpose so we could look at the part that decides
       whether this is worth building."
01:45 [SEEDED EXAMPLE 1 — click, then SILENCE while it runs ~6s]
02:30 "Eight fields, each with the exact text it came from. This one it's
       92% sure about; this one it flagged — watch what happens." [correct it]
05:00 "Trang — would you try one of yours?"        [SLIDE LAPTOP. STOP TALKING.]
06:30 "Here's one it gets wrong." [forwarded chain] "4 of our 31 failed, all
       the same category: the reference is in the subject, not the body."
08:00 [expect questions — 'I don't know, I'll find out by Thursday' is fine]
09:00 "Two options: two weeks standalone for five operators, or six weeks
       integrated into the TMS. Which one do you want me to size properly?"
09:45 [STOP TALKING. The silence after the ask is theirs, not yours.]
2
Record take 1 (15 min). Screen + your voice, standing up if you'll be standing. Full ten minutes, no restarts — restarting hides exactly the problems you're hunting.
3
Watch it back with a scorecard (20 min). This is the part people skip and the part that works. Score honestly: 
1. Time to first visible result:            ____ (target < 2:30)
2. Seconds of architecture talk:            ____ (target 0 before the run)
3. Did I narrate the spinner?               Y / N   (target N)
4. Did I hand over the keyboard?  At what time? ____ (target < 6:00)
5. Longest uninterrupted monologue:         ____ (target < 60s)
6. Did I state what was skipped, unprompted? Y / N  (target Y, before 02:00)
7. Did I show a failure on purpose?         Y / N   (target Y)
8. Filler words / "basically" / "so yeah":  ____ (just count; awareness fixes it)
9. Did I end with ONE clear ask?            Y / N
10. Watching as the customer: what would I ask that I couldn't answer? ____
4
Fix the three worst moments and re-record just those (15 min). Not the whole demo — the three specific moments. Usually: a rambling opening, a dead 8-second silence you filled with apology, and the transition into the ask.
5
Build the failure plan (15 min). Four artifacts, actually produced: 
[ ] fallback.mp4        3-min happy-path recording, DOWNLOADED locally
[ ] seeded examples     3 samples, one click, cache warm
[ ] offline mode        FDE_OFFLINE=1 runs the full script with wifi OFF
[ ] latency cover       one prepared sentence for any wait > 5s

$ python warm.py                 # warmed 12/12 seeded inputs
$ FDE_OFFLINE=1 streamlit run app.py
6
Break it on purpose (15 min). The real test. Run the demo while sabotaging it, one failure at a time, and time your recovery. Target: back on track in under 20 seconds, without apologising twice. 
# 1. kill the network mid-demo
$ sudo ifconfig en0 down          # macOS · turn wifi off physically for realism
→ does the app still run from cache? did you notice before the audience did?

# 2. break the API key
$ ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=bad streamlit run app.py
→ do you get a readable error, or a red Streamlit traceback on the projector?

# 3. feed it garbage — because someone WILL upload the wrong file
$ cp ~/Downloads/holiday_photo.jpg data/  # then upload it live
→ target: "That's not a booking email — here's what it says instead."
→ failure: an unhandled exception with a stack trace on screen

# 4. the 40MB scan
$ ls -lh data/big_scan.pdf        # 38M
→ time it. If > 15s, exclude it from the demo path or pre-cache it.
Add a top-level try/except around the run button that shows a friendly message rather than a traceback. Ten lines; saves the room.
7
Optional but effective: the hostile dry run. Give a colleague this brief — "You're the IT security lead who was burned by a vendor in 2024. Interrupt twice, ask where the data goes, ask what happens when it's wrong, and ask for something that obviously isn't built." Ten minutes of that is worth an hour of solo practice.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "I hate watching myself." Everyone does. Watch at 1.5× with the scorecard in front of you — it turns the experience into a measurement instead of a performance review.
  • "The demo runs 18 minutes, not 10." You are explaining how it works. Cut every sentence containing "under the hood", "the way this works is", or a library name. Those belong in the technical deep-dive that someone will request afterwards.
  • "I can't do a live run — the data can't leave their network." Then the demo happens on their machine or via a screen-share from their environment, and your rehearsal must happen there too. Rehearse in the actual venue conditions whenever they are unusual.

✅ Done when

A written script, a recorded take, a scorecard with your numbers, three fixed moments, and a failure plan that you have verified by breaking the app — including a full run with the network off. Keep the script; it is the first draft of every demo script you will write.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~2 h + the demo
Deliver the PoC

🎯 Objective

Produce the catalog evidence: "PoC delivered within days that advanced the engagement." Both halves matter — within days (the clock is part of the evidence) and advanced the engagement (a demo that produced no decision is a nice afternoon, not evidence). Real engagement if you have one; if not, run it internally against a proxy audience — the artifacts are identical.

📦 Setup — the audience

  • Real: the stakeholders from your discovery. Ideally three roles in the room — sponsor, end user, and the skeptic/IT.
  • Proxy: an internal audience with assigned roles who will actually behave like stakeholders: a senior who plays the budget holder, someone from the domain who plays the end user, and a security-minded engineer who plays the skeptic. Give them the roles in writing beforehand — a polite audience produces worthless evidence.
  • Non-negotiable: at least one person who did not help you build it must operate it with their own hands during the session.

🔬 Steps

1
Deliver it. The rehearsed ten minutes from Lab 3, followed by open discussion. Record the session (ask first). If you have a second person, they observe and write — they capture reactions, you drive.
2
Capture feedback in the room, structurally. Fill this during and immediately after — within 30 minutes, before the details blur: 
# feedback-log.md — {use case} demo — {date} — {who was there}

REACTIONS (who + what + when in the demo)
- Trang leaned in at 02:30 when the confidence flag appeared
- Minh (IT) went quiet after the upload — asked about data location at 08:10
- Sponsor photographed the results table at 05:40          # buy signal

ASKED FOR (verbatim · × how many different people)
- "Can it flag duplicate bookings?"                        ×2  ← real scope
- "Does it work for the sub-contractor template?"          ×1
- "Can it write straight into the TMS screen?"             ×3  ← biggest

OBJECTIONS / RISKS RAISED
- "Where does the data go?" (Minh) — answered: I don't know their policy;
  owner = Minh; follow-up call booked
- "What happens when it's wrong at 2am?" — no batch runs in phase 1

NO REACTION (cut candidates)
- the CSV export nobody mentioned
- the prompt expander I was proud of

QUESTIONS I COULD NOT ANSWER
- real accuracy (needs a labelled set) · TMS payload format · their DPA

THE DECISION
- Outcome: ____________________  Owner: ________  Date: ________
3
Send the 24-hour follow-up. Use the Part IV template: recording link, what we showed with real numbers, what we heard (their words), what I don't know yet, two sized options, one ask. Same day if at all possible. Save a copy in the pack — the sent email is evidence.
4
Assemble the pack. One folder, this structure, so a reviewer can evaluate it without you present: 
poc-{customer}-{date}/
├── README.md              # 1 page: use case, clock, outcome — see below
├── demo-recording.mp4     # or 4–6 screenshots of the real run
├── demo-script.md         # from Lab 3
├── sprint-log.md          # from Lab 2 — the clock IS the evidence
├── feedback-log.md        # from step 2
├── gap-list.md            # the 7 rows with sizes
├── results.csv            # corpus run: N docs, misses, latency, cost
├── followup-email.md      # what you actually sent
└── decision.md            # what happened next, and who owns it
5
Write the README — this is the evidence itself. Half a page, numbers only, no adjectives: 
# PoC — booking email extraction — Freight Co.

USE CASE     Extract 8 booking fields from customer emails → prefilled TMS entry
ORIGIN       FD1 discovery, 2026-07-2x · problem statement signed by Linh (Ops)

CLOCK        Use case agreed  Tue 09:00
             Data received    Tue 18:40  (31 real emails, redacted by champion)
             Build start      Wed 09:00
             Demo delivered   Fri 10:00
             → 2 working days build · 3 days end to end

BUILT        Streamlit · Claude Sonnet · upload → extract → confidence-flagged
             editable table · deployed to a URL · offline fallback
SKIPPED      auth · TMS write-back (mocked) · evals · monitoring · non-standard
             templates — all stated explicitly in the demo

MEASURED     31 documents · 27 clean · 4 misses (all forwarded chains, ref in
             subject) · p50 6.2s · $0.011/document · ≈$46/month at 140/day

AUDIENCE     Linh (Ops lead, sponsor) · Trang (senior operator) · Minh (IT)
             Trang operated it herself on 2 of her own emails

OUTCOME      Phase 2 approved Wed 08/xx: option B (6 weeks, TMS-integrated,
             + duplicate flagging). Trang to supply 200 labelled emails for
             the eval set. Security review booked with Minh for 08/xx.
             → sized in FD3 · value case in FD4 · evals in AI9
6
The reviewer test. Hand the folder to a senior who was not there and ask them four questions: What was built? How long did it take? What did the customer actually do with their own hands? What decision came out of it? Any hesitation marks the file to rewrite. If they cannot find a decision, the PoC has not yet "advanced the engagement" — go and get the decision, then update decision.md.
7
Close the loop back into the kit. Ninety minutes: pull every reusable piece you wrote into your Lab 1 template, delete the customer data per your retention promise, and note in the kit README what this engagement added. This step is what makes the next PoC faster — skipping it is how FDEs stay at score 2 for years.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "There was no decision — they said 'nice, let's discuss internally'." Common, and recoverable. Chase within a week with a smaller, easier ask ("15 minutes to pick A or B", or "can Trang send 200 examples?"). Record what finally moved it in decision.md; a delayed decision you drove is still evidence, a decision you never chased is not.
  • "The demo went badly — a live run failed." Keep the pack anyway and write the honest post-mortem: what failed, what the recovery was, what you changed. A recovered failure, documented, is genuinely stronger evidence of score 3 than a smooth demo on cherry-picked inputs.
  • "I have no real engagement." Run it internally on a real Golden Owl workflow with a genuine internal owner who can say yes or no to a next step. Real internal pain, a real decision, real evidence — just note the context in the README.
  • "The customer wants to keep using the prototype." Excellent signal, dangerous default. Have the prototype-debt conversation from Part V now: named users, fixed end date, written "unsupported pilot" line, and the gap list as the phase-2 scope.

✅ Done when

The pack exists, the README shows a build measured in days, a non-builder operated it with their own hands, decision.md names a real outcome with an owner and a date, and a senior reviewer answered all four questions without asking you anything. That folder is your catalog evidence — and the template for every PoC you deliver from here on.
