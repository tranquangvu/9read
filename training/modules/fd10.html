Documentation, Handover & Adoption

Create documentation, training and operational handover so the customer can own the solution — and then make sure people actually use it. The last mile where good projects quietly die.

Why it matters for an FDE: A successful FDE project is one the customer can operate themselves — good enablement creates the next contract.
Part I · Mindset

Your success is measured by them not needing you

Every other module in this program makes you more useful inside the engagement. This one makes you removable from it — and that is the paradox worth internalizing early. The engineer who becomes the single point of knowledge feels indispensable for about two quarters, then becomes the reason the account can never expand: nobody will buy a second system from a team whose first system only runs while one specific person answers Slack messages. Renewals are signed by customers who feel ownership, not dependency.

Handover is also the moment your engineering quality gets audited by reality. A system you can operate because you remember which container to restart is not a finished system; it is a demo with a production URL. Writing the runbook is how you discover the missing health check, the undocumented env var, and the alarm that has no response.

What "they own it" actually requires

1 · Operate

Deploy a change, read the dashboards, know what "healthy" looks like, respond to the five alarms that actually fire (PR1).

2 · Debug

Take a user complaint ("it got this one wrong") to a trace, to a cause, to a fix — without guessing (PR11).

3 · Extend

Change a prompt, add a document type, adjust a threshold — and prove it didn't break anything, via the eval suite (AI9).

4 · Know the limits

Know what the system is not allowed to be trusted with, and what to do when it is wrong. Undocumented limits become tomorrow's incident (FD6).

Three of those four are impossible to hand over in a slide deck. That is why this module is 20% writing and 80% rehearsal: the customer's engineer does the deploy, runs the eval, works the alarm — with you watching, not typing.

The handover curve

Most engagements never draw this curve, so the crossover never happens: the FDE stays the operator until the budget ends, and then the system degrades within a quarter. Put the four phases in the plan on day one (FD8) with a named customer owner per phase.

Why AI systems need more handover than normal software

PropertyNormal softwareAI system — the extra handover burden
BehaviourDeterministic: same input → same outputNondeterministic. "It worked yesterday" is not a bug report. Operators need a mental model of distributions, not cases (FN2)
CorrectnessTests pass or failQuality is a score on a golden set. Whoever owns the system must be able to run and read an eval (AI9)
The "source code"Code in git, reviewedPrompts are logic. Ungoverned prompt edits are the #1 post-handover regression (AI1)

The rule this table implies: handing over an AI system means handing over an evaluation capability, not just a deployment. If the customer cannot answer "is it still as good as at go-live?" without you, you have not handed anything over.
Part II · The documents

Four documents, four different readers

Documentation fails when one document tries to serve everyone. The Diátaxis framework explains why: there are four distinct needs — learning, doing, looking up, and understanding — and mixing them produces a document that is too long to use and too shallow to learn from. Map each need to a document with one named owner and one named audience.

Tutorial · learning

"Your first week with the system." Hand-held, guaranteed to succeed, one happy path. Lives inside the user guide and the training session.

How-to · doing

"Deploy a prompt change." "Handle a stuck queue." Task-shaped, numbered, assumes competence. The runbook + AI-maintenance guide.

Reference · looking up

Field dictionary, config vars, alarm catalogue, API contract. Dry, complete, scannable. Generated where possible so it cannot rot.

Explanation · understanding

Why this architecture, why this threshold, what we rejected. The architecture & decision doc (FD3). Read once, referenced for years.

1 · The runbook — the document that lets you sleep

The runbook is written for a competent engineer who has never seen your system, at 2am, under stress. Optimize for that reader: commands they can copy, screenshots of what "normal" looks like, and no prose they have to interpret. Every alarm you configured in PR1 needs a written response here, or it is just a notification that trains people to ignore notifications. 

# RUNBOOK — {system name}        owner: {team} · on-call: {rota} · updated: {date}

## 0 · What this is (3 sentences)          # + link to architecture doc
## 1 · Access checklist                    # repos, cloud console, dashboards, secrets store, vendor accounts
## 2 · Deploy
    normal:    git tag → CI pipeline "prod-deploy" → smoke check §3
    hotfix:    ...
    who may:   {role}                      # not a person's name
## 3 · Health checks — what "normal" looks like
    dashboard {url}: p95 latency < 4s · error rate < 1% · queue depth < 200
    quality panel:   low-confidence rate 8-12% · override rate < 10%
    daily 09:00: eyeball the quality panel for 60 seconds       # the cheapest control you own
## 4 · Alarms → response (§4.1 … §4.5)     # template below; ONE per real alarm
## 5 · Routine maintenance
    weekly:    review override samples (15 min) → feed AI-maintenance guide §2
    monthly:   cost review · key rotation check · eval suite full run
    quarterly: model deprecation check · golden-set refresh
## 6 · Rollback
    app:       `make rollback RELEASE=<prev>`   (≈ 4 min, no data migration)
    prompt:    `make prompt-rollback VERSION=<prev>` (≈ 40 s, takes effect on next request)
    kill switch: feature flag `ai_extraction_enabled=false` → falls back to manual queue
## 7 · Escalation ladder
    L1 customer ops (business hours) → L2 customer platform team → L3 {us}, {channel}, {SLA}
## 8 · Known quirks                        # the things you'd say out loud but never write down. Write them down.

Each alarm gets the same five-line shape, so a stressed reader never has to parse a new format: 

### 4.2 ALARM: low_confidence_rate > 20% for 30 min
SYMPTOM    operators see a flood of "needs review" items; queue grows
FIRST LOOK dashboard {url} → panel "confidence by document type" → which type moved?
LIKELY 1   new template from a customer → add 3 examples to the prompt
           (AI-maintenance guide §3) → run evals → canary
LIKELY 2   upstream OCR degraded → check the document pipeline queue (PR9)
LIKELY 3   model provider incident → status page {url} → switch route (PR2)
MITIGATE   raise manual-queue staffing; the system is degraded, not broken —
           it is flagging rather than guessing. Say that to the business.
ROLLBACK   `make prompt-rollback VERSION=<prev>` if it started after a prompt change
ESCALATE   > 2h or accuracy < 90% on spot-check → L3 + notify the process owner
Runbook test: a colleague who has never touched the system performs a deploy and works one simulated alarm using the document alone, while you stay silent. Every question they ask out loud is a missing line. This is Lab 1 — and it is the only reliable way to know a runbook is real.

2 · The architecture & decision doc

You already wrote most of this in FD3; handover means updating it to as-built and adding the decision log. The decisions matter more than the diagram: in eighteen months someone will ask "why don't we just fine-tune?" or "why is the threshold 0.85?" and without a record they will re-litigate it badly. Keep each decision to five lines — context, options, choice, consequence, revisit-when. 

## ADR-007 · Human review below 0.85 confidence            2026-05-14
CONTEXT     invoice extraction; a wrong amount reaches payment run in 1 step
OPTIONS     (a) auto-post everything (b) review all (c) confidence gate
DECISION    (c) gate at 0.85 → ~12% of volume to review queue
CONSEQUENCE ~97% of value auto-posted; review team of 1 instead of 4;
            residual risk: silent errors above the gate ≈ 0.4% of items
REVISIT     when field-F1 on the golden set > 0.97 for 2 consecutive months,
            or if the review queue exceeds 20% of volume

3 · The user guide — and its most important section

Write it task-oriented ("Correct an extracted invoice"), never feature-oriented ("The Corrections Panel"). Users arrive with a job, not a curiosity. Screenshots beat paragraphs; a 90-second screen recording beats both for the top three tasks.

And then write the section nobody writes: what it gets wrong. Stating limits plainly is the single highest-leverage trust move available to you (FD6). Users who are told "it misreads handwritten totals about one time in ten — that's why the field is highlighted" trust the other nine. Users who discover the failure themselves conclude the whole thing is broken and go back to the spreadsheet. 

# {System} — user guide

## What this does for you
One paragraph, in their words, from the discovery quotes (FD1).

## Task 1 · Process a booking email          # numbered, screenshots, 6 steps max
## Task 2 · Correct a wrong field
## Task 3 · Handle an item the AI flagged
## Task 4 · Find something processed last week

## What it can't do — read this once           # ← the trust section
· It reads handwritten totals wrong ~1 in 10. That field is highlighted
  yellow — always check it.
· It does not know about contracts signed in the last 24 hours.
· It will NOT be right about currency on multi-currency invoices.
  For those, the item is flagged and comes to you.
· It never sends anything to a customer by itself.
· When it is unsure it flags rather than guesses — a full review queue
  is the system working, not failing.

## When something looks wrong
1. Fix it in the panel (your correction is recorded and improves the system)
2. If the same wrong pattern happens 3+ times → tell {role} with 2 examples
3. Never "work around" it silently — silent workarounds are how we lose the
   signal that something changed.

## Getting help — {channel}, {hours}, {who}

4 · The AI-maintenance guide — the one that is always missing

This document has no equivalent in traditional handover, and it is the difference between a system that improves after you leave and one that decays. It answers three questions: how do I change a prompt without breaking it?, how do I know quality is still fine?, and what do I do when the model I depend on is deprecated? 

# AI-MAINTENANCE GUIDE — {system}

## 1 · Where the "AI logic" lives
prompts/            versioned in git — treat as code, PR + review required
  extract_v7.md     ← current production (see config PROMPT_VERSION)
evals/golden/       142 labelled cases; add to it, never shrink it
config/thresholds.yaml   confidence gate 0.85 (ADR-007)

## 2 · How to change a prompt SAFELY          # the drill — rehearse it in training
1. Never edit prompts in the vendor console. Ever. It is not versioned.
2. Branch → edit prompts/extract_v8.md → describe the intent in the PR
3. `make eval SUITE=golden`      → field-F1 must be ≥ 0.94 AND ≥ baseline − 0.005
4. `open reports/eval_diff.html` → LOOK at the 5 newly-failing cases.
   A passing average with new failure modes is a regression in disguise.
5. Merge → deploy to canary 10% → watch 24h: override rate, low-conf rate, cost
6. Promote to 100%. If override rate rose > 2 points → rollback (runbook §6)

## 3 · Adding a new document type / edge case
1. Collect 5+ real examples → label them → add to evals/golden/
2. THEN change the prompt (examples first: they define "correct")
3. Follow §2 from step 2

## 4 · Reading the quality metrics (what is normal, what is action)
override rate      8-12%  normal   |  > 15% two weeks running → investigate
low-conf rate      8-12%  normal   |  sudden jump → input changed, not model
eval field-F1      ≥ 0.94          |  below → do not deploy
cost / 1k items    $X            |  > 1.3× → check retries + context growth (PR2)
user thumbs-down   < 3%           |  read every one of them weekly
NB: none of these mean anything alone. Always compare to the same
weekday/week — volume seasonality moves every ratio.

## 5 · When the model is deprecated (expect this within 12 months)
1. Vendor notice → put a dated ticket in the backlog immediately
2. Run the full golden set against the candidate model → diff report
3. Expect prompt tweaks; budget 3-5 days, not 3 hours
4. Canary 10% for 3 days, compare override rate before promoting
5. Update ADRs + this guide with the new baseline numbers
The failure this prevents: six weeks after handover, a smart customer engineer "improves" the prompt in the vendor playground, pastes it into production, and accuracy drops four points on document types nobody tested. Nothing alerts. It surfaces in a finance audit two months later — and the story becomes "the AI system degraded", not "we skipped the eval". Rehearse §2 with them during training.
Part III · Enablement

Training: three audiences, three different sessions

The classic handover mistake is one big session for everyone: two hours in which operators sit through user-interface tours, users sit through deployment pipelines, and nobody retains anything. Split by what the person must be able to do on Monday.

AudienceMust be able toFormatPassing test
End usersDo their 3 daily tasks; know the limits; report a bad output usefully45 min, small groups (≤ 8), on their real data, hands on keyboard from minute 10Each person completes 3 real items unaided while you watch
Operators / ITDeploy, monitor, work the top-5 alarms, roll back90 min, runbook-driven, they drive; end with a simulated incidentThey resolve a fault you inject, using only the runbook
Maintaining engineersChange a prompt safely, run + read evals, handle a model update2 × 90 min, code walkthrough + a real change shipped togetherThey ship a small prompt change through eval → canary → promote
Sponsor / managersRead the value dashboard; know what to escalate and when20 min, no architecture, only numbers and decisions (FD5)They can explain the ROI number in their own words

The hands-on session shape (45 minutes)

00–05 — Why this exists, in their language: the pain quote from discovery, and the honest promise. "This should take the Friday report from three hours to about forty minutes. It will be wrong sometimes — I'll show you exactly where."
05–10 — One live demo of the happy path. Real data, one task, start to finish. No feature tour.
10–30 — Hands on keyboard. They do the same task on their own items while you circulate. This is the session; everything else is packaging. Write down every hesitation you observe — each one is a UI fix or a guide fix.
30–38 — The limits, demonstrated: deliberately run a case it gets wrong. Show the flag, the correction flow, and where the correction goes. Trust is built here, not in the demo.
38–45 — Close: where the guide lives, how to report a bad output, office-hours times, and the one commitment you want ("use it for every item this week, not just the easy ones").
  • Record every session (with permission). New joiners for the next two years will watch it, and it is the cheapest evidence for your own portfolio.
  • Train on their data, not your demo fixtures. The moment a user sees their own messy case processed, adoption stops being an argument.
  • Never train the week before go-live only. Skills decay; run a 20-minute refresher two weeks after launch, once real questions exist.
  • Watch for the silent user. The person who asks nothing in a training session is usually the one who never logs in again. Go to them individually.

Train the trainer: the champion model

You cannot train 200 users and you should not try. Train 3–5 champions properly — the people the team already asks for help (FD7 covers how to find and keep them) — and have them run the sessions for their own teams while you sit at the back. Three effects: your reach multiplies, the knowledge stays after you leave, and peer credibility does work no vendor can do ("the consultant said it works" versus "Mai from our team uses it every day").

Office hours during the fade-out

A standing 30-minute weekly slot for the first 6–8 weeks after go-live outperforms an always-open Slack channel: it batches interruptions, makes questions visible to the whole group, and — crucially — gives you a defensible way to stop being on demand. Keep a public log of every question and its answer; when the same question appears twice, it belongs in the guide, not in the log. Watch the attendance curve: it should fall. If it does not, adoption is failing somewhere upstream and no amount of support will fix it.

Part IV · Adoption

Adoption is a metric, not a hope

This is the part that decides renewal. A technically excellent system with 20% usage is, to the person who signed the invoice, a failure — and they are right, because the ROI case was built on everyone using it (FD4). Instrument adoption on day one of go-live, put it on the same dashboard as latency and cost (PR1), and review it weekly with the champion.

MetricDefinitionWhat a bad number is telling you
Activation% of trained users who completed ≥1 real task in week 1Low → the training didn't land, or access/permissions are broken. Check logins before blaming motivation
Task coverage% of eligible items processed through the system (not around it)The truest adoption number. Low → people are cherry-picking easy cases, or a workaround still exists
Week-4 retention% of week-1 users still active in week 4The novelty cliff. A drop here almost always means quality disappointed, not that the UI is hard
Override / correction rate% of AI outputs edited by a human before useRising → quality drift (AI9). Near zero → nobody is checking, which is worse
Time-to-taskMinutes per item, before vs after (the baseline from FD4)No improvement → the AI is added work, not replaced work. Redesign the workflow, not the model
DepthUsers using ≥2 of the 3 core tasksShallow use predicts churn at renewal; it means only one habit formed
Instrumentation note: "eligible items processed through the system" requires knowing the denominator — the total volume, including what went around you. Get that number from the source system during discovery (FD1), or you will be reporting a percentage of an unknown.

Two details do most of the work here. First, the pilot group must be friendly but real — enthusiasts who handle genuine volume, not managers trying it out. Second, turning off the old path: as long as the spreadsheet still exists, coverage stalls around 50% and the ROI never materializes. Agree the shutdown date with the process owner before launch, conditional on the pilot metrics — asking afterwards turns a plan into a negotiation.

Remove friction: put the AI where the work already happens

The most common adoption killer is not quality — it is a new tab. If a user must leave the CRM, log into a separate portal, paste something, and copy the result back, you have added steps to their day and asked them to be grateful. Integrating into the system of record (PR8) routinely doubles usage with no model change at all.

✗ Friction patterns
  • Separate portal + separate login
  • Copy-paste in either direction
  • Output in a format they must reformat
  • Any wait longer than the task deserves
  • Asking for feedback with a form nobody opens
✓ Zero-friction patterns
  • A prefilled field in the tool they already use
  • A draft waiting in their inbox / queue each morning
  • A Slack or Teams thread where the work is discussed
  • Feedback as one click inside the flow (👍 / edit)
  • SSO — never a new password (PR13)

The quality dip after go-live — plan for it

Every AI go-live dips. The pilot ran on tidy cases with motivated users; production brings the scanned fax, the customer who writes in three languages, and a colleague who tries to break it on purpose. Perceived quality falls exactly when attention is highest. What separates recovery from abandonment is not accuracy — it is visible responsiveness:

  • Pre-frame it before launch. "Weeks one to three will surface cases we've never seen. We ship fixes every Friday during that period." Predicted problems are competence; surprise problems are failure (FD6).
  • Ship something every week for the first month — even small. The pattern of improvement matters more than the size.
  • Close the loop on every reported error, by name. "Mai reported the multi-currency case on Tuesday — fixed and in the golden set as of today." One public example teaches a hundred people that reporting works.
  • Separate "wrong" from "flagged". Users read a full review queue as failure. Explain that flagging is the design, and show the number it saved.

Hands-on practice

Handover skill is proven by other people succeeding without you — every lab below is validated by someone else, not by you re-reading your own document.

LAB 1 ~2.5 h

Write the runbook — then let it be tested

Full runbook for a system you built: deploy, health checks, top-5 alarms with responses, rollback — validated by a colleague working an injected fault.

LAB 2 ~2 h

The user guide & its limits section

Task-oriented guide with an honest "what it gets wrong" section, tested by watching someone complete a task with the guide alone.

LAB 3 ~3 h incl. prep

Run a 45-minute training session

Deliver and record a real hands-on session for operators or maintainers, with an exercise, a deliberate failure demo, and a feedback loop.

LAB 4 · EVIDENCE ~4 h + real usage

The handover pack & adoption evidence

Runbook + user guide + AI-maintenance guide + training recording, plus usage numbers or a signed acceptance.

In the field

Week 12 of an invoice-extraction engagement. Accuracy is 96%, the pilot team loves it, and the FDE writes a 40-page handover document, presents it in a two-hour session to twenty people, and rolls off.

Week 16: usage is at 30%. Half the AP team never logged in again after training. The one alarm that fires — low-confidence spikes on a new supplier template — has no written response, so the customer's ops lead mutes it. Nobody has run the eval suite; nobody knows how.

The recovery took three weeks and looked nothing like documentation. The FDE returned, sat with four AP clerks for a morning (finding that the review queue opened in a separate portal — an extra login per item), moved the review panel into the ERP screen they already used, trained two champions properly, and wrote a six-page runbook with five alarms and a rollback command. Coverage went from 30% to 82% in a month. The 40-page document had never been the problem — nobody had ever watched a real user try to use the thing.

At the renewal meeting, the number that mattered was not accuracy. It was "82% of invoices, 1,900 hours a year, operated entirely by your team since March". That sentence funded phase two.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Documentation as a final-week activity

Written from memory, under deadline, by someone who has stopped caring. Write the runbook the first time you deploy and the ADRs the day you decide — both are cheap then and expensive later.

✗ The 40-page monolith

One document for four audiences is read by none of them. Four short documents with clear readers beat one comprehensive one, every time.

✗ Training by demo

Watching you succeed teaches nothing. If hands were not on keyboards for 20 of the 45 minutes, it was a presentation, not training.

✗ Hiding the limits

Users always find the failure mode; the only variable is whether they hear it from you first. Undocumented limits are discovered as betrayal (FD6).

✗ Handing over without the eval capability

Deployment access without the ability to measure quality guarantees silent degradation. The golden set and the eval command are part of the handover, not a nice-to-have (AI9).

✓ Make them drive during training

Your hands off the keyboard. Every place they get stuck is a documentation bug found for free — fix it the same day, while the session is fresh.

✓ Report adoption like you report uptime

Coverage, retention and hours saved on the weekly dashboard from day one. It shows problems early, and at renewal it is the story (FD4).

✓ Answer twice, then document

A rule that keeps documentation honest and small: the second time a question is asked, the answer goes into the guide — with the questioner's wording, not yours.

FDE Mastery · FD10 Documentation, Handover & Adoption
Lab 1 · ~2.5 h
Write the runbook — then let it be tested

🎯 Objective

Produce a runbook that a competent engineer who has never seen your system can use to deploy it, understand whether it is healthy, respond to its five most likely alarms, and roll back — validated the only way that counts: by watching someone else do it while you stay silent.

📦 What you need

  • A system you actually built — a lab project from AI4/PR3 is fine; a real engagement is better.
  • Its dashboards/traces (PR1) — a runbook without observability is fiction.
  • A colleague willing to spend 45 minutes being your test subject.
  • A staging environment where you can safely break something.

🔬 Steps

1
Skeleton first, from the template in Part II. Create the file with all eight section headings empty. Filling headings is easy; inventing structure at midnight is not. 
docs/
  runbook.md          ← this lab
  user-guide.md       ← Lab 2
  ai-maintenance.md   ← Lab 4
  architecture.md     ← from FD3
  README.md           ← index: 4 links + "who to call" + last-updated dates
2
Write §2 Deploy and §6 Rollback by doing them. Run a real deploy and a real rollback with the document open, writing each command as you execute it. Anything you did from memory is exactly what the reader will not know. Record the wall-clock duration of each — "rollback ≈ 4 min" is the sentence an incident commander needs.
3
Write §3 Health checks as numbers, not adjectives. "Normal" must be a range someone can compare against at 2am: 
p95 latency        < 4s      (typical 1.8-2.6s)
error rate         < 1%      (typical 0.2%)
queue depth        < 200     (typical 20-60, spikes at 09:00)
low-confidence     8-12%     ← quality signal, not an infra signal
cost / 1k items    ~$Y       (alert at 1.3×)
Screenshot the dashboard in its healthy state and paste it in. A picture of "normal" is worth a page of thresholds.
4
Pick the top five alarms — from evidence, not imagination. Rank by (likelihood × pain): what has already broken in dev, what depends on a third party, what has a queue, what involves a credential that expires, what changes when input data changes. Write each in the five-line format from Part II. If an alarm has no written response, either write one or delete the alarm — a notification nobody can act on trains people to ignore all of them.
5
Write §8 Known quirks. Ten minutes, stream of consciousness: everything you would tell a teammate over coffee. "The first request after a cold start takes 30s." "The seed script must run before the import job." "Vendor rate limits reset at midnight UTC, not local." This section is often the most-read page of the whole pack.
6
The cold-read test — the actual lab. Hand the runbook to your colleague. Inject one fault into staging beforehand (revoke an API key, fill the queue, point at a wrong model name, stop a worker). Then: 
Brief to your tester:
"Using only this document, (1) deploy the current version,
 (2) tell me whether the system is healthy and how you know,
 (3) something is wrong — find it, respond to it, and roll back
     if the runbook tells you to."

Your rules: say NOTHING for 45 minutes. Write down every question
they ask out loud and every place they hesitate for > 10 seconds.
7
Fix the document, not the tester. Every question becomes an edit. Then have a second person do the same test with a different injected fault. Two passes is where a runbook usually crosses from "written" to "usable".

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • "My system has no alarms." Then you have found the real gap — go set up three in PR1 first. A runbook is a response layer over a detection layer; there is nothing to write without one.
  • The tester finishes with no questions. Suspicious. Usually they know your system already, or the injected fault was too obvious. Retest with someone further away and a subtler fault (degraded quality rather than a hard crash).
  • The document is 20 pages. You are writing explanation inside a how-to. Move the "why" to the architecture doc and keep the runbook at procedures. Target: 4–8 pages.
  • Commands don't work on their machine. Excellent find — §1 Access checklist is incomplete. That is the most common real-world handover failure and you just caught it for free.

✅ Done when

Two different people have each deployed, assessed health, and resolved an injected fault using the runbook alone, and the second run produced fewer than three questions. The document is under 8 pages, every command has been executed at least once from the page, and every configured alarm has a written response.
Lab 2 · ~2 h
The user guide & its limits section

🎯 Objective

Write a task-oriented user guide including the section most teams omit — an honest account of what the system gets wrong and what to do about it — then prove it works by watching a real person complete a task with the guide and no help from you.

📦 What you need

  • The same system as Lab 1, running with realistic data.
  • Its eval results or error log — you cannot write honest limits from intuition; you need the actual failure distribution (AI9).
  • A test reader who is not an engineer if at all possible.
  • Screen-recording tool (Loom, QuickTime, OBS) for the top-3 task clips.

🔬 Steps

1
List the tasks, not the features. Write down what a user comes to the system to get done, in their vocabulary. Rank by frequency. Keep the top 4; everything else goes in a "less common tasks" appendix. 
✗ feature-shaped         ✓ task-shaped
"The Review Panel"       "Correct a field the AI got wrong"
"Confidence scoring"     "Decide whether to trust an extraction"
"Export module"          "Get last week's items into the month-end file"
2
Write each task in ≤ 6 numbered steps, each starting with a verb, each with a screenshot when the UI is involved. If a task needs more than 6 steps, that is a product finding — note it; it is probably also your adoption problem.
3
Derive the limits section from data. Take 50 recent outputs (or your eval failures) and cluster the errors. Write the top 4 clusters as user-facing sentences with a frequency and an action: 
Pattern:  "It gets {X} wrong about {N} times in {M}.
           You'll see {visible signal}. Do {action}."

Examples:
· It misreads handwritten totals about 1 in 10 times. Those fields
  are highlighted yellow — always check them against the document.
· On multi-currency invoices it may pick the wrong currency. Anything
  with two currency symbols is flagged and comes to you automatically.
· It knows nothing about contracts signed in the last 24 hours.
· It never sends anything to a customer on its own. Ever.
Frequencies must come from measurement. If you don't have the number, write "sometimes" and go measure it this week — but never round down to make it look better; the first time reality exceeds your stated limit, you lose the room (FD6).
4
Write the "when something looks wrong" flow — three lines: fix it here, report it like this (2 examples, this channel), never work around silently. Users need permission to report and a channel that answers; without both, your quality signal disappears.
5
Record 90-second clips for the top three tasks. No editing, no intro music, your normal voice. Most users will watch these instead of reading — and they are reusable in the training session (Lab 3).
6
The silent usability test. Give your test reader one real task and the guide. 
Brief: "Complete this task using only the guide. Please think out
loud — say what you're looking for and what you expect to happen.
I won't answer questions, and that's not because you're doing badly."

You log:  time to complete · every hesitation > 10s · every place
they scrolled back · every wrong turn · every question asked aloud
· the moment (if any) they lost confidence in the system
Three testers is the sweet spot: the first finds structural problems, the second confirms them, the third finds the wording.
7
Rewrite from the log, then close the loop with the AI-maintenance guide (Lab 4): every limit you documented should map to an eval case. Documented limits that nobody tests silently become documented lies as the system changes.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • "Writing the limits will scare the customer." The opposite is true, consistently — and the limits will be discovered anyway. Stated limits are a promise you control; discovered limits are a broken one. Frame each with its mitigation ("that's why it's highlighted").
  • Your tester keeps asking you things. Hold the silence. It feels rude for about ninety seconds; it is the whole value of the exercise.
  • The guide is fine but the task is painful. Documentation cannot rescue a bad workflow. Log it as a product change — Part IV's friction list is where adoption is actually won.
  • You can't measure error frequencies. Sample 50 outputs by hand and count. Two hours of manual labelling gives you honest numbers and doubles as the seed of a golden set.

✅ Done when

A non-author completed a real task using the guide alone, with fewer than three hesitations and no questions to you; the limits section names at least four specific failure modes with measured frequencies and actions; and three task clips exist. Bonus signal of a good guide: your tester says something like "oh, so that yellow field means it isn't sure" — the limits landed.
Lab 3 · ~3 h incl. prep
Run a 45-minute training session

🎯 Objective

Deliver — and record — a hands-on training session in which the participants, not you, do the work; then measure whether they can actually do the thing afterwards. This is the artifact that proves enablement, and the recording is reusable evidence for the skill matrix.

📦 What you need

  • 2–6 participants: colleagues, other trainees, or real users. Choose one audience only — operators or users or maintainers.
  • Working accounts/access for every participant, verified the day before. Access failures eat 20 minutes and all of the goodwill.
  • Real (or realistic) data for each participant to work on — including two ugly cases.
  • Recording set up, with everyone's permission stated on tape.

🔬 Steps

1
Write the one-sentence objective before anything else. "By the end, each operator can resolve a low-confidence alarm and roll back a prompt." If you cannot name an observable action, you are planning a presentation. Everything that does not serve that sentence gets cut.
2
Build the run sheet from the 45-minute shape in Part III — with the exercise designed first and the slides (if any) last: 
00-05  why this exists — the pain quote + the honest promise
05-10  one live demo, happy path, real data, no feature tour
10-30  HANDS ON: participants do it themselves          ← the session
       · task A on an easy case  (everyone succeeds)
       · task B on an ugly case  (everyone struggles a bit)
       · you circulate, hands OFF keyboards, logging hesitations
30-38  the limits, demonstrated live — run a case it gets wrong,
       show the flag, the correction, and where the correction goes
38-45  where the docs live · how to report a bad output ·
       office hours · one explicit commitment you ask for
3
Prepare the exercise data personally: one clean case per participant plus two known-ugly cases. Test every single one the day before — an exercise that fails for technical reasons destroys the session's credibility in a way content cannot recover.
4
For an operator/maintainer session, end with a drill instead of Q&A: inject a fault and let them work it from the runbook while you time it. For a maintainer session, the drill is a full prompt change: edit → make eval → read the diff → canary → promote or roll back. The drill is what converts "we were shown" into "we did it once".
5
Deliver it. Keep your hands off the keyboard. When someone is stuck, ask "what does the guide say here?" rather than taking over. Note the timestamps of every stuck moment — the recording plus those timestamps is your improvement backlog for both the product and the docs.
6
Measure it — three questions, sent within the hour: 
1. Can you now do {the objective} on your own?   yes / with notes / no
2. What is one thing this system will NOT do well?   (free text)
3. What one thing was confusing?                     (free text)
Question 2 is the real test: if participants cannot state a limit, your trust section did not land, and you will pay for it during the post-launch dip.
7
Self-review the recording within 24 hours with a stopwatch: how many minutes did participants have hands on keyboards? Under 15 out of 45 means it was a talk. Also count your filler-phrase habits and how often you rescued someone. Then fix the docs the session exposed — same week, while the evidence is fresh.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • Access breaks at minute 3. Have a shared fallback environment ready and pair people up. Never spend the session debugging logins — reschedule the hands-on part rather than deliver a demo.
  • Nobody asks anything. Silence in training is not comprehension. Switch to a direct prompt: "Nam, walk us through what you'd do with this flagged item." Participation returns immediately.
  • One senior person dominates. Give them a role — "you take the ugly case and tell us what you find" — and go around the room by name for the rest.
  • The live failure demo doesn't fail. Keep two known-bad cases in reserve. If it stubbornly succeeds, show a recorded failure instead; do not skip the limits section, it is the most valuable eight minutes of the session.
  • You run out of time. Cut the demo, never the hands-on block. The demo is for you; the exercise is for them.

✅ Done when

A recording exists in which participants spent 15+ of 45 minutes working themselves and completed the objective task; the feedback shows ≥ 80% answering "yes" or "with notes" to question 1, and a majority able to state at least one real limit; and the documentation edits the session exposed have been shipped.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~4 h + real usage
The handover pack & adoption evidence

🎯 Objective

Assemble the catalog evidence — "runbook, user guide, training and adoption evidence" — as one coherent pack that proves a customer team could own your system, plus the numbers or the signature that proves someone actually used it.

📦 Component 1 — the pack structure

One folder, one index page, four documents, every one with an owner and a last-updated date. The index is not optional: a folder of unlabeled files is a search problem, not a handover.

handover/{system}/
  README.md               # index: what each doc is FOR, who owns it, dates
  runbook.md              # Lab 1 — operators
  user-guide.md           # Lab 2 — end users (+ 3 task clips)
  ai-maintenance.md       # this lab — maintaining engineers
  architecture.md         # from FD3, updated to AS-BUILT + ADR log
  training/
    operators-2026-09.mp4 + slides.pdf + exercise-data/
    users-2026-09.mp4
  acceptance/
    adoption-report.md    # the numbers
    signoff.pdf           # or the email that serves as one

📦 Component 2 — the AI-maintenance guide

The document unique to AI handover; write it from the Part II template against your own system. Three sections must be executable, not descriptive — the safe-prompt-change drill, the metric-reading table, and the model-deprecation procedure. Test it the way you tested the runbook:

Validation: a colleague makes ONE real prompt improvement using only
this guide, and gets it to production (or correctly refuses to, because
the eval regressed). Watch silently. If they edit the prompt in a vendor
console at any point, section 2 is not clear enough.

Log for the pack:
  change made:        ...
  eval before/after:  field-F1 0.941 → 0.948, 2 new failures reviewed
  decision:           promoted / rolled back, because ...
  time taken:         ... min          ← quote this in the exec readout

📦 Component 3 — adoption evidence

Numbers if the system is live, a signed acceptance if it is not, and ideally both. Keep it to one page in the sponsor's language (FD5), with the baseline stated:

# Adoption report — {system}                    period: {4-8 weeks}
USERS        trained 24 · activated 22 (92%) · active in week 4: 19 (79%)
COVERAGE     1,840 of 2,240 eligible items processed in-system (82%)
             ↑ from 31% in week 2 — after moving review into the ERP screen
TIME         12.4 → 3.1 min median per item (baseline measured wk 0, FD4)
             ≈ 1,900 staff-hours/year at current volume
QUALITY      override rate 9.4% (stable) · field-F1 0.951 on golden set
             · 3 defects reported by users, all closed, 2 added to evals
OWNERSHIP    customer team has: deployed 4× · resolved 2 alarms unaided ·
             shipped 1 prompt change through the eval gate
OPEN         multi-currency invoices still manual (ADR-011, phase 2)

If you have no live system, the substitute is a written acceptance from the person who received the handover — and it must be specific enough to be falsifiable:

"I confirm that {team} received the handover pack for {system} on {date},
that {name} performed a deploy, an alarm response and a prompt change
using the documentation without assistance, and that we accept
operational ownership from {date}."     — {name, role}, {signature/email}

🔬 Final steps

1
Assemble the folder and write the README index — for each document: who it is for, when to open it, who owns it now, when it was last updated.
2
Run the ownership test with the receiving team (or two colleagues playing them): one person deploys and works an injected alarm from the runbook; another makes a prompt change through the eval gate from the maintenance guide; a third completes a user task from the guide. You observe in silence and time each one.
3
Hold the handover meeting — 30 minutes, four slides: what you're handing over, what the team demonstrated they can do (with the times from step 2), what is explicitly out of scope, and the fade-out schedule with dates and the escalation path. Ask for the acceptance at the end of that meeting, not by email a week later.
4
Collect adoption numbers 4–8 weeks after go-live and write the one-page report. If coverage is stalling, diagnose before reporting: friction, quality, or an alive workaround — Part IV's table maps each symptom to its cause.
5
Write your own two-paragraph retrospective for the portfolio: what the cold-read tests exposed, what the adoption curve did and why, and the one thing you will do earlier next time. This is what turns an artifact into evidence of judgment.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • No real customer to hand over to. Hand over to another Golden Owl team as a genuine transfer of ownership — they take on-call for two weeks. Realistic enough to expose every gap, and the acceptance is real.
  • The receiving team won't engage. A serious signal, not an admin problem: it usually means no named owner was ever agreed. Escalate it as a delivery risk (FD8) — a handover to nobody is an outage scheduled for later.
  • Adoption numbers are bad. Report them anyway, with the diagnosis and the fix plan. Sponsors forgive a bad number that arrives early with a plan; they do not forgive discovering it themselves.
  • No usage data exists. Instrument it now — even a daily count in a spreadsheet from the source system beats "everyone seems happy". Then state the baseline you compared against.
  • The pack goes stale immediately. Put the last-updated date at the top of every document and one line in the runbook's monthly maintenance: "review docs against reality (15 min)". Ownership of documents transfers with the system.

✅ Done when

The pack exists as one folder with an index; the receiving team demonstrated deploy, alarm response, prompt change and a user task using only its documents; a training recording is included; and either a one-page adoption report with real numbers or a specific signed acceptance sits in acceptance/. At target score 2 you produce this with a senior reviewing it; the day you can also diagnose a stalled adoption curve and fix its cause, you are at 3.
