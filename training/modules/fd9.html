Domain Ramp-up

Learn a new industry — fintech, healthcare, legal, logistics — in 1–2 weeks: glossary, key workflows, who to ask. Enough to design well and to be taken seriously, without pretending to be an expert.

Why it matters for an FDE: Every customer is a different industry — how fast you understand the business determines solution quality and customer respect.
Part I · Why it decides everything

You cannot design for a business you do not understand

An FDE lands in a new industry every few months. Freight forwarding in March, clinical operations in June, insurance claims in September. Nobody expects you to be an expert — but the gap between "knows nothing" and "understands the business well enough to argue about it" is where solution quality lives, and it is crossable in about ten working days if you attack it deliberately.

Three concrete things domain knowledge buys you

Better use-case selection

Without the value chain you cannot tell an expensive workflow from a visible one. Domain-blind FDEs automate what customers mention, not what costs.

Correct edge cases

In freight, a "split shipment" changes everything downstream. In claims, "subrogation" does. Miss the domain exception and your accuracy number is measured on the easy half.

Credibility, fast

Using their vocabulary correctly in week one changes how every later conversation goes. It is the cheapest trust available and it compounds.

The credibility effect is real and it is measurable in minutes

Sit in two kickoff meetings and you will see it. Engineer A says: "so when an order comes in…". Engineer B says: "so once the booking is confirmed and you've got the B/L draft — is that when the customs declaration gets prepared, or does that wait for the packing list?" The room reorganises around Engineer B within five minutes. Not because B knows more — B learned those four terms two days ago — but because B has demonstrably done homework, and homework signals that the customer's business matters to you.

The inverse is equally fast. Say "invoice" when they say "advice note", ask them to explain something printed on the first page of their own website, or use a term slightly wrong with confidence, and you get downgraded to "the technical vendor" — someone who is told requirements rather than consulted about them. Once that framing sets, discovery (FD1) gets dramatically harder: people simplify their answers for you.

The honest positioning. You are not claiming expertise; you are demonstrating homework. The line that works in week one: "I've spent a few days on how freight forwarding works, so I won't waste your time on basics — but I'll definitely get things wrong, and I'd rather you correct me hard than be polite." This buys goodwill and licenses your naive questions for the rest of the engagement.

What "enough" looks like — and where it stops

The failure mode on the other side: the engineer who reads for three weeks and starts telling the customer how their industry works. Two days of reading plus one veteran's correction beats two weeks of reading alone, every time. Domain knowledge is for asking better questions, not for winning arguments with people who have done the job for fifteen years.
Part II · The protocol

The 10-day ramp-up, day by day

Ten working days, roughly two hours a day, running in parallel with the first week of the engagement — not instead of it. The protocol exists because unstructured learning collapses into reading whatever is easiest to find (usually vendor marketing) and produces confidence without coverage.

Sources, in strict order of value

Most people get this backwards — they start with a web search and arrive with a generic mental model that does not match the customer. The order below is not a preference; it is the difference between learning this business and learning an industry stereotype.

The day-by-day

DAY 1 — Their artifacts. Get 10 real documents and a screen-share recording. Read them without judgment and collect every unfamiliar term into a raw list. Do not look anything up yet — the list itself is the day's output, and its length is your ignorance measured honestly.
DAY 2 — Their public face + first glossary pass. Website, annual report, exec LinkedIn. Now define the raw terms — but mark each definition [guessed], [from their doc] or [confirmed by human]. Most will start as guessed. That tag is the discipline of the whole module.
DAY 3 — Industry primer. Two hours maximum on how the industry works generically. Then immediately diff it: which parts of the standard model does this customer not do, and which do they do differently? The diff is more valuable than the primer.
DAY 4 — Value chain map. Draw the money path end to end and place the customer, their customers, and your use case on it. Mark where value is added, where cost accumulates, and where the process waits.
DAY 5 — Job ads + training material. Ten job ads for the role you are automating, plus any certification syllabus. Extract: tasks, tools, required judgment, what juniors get wrong. This tells you exactly what your AI has to be good at.
DAY 6 — Domain interview #1: the veteran. Not a discovery interview — a lesson (Part III). Bring your glossary and get it corrected line by line.
DAY 7 — Shadow + interview #2: the recent joiner. Watch the work happen. The person who joined six months ago still remembers what was confusing and can tell you what the veteran has forgotten they know.
DAY 8 — Regulatory frame. Which rules constrain the design? Produce the constraint list in engineering terms — retention, logging, human review, data residency, explainability — and take it into the security conversation (PR5).
DAY 9 — Teach-back. Explain the domain to someone who will correct you: a customer contact, ideally. Every correction is worth an hour of reading. Log them all.
DAY 10 — The domain brief. Write it (Part V), circulate it to your champion for correction, and file it in your personal domain library.

The regulatory frame — as engineering constraints

You do not need legal depth. You need the translation from regulation into things the system must do, written in a form a security reviewer recognises. Ask your champion two questions — "what would an auditor ask about this workflow?" and "has anything here ever been the subject of a complaint or an audit finding?" — then write the constraint table.

DomainTypical frameDesign constraint it creates
HealthcarePatient data protection; clinical safetyNo PHI to external APIs without a signed agreement; clinical decisions need a named human; full audit trail per record accessed

InsuranceFair-treatment rules, claims handling standardsDeclines cannot be automated; reasons must be stated in plain language; bias monitoring on any scoring

Use this table as a hypothesis generator, not as fact. Every row is a starting question for the customer's own compliance person — jurisdictions differ, and their internal policy is usually stricter than the regulation. Take the answers into PR5 before the architecture is settled, not after.
Part III · Learning from people

The domain interview: you are the student, not the consultant

This is the opposite posture from discovery. In FD1 you are an investigator hunting for a use case, careful not to lead the witness. Here you are an apprentice asking to be taught, and the more openly ignorant you are, the better it works. Mixing the two in one meeting is the classic mistake: the customer cannot tell whether to teach you or sell you, and you get a watered-down version of both.

The recent joiner is the most under-used person in the building. Expertise erases its own path: after ten years the veteran genuinely cannot remember that "surrendered" and "telex release" mean the same thing to some customers and different things to others, because it stopped being confusing a decade ago. The person who was confused last March still has the map.

The "explain it to me like I'm new" script

Forty-five minutes, six moves. Bring your draft glossary and your value-chain sketch — arriving with a wrong drawing is far more productive than arriving with a blank page, because people cannot resist correcting a diagram.

00–03 — Frame the posture. "I'm not here about the project today. I want to understand your world well enough to not waste your time later. Please correct me hard — I'd rather be embarrassed now than wrong in front of your boss."
03–15 — The end-to-end walk. "Take one real job from the moment it arrives to the moment you're paid. What happens, in order, and who touches it?" Then the follow-up that finds every hidden system: "and where does that live?"
15–25 — Glossary correction, out loud. Read your definitions back and let them fix each one. "I wrote that a consignee is the paying customer — is that right?" Wrong definitions get corrected faster and more precisely than blank ones get filled.
25–33 — The exceptions. "What percentage of jobs go exactly like you described?" (Never 100 — usually 60–80.) "Tell me about the ones that don't." This is where your edge cases and your accuracy ceiling live.
33–40 — The judgment step. "Where in this do you have to decide something rather than look something up? How do you decide?" This single question maps exactly what an AI can and cannot take over.
40–45 — Close. "If you were teaching me this job, what would you make me read?" · "Who else should I learn from?" · "Can I come back with the dumb questions I'll have on Thursday?" — always secure the return visit.

The naive-question licence

There is a window — roughly the first two weeks — where asking anything is free and even charming. After that the same question makes people wonder what you have been doing. So front-load ruthlessly: keep a running list of dumb questions and burn through them early. Two framings that keep the licence open longer: "Stupid question, but I want to make sure I've got this exactly right —" and "I read X; is that how it actually works here, or is that the textbook version?" The second one is particularly good, because it invites the expert to display superiority over a textbook, which almost everyone enjoys.

Verification: teach it back and be corrected

Reading produces the feeling of understanding; explaining produces the fact of it. On day 9, explain the domain out loud to someone who can correct you — the champion, a domain contact, or (weaker but still useful) a colleague who will ask "why?" at every step. Ten minutes, no notes, in this order: what business is this · how does money flow · the five workflows · the three things that go wrong · where our use case sits and what constrains it.

Score the teach-back by corrections received, not by how smooth it felt. A fluent explanation nobody corrected usually means you stayed at a safe altitude — "they handle bookings and then shipping." Go specific enough to be wrong: name the exception percentages, the system boundaries, the judgment calls. Being corrected fifteen times in ten minutes is a successful teach-back.
Part IV · AI as accelerant

Using Claude to ramp faster — and the rule that keeps you safe

An LLM compresses the reading half of ramp-up dramatically: it drafts glossaries from their documents, generates the questions you do not know to ask, and roleplays an expert for practice. It also invents plausible domain facts with total confidence (FN2) — and a confidently wrong domain claim in front of a customer costs more credibility than an honest question ever would.

THE VERIFICATION RULE — non-negotiable: never repeat an AI-generated domain fact to a customer unverified. Verified means one of: it appears in their own documents · a human practitioner confirmed it · it comes from a primary source you actually opened. Everything else stays in your notes tagged [unverified] and, if it matters, becomes a question rather than a statement. The failure is specific and brutal: you confidently use a term slightly wrong in a meeting, someone corrects you politely, and everything else you have said gets silently re-audited.

Four uses that genuinely work

1 · First-pass glossary from their own documents. This is the safest and highest-value use, because the source material is theirs — the model is extracting, not recalling.

I'm an engineer starting a project with a freight forwarding company.
Attached are 10 real booking emails and 3 Bills of Lading from them.

1. Extract every domain-specific term, abbreviation and code that
   appears, with the sentence it appeared in.
2. For each, give your best inferred definition — and label it
   [from document] if the document itself makes it clear, or
   [inferred] if you're reasoning from general knowledge.
3. Flag any term where their usage looks DIFFERENT from the standard
   industry meaning. Those are the important ones.
4. List 10 terms you'd expect in this industry that are absent here —
   absence might mean they outsource that part of the process.

Do not fill gaps with general knowledge presented as fact.
# then: every [inferred] row is a question for the day-6 interview

The traps, precisely

Plausible specifics

Invented percentages, regulation article numbers, "industry standard" thresholds and named forms. Numbers and citations are exactly where fabrication is most fluent and most damaging — verify every one before it enters a document.

Global average, not this customer

The model describes the textbook industry. Your customer is a mid-size firm in one country with a twenty-year-old system and three habits nobody else has. The variance is the design problem.

Stale regulation

Rules change and training data has a cutoff. Never take a compliance constraint from a model into a design decision — take it to their compliance person as a question (PR5).

Fluency mistaken for knowledge

The most dangerous trap: after an hour of good conversation with a model you feel knowledgeable and stop asking humans. The teach-back exists specifically to break this illusion.

Build a personal domain library

Ramp-up compounds only if you keep the artifacts. Every engagement leaves a brief; after five engagements you have a library, and the sixth industry is genuinely faster because the meta-patterns transfer even when the vocabulary does not — every domain has a document that is the legal source of truth, a step where everything waits for one person, a regulation that forbids automating the final decision, and a gap between the documented process and the actual one.

Part V · The deliverable

The domain brief, and knowing when you are ready

The brief is what makes this skill assessable instead of a feeling. Two to four pages, written by day 10, circulated to the champion for correction — and that circulation is not a formality. A brief the customer corrects is worth five times one they merely receive, and asking for corrections is itself a credibility move.

Readiness signals — the no-notes test

You are ready when, without looking at anything, you can name:

The 5 workflows

In order, with who does each and roughly how long it takes.

The 10 KPIs

And which two the sponsor is actually measured on (FD4).

The 3 regulations

As design constraints, not as names — what each one forbids you from building.

The 5 systems

What each is the source of truth for, and which one everybody quietly works around.

The top 3 pains

In their words, with a number attached to at least one.

The unusual-statement sense

The final signal — you can hear "we do all our declarations in-house" and think that's unusual at this size, why?

That last one is the real graduation. Recall is memorisation; noticing that something is odd for this industry means you have a model with expectations in it. It is also commercially the most valuable thing on the list — anomalies are where the interesting use cases hide, and asking "that's unusual, why do you do it that way?" is the single most respected question an outsider can ask.

Self-check before claiming 3: in your last engagement, how many corrections did you get in weeks 3–4? If the answer is "none", it is not because you were right — it is because you stopped asking, or people stopped bothering. Corrections in the later weeks are a health signal, not a failure signal.

Hands-on practice

Domain ramp-up is a repeatable procedure, and the only way to trust it is to have run it on an industry you knew nothing about. Click a lab for the full guide.

The full ten-section brief for a real engagement, circulated for correction and filed in your domain library.

In the field

Day 3 of an engagement with a specialty insurer. The kickoff is tomorrow. The engineer has never worked in insurance.

The score-1 version shows up and asks good, careful, generic questions. "So when a claim comes in, what happens?" The claims manager gives the org-chart answer, in the simplified language people use for outsiders, and the meeting produces a requirements list that describes the happy path only.

The score-3 version spent two hours the previous evening on ten job ads for "claims handler" — which listed reserving, subrogation and leakage as core responsibilities — plus one regulator page on claims handling standards, and read twenty real claim files the customer had already shared. In the meeting: "I saw that about a fifth of the files have a reserve revised more than twice. Is that where the leakage sits, or is it more about subrogation opportunities being spotted late?"

The claims manager stopped, sat forward, and talked for twenty minutes about the thing that actually costs the company money — missed subrogation recoveries, because handlers do not have time to read every file closely enough to spot a third party at fault. That became the use case: an AI pass over incoming claim files flagging likely subrogation candidates for a human to review. It was not on anyone's list at the start of the meeting.

Total domain investment: about six hours across two evenings. The term "leakage" alone — used correctly, once — is what opened the conversation. And when the engineer got subrogation slightly wrong ten minutes later, the correction was warm rather than dismissive, because the homework had already been demonstrated.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Faking expertise

Using a term you half-understand to sound credible. One correction from a veteran and everything else you said gets re-examined. "I don't know that term — what does it mean here?" costs nothing and buys respect.

✗ Reading instead of asking

Two weeks of documents feels productive and safe. Thirty minutes with the veteran who knows why the weird rule exists beats all of it — and the documented process is never the real one.

✗ Trusting the AI-generated primer

It gives you the global textbook average with invented specifics attached. Excellent for generating questions, dangerous as a source of facts. Verify before it leaves your notes (FN2).

✗ Wasting the naive-question window

Saving dumb questions for later out of embarrassment. In week one they are charming; in week five they signal you have not been paying attention. Front-load everything.

✗ Learning the process, not the exceptions

The happy path is 60–80% of volume; the remaining 20–40% is where your accuracy number dies. Always ask "what percentage go exactly like that?" and then chase the rest.

✓ Interview the newest person

Whoever joined 3–12 months ago still remembers the confusion the veterans have forgotten. Highest information-per-minute of anyone in the building, and they are usually flattered to be asked.

✓ Read job ads for the role you're automating

A job description is a free, structured list of the tasks, tools and judgment the work requires — written by someone who had to be precise. Ten ads is one hour and worth a textbook.

✓ Bring a wrong diagram

People correct a drawing far more readily than they fill a blank page. Your imperfect value chain is a machine for extracting precision from experts who "don't have time for a long meeting".

Evidence checklist

Resources

  • The Mom Test — Rob Fitzpatrick. Written for discovery, but the discipline of separating what you were told from what you assumed applies directly to glossary building.
  • Ultralearning — Scott Young. Directness and rapid feedback: the theory behind why teach-back beats re-reading, in a form you can apply on day 9.
  • Working Backwards — Bryar & Carr. The narrative discipline that forces you to state a business model plainly enough to be wrong about it.
  • Palantir — A day in the life of an FDE — the embedded model that makes fast domain ramp-up a job requirement rather than a nice-to-have.
  • Internal: read a colleague's domain brief from a different industry before writing your own — then read their corrections.md, which is the more instructive file.
FDE Mastery · FD9 Domain Ramp-up
Lab 1 · 3 days · ~2 h/day
Cold-start sprint on a new domain

🎯 Objective

Compress the protocol into three days on an industry you genuinely do not know, and prove it works by producing a 30-term glossary where every term carries a source and a confirmation status. The point is not the domain — it is trusting the procedure before an engagement depends on it.

📦 Setup — pick one domain, honestly unfamiliar

If you already know one of these, pick a different one. Self-deception here wastes three days.

A) FREIGHT FORWARDING — Vietnamese forwarder, 300 staff, sea + air
   role to study: operations executive / documentation staff
B) INSURANCE CLAIMS — specialty insurer, motor + property claims
   role to study: claims handler
C) CLINIC OPERATIONS — 12-clinic outpatient group
   role to study: front-desk / billing coordinator
D) your next real engagement's industry (best option if you have one)

🔬 Steps

1
DAY 1 · Artifacts first (2 h). Find 10 real documents of the type at the centre of the work — public examples are everywhere: sample Bills of Lading, blank claim forms, published clinic billing codes, regulator templates, real RFP documents. Read them cold and write every unfamiliar term into a raw list. No lookups today.
Output: a raw term list (expect 40–80 entries) and three sentences on what you think this business does. Save that guess — you will grade it on day 3.
2
DAY 1 · The job-ads pass (30 min). Search the role title on job boards and read ten postings. Extract into three columns: tasks · tools/systems · the judgment words ("assess", "determine", "review", "approve"). Those judgment words are where an AI must hand back to a human — mark them. 
| Task (from ad)                  | Tool named   | Judgment word | AI can?
|---------------------------------|--------------|---------------|--------
| "Prepare export documentation"  | TMS, Excel   | prepare       | draft ✔
| "Assess claim validity"         | claims sys   | ASSESS        | assist only
| "Liaise with customs brokers"   | email        | liaise        | draft ✔
3
DAY 2 · Glossary v2 with AI, verification tags mandatory (90 min). Use the Part IV prompt on your document set. Then, for every term, assign a status: [from document] · [inferred] · [unverified]. Add the two columns that make it useful: why it matters to a build and source. 
| Term | Their/standard definition | Why it matters to the build | Source/status
|------|---------------------------|-----------------------------|---------------
|      |                           |                             | 🟡 inferred
Target 30 terms. Fewer than 20 means your document set was too narrow; more than 50 means you are collecting vocabulary instead of understanding.
4
DAY 2 · Primer + diff (60 min). Read one trade-association or textbook "how it works" overview. Then write the diff: what did the primer say that your ten documents did not show, and what appeared in the documents that the primer never mentions? The second list is where reality lives.
5
DAY 3 · Naive-questions list + expert sparring (90 min). Generate the 30 questions with the Part IV prompt, then spar with the simulated expert persona. Every time you are corrected, fix the glossary entry and mark it — corrections from a model are still [unverified], they only tell you where to look. 
Role-play a 15-year {role} at a {company type}. Practical, busy,
uses jargon without explaining it. I've read for 2 days and will now
try to discuss the work with you.
- Correct me briefly whenever I use a term wrongly. Don't be gentle.
- Flag anything you're unsure about as [I'd need to check] rather
  than inventing specifics.
- On "DEBRIEF": list every term I misused, every gap I revealed, and
  the 5 claims I must verify with a real practitioner.
6
DAY 3 · Grade yourself (20 min). Re-read your day-1 three-sentence guess about the business. Write what was wrong with it and why you believed it. Then answer without notes: what are the five workflows, where does the money come from, what is the most expensive mistake someone in this role can make? Gaps here are the agenda for a real human conversation.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "I can't find real documents." Look for regulator templates, published tender documents, sample forms in training material, and industry association examples. Failing that, search the document name plus "sample" or "filled example". Every regulated industry publishes specimens.
  • "The glossary is 80 terms and I understand none of them." You collected an alphabet instead of a language. Keep only the terms that appeared in three or more documents, then explain those to yourself in one sentence each.
  • "The simulated expert agrees with everything." Your persona prompt is too soft. Add impatience, add "correct me the way a busy expert would", and ask it to grade you at the end — evaluation prompts produce far sharper behaviour than conversation prompts.
  • "This feels artificial without a real customer." Correct — that is why Lab 3 and Lab 4 exist. The sprint builds the procedure; humans supply the truth.

✅ Done when

A 30-term glossary exists with source and status on every row, a job-ads task table with the judgment words marked, a written primer-vs-reality diff, and a graded day-1 guess. You can state, without notes, what this business sells and where its money actually comes from.
Lab 2 · ~2 h
Value chain & workflow map

🎯 Objective

Turn vocabulary into a map you can reason about: where money enters, where cost accumulates, where the process waits — and what a use case in each position would be worth. This is the artifact that converts domain knowledge into architecture decisions.

📦 Setup

Use the domain from Lab 1 (or your live engagement). Mermaid, a whiteboard photo or paper — the medium does not matter, the annotations do.

🔬 Steps

1
Draw the money path first (20 min). Not the workflow — the money. Who pays whom, for what, and at which step does the customer's revenue get recognised? Six to nine boxes, left to right, from first contact to cash collected. If you cannot draw this, you do not yet understand the business, and no amount of workflow detail will fix that. 
flowchart LR
  A[Enquiry] --> B[Quote] --> C[Booking] --> D[Documentation]
  D --> E[Customs] --> F[Transport] --> G[Billing & settlement]
2
Annotate each box with four marks (30 min). This is the step that produces the insight — an unannotated chain is a picture, an annotated one is an argument. 
💰 revenue enters here        ⏱ the process WAITS here (and for what)
💸 cost/leakage happens here  ⚖ regulated — human sign-off required

Example (freight):
  Quote      💰 (margin is set here — a bad quote loses money later)
  Booking    ⏱ 90 min/day re-keying · waits on customer email clarity
  Documents  ⚖ legal filings, human sign-off mandatory
  Billing    💸 demurrage disputes, ~2% of revenue leaks here
3
Detail the 5 core workflows (30 min). For each: trigger · steps · who does it · systems touched · how long · what goes wrong. Keep them to five lines each; you are mapping shape, not writing an SOP. 
WORKFLOW 2 — Booking → TMS entry
  trigger:  customer email arrives in shared inbox
  steps:    read email → find/create customer → key 14 fields → attach
            docs → confirm to customer
  who:      ops executive (5 people)
  systems:  Outlook · TMS (SQL) · rate macro (Excel)
  time:     ~6 min/booking, ~140/day
  fails:    ambiguous port names, split shipments, missing weights
4
Place three candidate use cases on the map (20 min) — not one. Position each against the annotations and compare: a use case at a ⏱ step saves time; one at a 💸 step saves money (usually more); one at a ⚖ step needs human sign-off by law, which caps how much it can ever automate. Note which is most visible to the sponsor — visibility and value are different axes, and knowing the gap is a consulting skill (FD1).
5
Derive the design constraints (20 min). For your chosen use case, write what its position on the chain forces onto the architecture: is a human sign-off legally required? Does an upstream system own the identifiers you need? Does a downstream step assume one record per job (and does your input ever produce two)? Take this list straight into FD3 and PR5. 
CONSTRAINTS INHERITED FROM POSITION
1. Upstream: customer emails are free text in 3 languages → extraction
   must handle EN/VI mix; no schema to rely on
2. Downstream: TMS assumes 1 booking = 1 record → split shipments
   (~7%) need explicit handling or they corrupt the record
3. Adjacent: rate macro owns pricing → do NOT compute rates
4. Regulatory: none at this step (documents step ⚖ is next door)
6
Get it corrected (15 min). Show the map to a practitioner if you have one, otherwise to the simulated expert with this prompt — and note that "what's missing" almost always returns a whole step you did not know existed: 
You are a 15-year {role}. Here is an outsider's map of how your
industry's money and work flow. Tell me: what's missing entirely,
what's in the wrong order, what's more important than I've made it
look, and which box hides three steps you'd never combine.
Be blunt. Flag anything you'd need to check rather than guessing.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "My chain has 20 boxes." You drew a process, not a value chain. Collapse to the steps where the commercial state changes — quoted, booked, delivered, invoiced, paid.
  • "I can't tell where the money leaks." Ask the question differently: what do they get penalised for, what do they write off, what do customers dispute? Leakage almost always hides in disputes, rework and penalties.
  • "All three use cases are at the same step." You are looking where the pain was described, not where it is. Force one candidate at a 💸 box and one at a ⏱ box, even if it feels artificial — the comparison is the exercise.

✅ Done when

One page holds an annotated value chain (💰⏱💸⚖ on every relevant box), five workflows in the six-line format, three positioned use-case candidates with a stated pick, and a numbered list of design constraints inherited from position. A practitioner or the expert persona has told you at least one thing that is missing.
Lab 3 · ~1 h + 10 min recording
Teach-back under correction

🎯 Objective

Convert the comfortable feeling of having read a lot into the measurable fact of understanding — by explaining the domain out loud, with no notes, to someone who will interrupt you. Corrections are the output. A smooth teach-back is a failed one.

📦 Setup — pick your audience, in order of value

  1. A domain practitioner (customer contact, champion, a friend in the industry) — highest value by far; their corrections are ground truth.
  2. A colleague outside the engagement — cannot correct facts, but their "wait, why?" finds every hole in your causal chain.
  3. A recording, then the expert persona — weakest, but always available and still catches vagueness.

Ten minutes, timer visible, no notes and no slides. Notes are the failure mode: they let you narrate the structure of your document instead of retrieving a model from memory.

🔬 Steps

1
Brief your audience to interrupt (2 min). Give them this instruction verbatim — politeness ruins the exercise: 
"I'm going to explain {industry} for 10 minutes. Please interrupt
every single time something is wrong, vague, or doesn't follow.
Don't be polite — corrections are the whole point, and a smooth run
means I wasted your time. If you're not sure, say 'I'd check that'."
2
Deliver the five-part structure (10 min, timed). Same order every time so you can compare runs across domains: 
1. WHAT BUSINESS IS THIS      (~90s) what they sell, to whom, why
                                     anyone pays for it
2. HOW MONEY FLOWS            (~2m)  revenue, costs, margin, the
                                     value chain end to end
3. THE FIVE WORKFLOWS         (~3m)  in order, who does each,
                                     roughly how long
4. WHAT GOES WRONG            (~2m)  top 3 pains, the exceptions,
                                     the % that don't follow the path
5. OUR USE CASE + CONSTRAINTS (~90s) where it sits, what it inherits,
                                     what it may not automate
Deliberately go specific enough to be wrong. "Roughly 7% of bookings are split shipments and each one breaks the one-record assumption" is correctable; "there are some edge cases" is not.
3
Log every correction immediately (10 min). Do it while it is fresh, and classify each — the pattern in the classification tells you what kind of learner you are being. 
| # | I said                | Correction              | Type      | Impact on the build
|---|-----------------------|-------------------------|-----------|---------------------
| 1 | "consignee = the      | "Consignee receives;    | TERM      | 2 name fields, never
|   | paying customer"      | payer can be a third    |           | conflate → extraction
|   |                       | party. Notify = 3rd."   |           | schema change
| 2 | "every booking is     | "~7% split into 2+      | EXCEPTION | breaks 1:1 record
|   | one shipment"         | shipments"              |           | assumption → design
| 3 | "customs is just      | "It's a legal filing,   | RULE      | human sign-off is
|   | paperwork"            | we sign, we're liable"  |           | mandatory, not a UX
|   |                       |                         |           | choice
| 4 | "the TMS has the      | "TMS has what we typed. | ASSUMPTION| data quality risk for
|   | shipment data"        | Truth is the email."    |           | evals (AI9)
Types: TERM (vocabulary) · EXCEPTION (the path you did not know) · RULE (a constraint, often regulatory) · ASSUMPTION (something you imported from software thinking).
4
Score the run (5 min). Count corrections by type and read the distribution honestly:
  • 0–4 total — you stayed too abstract, or your audience was being kind. Re-run with more specifics and a blunter brief.
  • 5–15 — healthy. This is what a real day-9 teach-back looks like.
  • 15+ and mostly TERM — vocabulary gap; go back to the glossary and the documents.
  • Mostly EXCEPTION — you learned the happy path only; go find the exception owners.
  • Any RULE correction — highest priority. A missed rule is a design that will be rejected at security review (PR5).
  • Mostly ASSUMPTION — you are modelling their business as a software system. The most common engineer failure, and the hardest to notice alone.
5
Fix and re-teach within 48 hours (10 min). Update the glossary and value chain from the log, then re-deliver parts 3–5 to the same person or the expert persona. The second run is where retention happens; skipping it wastes most of the value of the first. 
DEBRIEF prompt for the persona version:
"Score my explanation 1-5 on: correct vocabulary use · did I capture
the real workflow order · did I know the exceptions · did I identify
the regulatory constraints · would you trust this person to design a
system for your team? Quote the exact sentences that lost points, and
name the one thing I said that a real practitioner would find naive."

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "Nobody interrupted me." Either you were vague or they were being polite. Re-brief with "please stop me the moment something sounds off — I'm being assessed on corrections received."
  • "I ran out of things to say after 4 minutes." That is the finding: your model is thin. Sections 3 and 4 are where it usually collapses — go back to the workflow detail and the exceptions.
  • "I kept reaching for my notes." Do it again tomorrow. Retrieval failure is the signal; the fix is a second attempt, not a better document.
  • "The practitioner corrected me on everything and I felt terrible." That was the best possible outcome, and it happened in a low-stakes room instead of in front of a sponsor. Log it, thank them, come back.

✅ Done when

A classified correction log exists with 5+ entries, the glossary and value chain have been updated from it, and a re-teach of parts 3–5 ran cleaner. You can state which correction type dominated and what that says about how you have been learning this domain.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · 10 days · ~2 h/day
The domain brief

🎯 Objective

Produce the catalog evidence — "domain brief produced in the first two weeks of an engagement" — at a quality where a teammate joining in week three could be productive from it, and where the customer's own people correct details rather than rewriting the whole thing.

📦 Context — what counts

A live engagement is the real thing and should be used if you have one. Otherwise: a realistic engagement with a company you can actually access — a friend's industry, a former client, an internal Golden Owl customer with real documents. The requirement that cannot be simulated is at least two conversations with real practitioners. A brief with no human corrections in it is a research summary, not a domain brief, and it will not be accepted as evidence.

📋 The ten sections

# Domain brief — {industry} ({customer})       day 10 · v1.0 · {you}

## 1. The business in 5 sentences
What they sell · to whom · how they make margin · what makes it hard
· what has changed recently. No jargon in this section — if you can't
write it plainly, you don't have it yet.

## 2. Value chain & where we sit
The annotated chain from Lab 2 (💰 ⏱ 💸 ⚖) + one sentence on why our
use case is positioned where it is.

## 3. The 5 key workflows
Trigger · steps · who · systems · duration · what goes wrong. Mark the
one we are touching.

## 4. The 10 KPIs that matter
Name · how it's calculated HERE (they differ by company) · current
value if known · who is measured on it.       # feeds FD4 directly

## 5. Glossary
30-50 terms, separate file, with source + confirmation status.
State the count confirmed by a human vs still inferred.

## 6. Regulatory frame → design constraints
Each rule translated into what the SYSTEM must do or must not do.
# take this into PR5 before the architecture is settled

## 7. Systems of record
Each system · what it is the source of truth for · who administers it
· and the honest note about which one people work around.

## 8. Top 3 pains, in their words
Verbatim quotes with attribution and a number attached where possible.

## 9. Open questions
Everything still unverified, with who can answer it. This section is a
strength, not a weakness — it is what separates a brief from a bluff.

## 10. Who to ask what
Name · role · what they uniquely know · how responsive they are.

🔬 Steps

1
Days 1–5 — the reading half. Run Part II in order: their artifacts, their public face, the primer and the diff, the value chain (Lab 2), job ads and training material. Keep the glossary open the entire time and tag every entry as you write it. Do not skip the source order — reading the primer first is the mistake that produces a generic brief.
2
Days 6–7 — the human half. Two domain interviews minimum, using the Part III script: the veteran and the recent joiner. Bring your draft glossary and your (wrong) value chain to both. Book them in week one — practitioner calendars are the constraint on this whole protocol, and asking on day 6 for a day-6 meeting fails. 
Meeting request that gets accepted:
"Could I borrow 45 minutes to learn how {workflow} actually works?
Not about the project — I want to understand your world well enough
not to waste your time later. I'll bring what I've understood so far
so you can correct it, which should be faster than explaining from
scratch."
3
Day 8 — regulation and systems. Write section 6 as design constraints and section 7 as sources of truth. Then validate section 6 with the customer's compliance or security contact — this is also your opening move for PR5, and arriving with a draft constraint list rather than questions changes how that conversation goes entirely.
4
Day 9 — teach-back (Lab 3) and repair. Ten minutes, no notes, to a practitioner. Log the corrections, then fix every affected section. Keep the correction log in the brief's appendix — a reviewer reading what you got wrong and fixed learns more about your skill than the polished sections do.
5
Day 10 — write, circulate, ask for corrections. Two to four pages. Send it to your champion with a specific, low-effort ask — vague requests for "feedback" return "looks good": 
"Here's what I've understood about your business after two weeks.
I'm certain parts of it are wrong. Could you skim it and mark the
three things that are most wrong? That'll save us both weeks later.
Section 9 is my list of open questions — if any of those have easy
answers, I'd love them."
Then act on the reply within 24 hours and send v1.1. The speed of that turnaround is itself a credibility event.
6
File it in your library. Add the brief, the glossary, questions-that-worked.md, sources.md and corrections.md to ~/domains/{industry}/. Then spend fifteen minutes on _patterns.md: what was true in this domain that has now been true in every domain you have ramped on? That file is what makes ramp-up six get faster than ramp-up one.
7
The no-notes exam. Have a colleague ask you, cold: name the 5 workflows · the 10 KPIs · the 3 regulations and what each forbids · the 5 systems and what each owns · the top 3 pains. Then the graduation question: "tell me one thing about this customer that is unusual for their industry, and why you think it is." If you cannot answer the last one, you have facts but not a model — go back to the veteran with exactly that question.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "I can't get practitioner time." Ask for 20 minutes, not an hour, and ask through the champion rather than cold. Offer to come to them during a quiet period. Failing that, ask for a recorded screen-share of the workflow — worth more than a meeting and costs them nothing to agree to.
  • "My KPI section is guesses." Ask directly: "what number does your boss ask you about in the monthly review?" One question, and it usually returns the two KPIs that actually matter (FD4).
  • "The customer corrected almost everything." Good — better now than in month two. But check the cause: if the corrections are mostly TERM-type, you leaned on AI-generated definitions without verifying (FN2). Rebuild the glossary from their documents only.
  • "Nobody replied to the brief." Your ask was too big. Re-send with three specific yes/no questions inline. People answer questions; they do not review documents.
  • "Section 9 is embarrassingly long." It should be 5–15 items at day 10. A short open-questions list this early usually means unverified assumptions were quietly promoted to facts — go back through the glossary and count the terms still tagged inferred.

✅ Done when

The brief exists at v1.1+ (i.e. it was corrected by a real person), all ten sections are filled, the glossary shows 30+ terms with a majority confirmed by humans, the correction log is attached, and you passed the no-notes exam including the "what's unusual here" question. Filed in the domain library — at score 3 you will produce one of these in every engagement's first two weeks, and section 6 will land in PR5 and section 4 in FD4 before the architecture is fixed.
