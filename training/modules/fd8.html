Delivery Ownership

Own the engagement from discovery to production, including scope, milestones, risks and adoption. Not project management as ceremony — project management as the thing that stops a good build from dying in week six.

Why it matters for an FDE: The FDE is often the only person on the customer side — self-managing the project is the default, with no PM to lean on.
Part I · Mindset

Nobody is coming to run your project

On a normal product team, someone else holds the plan: a PM writes the roadmap, a scrum master chases blockers, a delivery lead talks to the client. On an FDE engagement, that person is you — usually alongside writing the code. The customer assumes their vendor is managing the work; your own company assumes the embedded engineer knows the status. Between those two assumptions there is exactly one person, and no ceremony to hide behind.

No PM in the room

Small engagements do not fund a delivery manager. The plan, the status, the escalation and the risk log all sit with the engineer.

Scope arrives fuzzy

AI work starts before anyone knows if the model can do the job. You are planning a project whose feasibility is still an open question.

Your critical path is theirs

Data access, VPN accounts, security review, an SME's calendar — the things that decide your dates are owned by people who do not report to you.

Ownership means the outcome, not the task list

A task-owner reports "the extraction pipeline is done, blocked on data access." An outcome-owner reports "we are two weeks from the pilot; data access is the only thing between us and it; here is what I did about it and what I need from you by Thursday." Same facts — completely different job. The line that separates score 1 from score 2 in this skill is whether a blocker that is not yours to fix still shows up in your update with an action attached.

SituationTask owner saysOutcome owner says

Note what the right column always contains: state, impact, action taken, decision needed, date. That five-part shape is the atom of delivery ownership. Everything else in this module — the plan, the weekly cadence, the risk log, the change note — is that atom applied at a different zoom level. The communication craft that carries it lives in FD5; the expectation-setting behind "it moves a week" lives in FD6.

Target is 2/4 — and that is deliberate. You are not being asked to be a programme manager. Score 2 means: you keep a real plan, you send a weekly update nobody has to chase, you keep a risk and decision log current, and you raise problems early with options. A senior helps you with the hard replans and the uncomfortable escalations. That is a completely respectable bar — and most engineers who "just build" never reach it.
Part II · The plan

A delivery plan that fits on one page

If the plan does not fit on one page, nobody reads it, which means it is not a plan — it is documentation of your anxiety. One page, five blocks: phases with exit criteria · milestones tied to customer value · dependencies with owners and dates · the critical path · what is out of scope. It is written in week one, revised whenever reality moves, and shown at every steering meeting.

Phases end on evidence, not on effort

A phase without an exit criterion never ends — it just becomes "still working on discovery" in week five. Borrow the phase spine from your architecture work (FD3) and give every phase a falsifiable exit: something that exists, or was measured, or was signed.

PhaseTypical lengthExit criterion (falsifiable)
0 · Discovery1–2 wksProblem statement signed by the sponsor; data sample opened; success metric agreed (FD1, FD4)
1 · Feasibility spike3–5 days20 real cases run end-to-end; measured accuracy on a golden set (AI9); go / no-go written down
2 · Prototype2–3 wksDemo run by a customer user, not by you (FD2); top-3 gaps listed
3 · Pilot build3–6 wksDeployed to the real environment with monitoring and a rollback (PR9, PR11); security review passed (PR5)
4 · Pilot run2–4 wksN real users × N weeks of real usage; baseline vs target measured; incident log empty or explained
5 · Scale & handover1–3 wksRunbook + owner named; adoption ≥ target; value story written (FD10)
The exit-criteria test: read each criterion and ask "could two people disagree about whether this happened?" If yes, rewrite it. "Prototype working" fails. "Three ops users each processed five real bookings through the prototype unassisted" passes.

Milestones name customer value, not your tasks

"Integration layer complete" is an internal task that means nothing to a sponsor and cannot be celebrated. "Ops team processes their first real booking through the assistant" is a milestone: it is visible, it is dated, and when it happens everyone in the customer org knows the project is alive.

✗ Task milestone✓ Value milestone
"Extraction pipeline built""20 of yesterday's real emails extracted correctly, reviewed by the ops lead"
"API integration done""First AI-drafted booking lands in the TMS and is confirmed by an operator"
"Eval harness set up""We can state accuracy with a number the customer trusts — 94% on 200 held-out cases"
"Deployed to prod""5 operators using it daily for a full week without falling back to manual"

The critical path always runs through other people

On AI engagements, two items are on the critical path in nearly every project, and neither is code: data access (PR8) and the security review (PR5). Both are owned by people outside your team, both have queue times measured in weeks, and both are usually started far too late because they feel like paperwork rather than engineering.

Dependencies: owner, date, and what happens if it slips

A dependency without a named human and a date is a wish. Keep this table in the plan and read it aloud at every steering meeting — reading a person's name next to a date in front of their boss is the most polite escalation mechanism ever invented.

The "if it slips" column is the whole point. Without it, a dependency table is a to-do list someone else ignores. With it, every row states a consequence in the customer's own currency — and the conversation stops being "please hurry" and becomes "which of these do you want to protect?"
Part III · Running the week

A cadence light enough that you actually keep it

Ceremony collapses under solo delivery — you will not run a stand-up with yourself. What survives is a three-beat week: Monday plan · mid-week unblock · Friday update. Ninety minutes total, every week, non-negotiable. It is the smallest structure that keeps a project honest.

Monday: three outcomes, not twenty tasks

Open the plan. Ask: what must be true by Friday for the next milestone to stay on its date? Write three outcomes — no more; three is what a week actually holds once meetings and surprises take their cut. Then, the step people skip: book the humans. Every outcome that needs someone else's time gets a calendar invite on Monday morning, not a Slack message on Thursday. 

# Week of Aug 11 — Monday plan (10 min to write)
OUTCOMES (must be true by Fri)
  1. Extraction accuracy measured on the 200-case golden set   # milestone M2
  2. Security questionnaire submitted (not "drafted")          # critical path
  3. Ops lead has run the prototype herself, once              # adoption signal
PEOPLE I NEED (booked today)
  - Duc/InfoSec 30 min Tue — walk the questionnaire together
  - Linh/Ops 45 min Thu — prototype session, her laptop, her data
RISKS I'M WATCHING
  - R2 data access (13 days open) → escalate Wed if no movement
NOT DOING THIS WEEK (say it out loud)
  - UI polish · the second use case · the Slack integration idea

The "not doing" line is a discipline, not a courtesy. Naming what you are consciously deferring is what makes the three outcomes real, and it gives you the sentence you need when someone asks on Wednesday why the Slack integration hasn't started.

Wednesday: the unblock sweep

Twenty minutes, one rule: every dependency that is not green gets an action today. Not a status check — an action. The escalation ladder, one rung per sweep, never skipping rungs:

RUNG 1 — Direct, specific, easy: "Nam, the read-only access on TMS_PROD — is there a form I should fill in, or a person I should ask? Happy to draft it." Make saying yes cheaper than saying nothing.
RUNG 2 — Offer a smaller version: "If full access needs a review, can I get a 500-row export today so the build continues?" Partial unblocks keep momentum and prove you are not being precious.
RUNG 3 — Route around: mock the interface, generate synthetic data, reorder the plan so the blocked work is not on this week's path. Then say so publicly — invisible workarounds get read as "it wasn't urgent after all".
RUNG 4 — Champion escalation (FD7): bring your champion the consequence, not the complaint. "Access has been open 13 days; the pilot date moves next Monday if it isn't resolved. Who should I be talking to?" Never escalate over someone's head without telling them first.
RUNG 5 — Written in the update: the blocker, its age, its consequence and the decision needed appear in Friday's update, in front of the sponsor. Nothing dramatic, just visible — visibility is the escalation.

Friday: the update that stops people chasing you

Same shape every week, five minutes to read, sent before you log off — the format lives in FD5, the discipline lives here. A weekly update that arrives without being asked for is the single cheapest trust-building act available to an FDE, and the first thing that disappears when a project is going badly — which is exactly when it matters most. 

Subject: [Booking Assistant] Week 3 update — on track, 1 decision needed

STATUS: 🟡 on track, one risk                       # 🟢 / 🟡 / 🔴, honestly
NEXT MILESTONE: first real booking processed — Aug 22 (unchanged)

  • Accuracy measured on 200 real cases: 91% field-level (target 95%)
  • Security questionnaire submitted; InfoSec call booked Aug 14
  • Linh ran the prototype herself — 5 bookings, 2 confusions (logged)

  • Close the 4% accuracy gap on the address field (root cause known)
  • Pilot environment + 5 test accounts stood up

RISKS / BLOCKERS

    on mock schema from Mon → ~3 days rework if the real schema differs.

    (ships Aug 22) or add ~1 week for a lookup integration (Aug 29)?

One decision per update, maximum. Ask for five decisions and you get zero. Ask for one, with a recommendation attached, and you almost always get it — plus a reputation for being easy to work with.

Tracking without ceremony: one living document

Resist the urge to install Jira for a three-person engagement. One shared document (or one board with three columns) that the customer can open is worth more than a tool nobody logs into. It holds: the one-page plan · the dependency table · the risk register · the decision log · this week's outcomes. Shared, not emailed — a doc the customer can read at 11pm is a doc that removes a status meeting.

The decision log

Engagements do not fail because a decision was wrong; they fail because nobody remembers making it. Four months later "why doesn't it handle phone bookings?" becomes an accusation instead of a reference to a line you both agreed to in week two. One line per decision, written the day it happens, in the shared doc.

The risk register — five columns, no theatre

A risk is not a worry; it is a named future event with an owner and a response. Keep 5–10 live risks. More than ten and nobody reads it; fewer than five and you are not looking. Score P × I on 1–5 each, sort by the product, and act on the top three.

Avoid

Change the plan so the risk cannot happen — pre-review the architecture with InfoSec before building it.

Mitigate

Reduce P or I — build on a mock schema so a late access grant costs days, not weeks.

Transfer

Give it to whoever can actually control it — the customer owns their own access approvals, in writing.

Accept

Say so explicitly and monitor. An accepted risk that was named is a risk; an unnamed one is a surprise.

Scope creep: the change note

Scope creep is rarely a villain — it is a hundred small yeses. "Could it also handle attachments?" "Can we add a dashboard?" Each is reasonable; together they eat the pilot date and nobody can point to the moment it happened. The fix is not saying no. The fix is making the cost visible at the moment of asking, in four lines you can write in ninety seconds: 

CHANGE NOTE CN-004                                        2026-08-14
WHAT:     Also extract PDF attachments (rate sheets) from booking emails
WHY:      ~15% of bookings attach the rates as PDF; ops re-keys them today
COST:     ~4 dev days + 1 day eval → pilot date Aug 22 → Aug 29
          (or: drop the reporting screen from pilot scope, date holds)
DECISION: [ ] add & move date  [ ] swap for reporting screen  [ ] defer to phase 2
          Decided by: ____________  on: ________

Three properties make this work. It never says no — it presents a trade. It puts the decision in the customer's hands, which is where authority already sits. And it creates a paper trail, so the date moving is a decision they made, not a failure you had. Send it same-day; a change note written a week later reads as an excuse.

The silent killer: changes absorbed without a note. You add the attachment parser over a weekend because it is "only four days" and you want to be helpful. The date slips, nobody knows why, and your reputation absorbs a cost the customer chose. Helpfulness that hides its price is not helpfulness.
Part IV · Ambiguity

Planning a project whose feasibility is unknown

Standard project management assumes the work is knowable and the only question is sequencing. AI delivery breaks that assumption in week one: you cannot estimate "make extraction accurate enough" because nobody knows if it is possible until you try. The answer is not to stop planning — it is to plan at the resolution the knowledge supports.

Rolling-wave planning

Plan the next two weeks in days, the next two months in phases, and the rest in intent. Re-cut the wave every Friday. This is not vagueness — it is stating precision honestly. A plan that claims day-level detail for week nine is lying, and everyone senior enough to fund it knows.

Timeboxes beat estimates for unknowns

"How long to get accuracy to 95%?" has no honest answer. Replace the estimate with a timebox and a decision point: "Five days on retrieval quality. On Friday we look at the number: if it's above 93% we push to 95%; if it's below 90% we change the design — narrower scope or a human-review step." You have now converted an unbounded risk into a bounded cost with a scheduled decision, which is the only form of estimate that survives contact with a probabilistic system.

Kind of workPlan it asBecause
Known engineering (CRUD, integration, deploy)Estimate in daysYou have done it before; variance is modest

Spike, then commit

The pattern that lets you give real dates in an unreal situation: refuse to commit to the phase you cannot size, commit hard to the spike that will let you size it. "I can't give you a pilot date today. I can give you one on the 15th, after a five-day feasibility spike on 20 of your real documents — and the spike itself is a date I'll hold." Customers accept this far more often than engineers expect, because it trades a fake certainty for a real one.

Replanning honestly when reality moves

Every engagement replans. The difference between a professional and a disaster is when and how. The rule: the day you know, they know — a slip announced three weeks early is management; the same slip announced two days before the date is a broken promise (FD6).

1 · NAME IT — "The Aug 22 pilot date is at risk." Lead with the headline; do not bury it in paragraph four.
2 · CAUSE — one honest sentence, no blame, no drama. "Access took 3 weeks instead of the 1 we assumed."
3 · OPTIONS — always two or three, with real costs: ship narrower on the date · full scope two weeks later · add a person (and what that costs in ramp-up).
4 · RECOMMEND — you have the most information in the room; say what you would do and why. Silence here reads as either helplessness or hidden preference.
5 · LOG IT — the decision goes into the log, the plan gets re-cut the same day, and the new date is the one you now defend absolutely.
Never replan twice for the same reason. A second slip caused by the thing that caused the first one destroys credibility permanently, because it proves the first replan was not honest. When you re-cut a date, re-cut it with the pessimistic number, not the hopeful one.
Part V · Adoption

Deployed is not done — used is done

The most common way an FDE engagement fails is not a failed build. It is a working system that three people tried once. The technology worked; the delivery did not, because "done" was defined as deployment. Ownership extends past the deploy button to the moment the workflow has actually changed.

The adoption plan — three named things

  1. Training that is not a demo. A demo is you driving; training is them driving, on their own data, with you silent. Two 45-minute hands-on sessions beat one 2-hour presentation. Record one for the people who join later, and leave a one-page cheat sheet (FD10).
  2. Two named champions, not "the team". Pick the respected user and the curious user. Give them early access, ask their opinion on a real design choice, and credit them publicly. Peer adoption runs on social proof — a colleague saying "it saved me an hour" outperforms any exec mandate.
  3. A feedback loop with a visible response. A channel, a form, or a weekly 15-minute drop-in — plus the part everyone skips: showing what changed because of the feedback. "Three of you asked for the date format fix; it shipped Tuesday" converts users into co-owners faster than any feature.

A definition of done that includes usage

# Definition of done — pilot phase (agreed with sponsor in week 1)
[ ] Deployed to the customer's environment with monitoring + rollback   # PR9 / PR11
[ ] Security review passed and documented                               # PR5
[ ] Accuracy measured on a held-out set, number shared and accepted     # AI9
[ ] 5 named users trained, each has completed 5 real cases unassisted
[ ] >= 60% of eligible volume ran through the system for 2 consecutive weeks
[ ] Baseline vs post metric captured (time/case, error rate)            # FD4
[ ] Runbook + named internal owner for support and prompt changes       # FD10
[ ] Open issues triaged: fix now / phase 2 / won't do — written down

Agree this list in week one, not week eight. A DoD negotiated at the end is a negotiation; agreed at the start it is a contract that protects both sides — it stops the customer expanding "done" and stops you declaring victory early.

Closing the engagement well

The last two weeks decide whether there is a next engagement. Three artifacts, each with a name attached:

Handover pack

Runbook, architecture note, prompt-change procedure, monitoring dashboard, escalation path — and a named internal owner who has actually used them once while you watched (FD10).

Value story

Baseline → result, in the sponsor's numbers, with the method stated so it survives scrutiny. This is what gets quoted in their budget conversation (FD4).

What's next memo

Two or three follow-on opportunities you saw while embedded, sized roughly. Written for the sponsor, not for sales — the credibility of the first win is what funds the second.

Then hold a 30-minute retrospective with the customer team: what worked, what we would do differently, what surprised us. Ask them to speak first. It costs half an hour, it is the fastest way to learn as an FDE, and it ends the engagement with the customer feeling like a partner rather than a project.

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

No exposure. Works from tickets. Status is "still working on it"; blockers surface when someone asks.
1
Awareness. Keeps a personal task list and reports progress when prompted. Knows what a milestone and a risk register are; has not run one alone.

Leads & coaches. Runs multi-workstream or multi-team engagements, sets delivery standards other FDEs reuse, rescues troubled projects, and shapes commercial terms from delivery reality (FD11).
Self-check before claiming 2: open your last engagement's folder. Is there a plan a stranger could read in two minutes? Are there three consecutive weekly updates? Is there a written record of the last scope change and who approved it? Three yeses is a 2. Any no is a 1 with good intentions.

In the field

Week 4 of an eight-week pilot. The build is going well. The DBA who owns the production database has been "getting to it" for eleven days.

The score-1 engineer keeps building on mock data and mentions the blocker in the Friday update as a bullet: "still waiting on DB access." Week 6: still waiting. Week 7: the sponsor asks why the pilot has not started, hears about the access issue for what feels like the first time, and the project's story becomes "the vendor was blocked and did not tell us."

The score-2 FDE does something small and unglamorous in week 4. The blocker moves to the top of the update with its age in days, the consequence spelled out ("pilot start moves from Aug 22 to Aug 29 if this is not resolved by Friday"), and one decision request. In parallel: a mock schema so the build continues, an offer to accept a 500-row export as an interim, and a quiet message to the champion — "not a complaint, just want you to see the date impact."

Access was granted the following Tuesday. Not because the FDE was more persistent, but because for the first time the delay had a price attached that someone with authority could see. The pilot shipped on the original date. And in the closing retrospective, the sponsor's comment was not about the model — it was: "we always knew where the project stood."

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Silent absorption of scope

Saying yes to every small request and eating the cost in evenings. The date slips, nobody knows why, and you carry a failure the customer would gladly have decided against. Write the change note.

✗ Optimistic status until the wall

Green, green, green, disaster. Any status that has never been yellow is not a status, it is a hope. A slip flagged three weeks out is management; two days out it is a broken promise.

✗ Treating access & security as paperwork

They are on the critical path of almost every AI engagement and they run on someone else's queue. Start them on day one, before the architecture is even settled.

✗ Heavy process nobody sustains

A Jira board, a burndown chart and a RACI matrix for a three-person engagement — abandoned by week three, leaving worse visibility than a shared doc that gets updated.

✗ Declaring done at deploy

Shipping to production and moving on, then discovering at renewal that usage is 8%. Usage belongs in the definition of done, agreed in week one.

✓ Never miss a Friday

The weekly update is the cheapest trust you will ever buy — and the first thing that vanishes when a project goes badly, which is exactly when sending it is worth the most.

✓ One decision per update

Always with your recommendation attached. Five asks return zero answers; one ask with a recommendation returns a decision and a reputation for being easy to work with.

✓ Write the decision the day it happens

Sixty seconds in the log now saves an hour of defensive archaeology in month four — and turns "you forgot phone bookings" into "we deferred it, here's when we agreed to revisit."

Lab 2 · ~2 h · simulated week
Run a week in 90 minutes

🎯 Objective

Compress a delivery week into 90 minutes and survive two realistic surprises, producing the three artifacts of the cadence: a Monday plan, a change note, and a Friday update that a sponsor rates as clear.

🔬 Steps

1

Lab 3 · ~1.5 h
Risk & decision log drill

🎯 Objective

Build both logs from scratch and — the part that actually matters — keep them alive through two changes without losing traceability. Most engineers can write a risk register once; almost none can show you last month's version and what changed.

📦 Setup

Use the VietFreight scenario (Lab 1) or your live engagement. Two tables in one shared doc. Timestamp every edit — the drill is worthless without the audit trail.

🔬 Steps

1
Seed 8 risks. Force at least one from each category, which is how you find the ones you are blind to: technical (accuracy plateau, latency, the undocumented Excel macro) · data (quality, volume, history, PII) · people (SME availability, operator resistance, champion leaves) · organisational (sponsor changes, budget freeze, competing priority) · external (vendor/API change, regulation, holiday shutdown).
Each must be a future event with a consequence, written as "X happens → Y results". "Data quality" is not a risk. "Address fields are free-text in 40% of records → extraction accuracy caps around 88% and the value story weakens" is.
2
Score and respond. P and I on 1–5, sort by P×I, and write a response for the top five using Avoid / Mitigate / Transfer / Accept. Two quality rules: a response containing the words "monitor closely" is not a response unless it names what you will look at and when; and every response has a human owner, which for at least half of them should not be you. 
| ID | Risk (event → consequence) | P | I | P×I | Response | Owner | Status
|----|----------------------------|---|---|-----|----------|-------|--------
3
Seed 8 decisions that this project has already implicitly made — the ones nobody wrote down. Channel scope (email only?), human-in-the-loop or not, where the model runs, what accuracy bar counts as success, which fields are in scope, what happens on low confidence, who can change prompts after handover, what "pilot" means in headcount. Use the Part III columns including Revisit.
Notice how many of these you were carrying only in your head. That number is the real output of this step.
4
CHANGE 1 — the good news that breaks things. InfoSec approves the Claude API via the customer's VPC with no data retention, faster than expected. But the approval is conditional: no PII in prompts, and customer names count as PII.
Update both logs: which risk closes (with date and evidence), which decision is superseded (D3 → D3a, never overwrite), and what new risk this creates — redaction adds a pipeline step, and redacting names may hurt extraction accuracy. Show the supersede, do not silently edit.
5
CHANGE 2 — the bad news that changes the shape. Week 6: the operators tell Trang, not you, that they do not trust the confidence scores and are re-checking every field manually — so the time saving is currently zero, even though accuracy is 93%.
This is an adoption failure wearing a technical costume. Update the logs: the risk you scored at P=3 (operator resistance) has materialised — record it as materialised with the date, not deleted. Add the decisions it forces: show the confidence score differently, or hide it and mark only low-confidence fields; run a side-by-side session with two operators; redefine the pilot success metric to include a trust question. Then write the two-line entry that will appear in Friday's update.
6
The archaeology test. Hand both logs to a colleague with one question: "It's November. The customer asks why the system doesn't handle phone bookings and why we show a confidence score at all. Answer them using only these logs." If they can answer both in under two minutes, the logs work. If they have to guess or ask you, find the missing column — it is almost always why or who decided.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • All 8 risks are technical. Predictable engineer failure mode — and people/organisational risks are what actually kill engagements. Re-do step 1 with the category quota enforced.
  • Every risk scores 4×5. You are worrying, not scoring. If everything is top priority, nothing is; force a spread and defend the low scores out loud.
  • You edited a decision in place. Restore it and supersede instead. The log's value is the history — an overwritten decision is a lost argument you will have to have again.
  • The logs took an hour to update. They are too detailed to survive a real week. Target: 10 minutes every Friday. Cut columns until that is true.

✅ Done when

Both logs exist with 8+ entries each, show at least one closed risk, one materialised risk and one superseded decision — all with dates — and a colleague passed the archaeology test in under two minutes.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · 3 weeks, ~30 min/wk
The delivery pack

🎯 Objective

Assemble the catalog evidence — "delivery plan, weekly updates and risk/decision log" — from three real weeks. Simulation builds the reflex; only real weeks prove you kept it when the work got busy, which is the entire skill.

📦 What counts as real

In priority order: a live customer engagement · an internal Golden Owl project with at least two other people and a real deadline · a cross-team initiative you are driving. What does not count: a solo side project (no dependencies, so no ownership to demonstrate) or three weeks of the VietFreight simulation. If you have no live engagement, take ownership of an internal project for three weeks — this is a standing, useful offer to make.

📋 Component 1 — The delivery plan

One page, from Lab 1, for the real project. Must contain all five blocks. Dated, and visibly revised at least once during the three weeks — a plan that never changed in three weeks was never used.

# Delivery plan — {project}                     v3 · updated 2026-08-22
## Objective (1 sentence, in the customer's words)
Cut booking-entry time from 90 to under 30 min/person/day by Q4.
## Phases & exit criteria
| Phase | Dates | Exit criterion (falsifiable) | Status |
## Milestones (customer-visible, dated)
| M1 | Aug 08 | 20 real emails extracted, reviewed by Trang     | ✅ |
| M2 | Aug 22 | Trang processes her first real booking end-to-end| 🟡 |
| M3 | Sep 12 | 5 operators, 2 weeks, >=60% of volume           | ⬜ |
## Dependencies (owner · needed by · if it slips)
## Critical path
data access → InfoSec sign-off → pilot build → pilot run → board demo
## Out of scope (phase 1)
phone bookings · auto-post without review · dashboard · non-EN emails

📋 Component 2 — Three consecutive weekly updates

Actually sent, to actual recipients, in three consecutive weeks. Consecutive is the requirement that has teeth: anyone can write one good update. Same structure each time so a reader learns where to look — and at least one of the three must contain something you would rather not have written. Three consecutive green updates with no risks and no decisions means you are reporting, not owning.

Subject: [{project}] Week {n} update — {status in 5 words}

STATUS: 🟢/🟡/🔴 {one line, honest}
NEXT MILESTONE: {name} — {date} ({unchanged | moved from ___, why})
DONE THIS WEEK
  • {outcome, not activity — what is now true that wasn't}
NEXT WEEK
  • {the 3 outcomes from your Monday plan}
RISKS / BLOCKERS
  • {risk} — open {n} days, owner {name}, consequence {date/scope}
DECISION NEEDED FROM YOU
  • {one decision} — my recommendation: {x}, because {y}

📋 Component 3 — The maintained logs

Risk register and decision log in the shared doc, with edit timestamps across all three weeks. Evidence of maintenance is what is being assessed, so the pack must show: at least one risk closed or materialised with a date, at least one new risk added after week 1, at least three decisions logged with who decided and why, and at least one superseded decision if anything changed direction.

🔬 Steps

1
Week 0 — set up in 45 minutes. Create the shared doc with all four sections (plan, dependencies, risks, decisions). Share it with the customer or team on day one; a private tracker is a personal habit, not delivery ownership. Put three recurring calendar blocks in place now: Monday 30 min, Wednesday 20 min, Friday 40 min. If they are not in the calendar, they will not happen in week 3 — which is exactly the week that counts.
2
Weeks 1–3 — run the cadence. Monday plan, Wednesday sweep, Friday update and log maintenance. Keep every artifact, including the ugly ones. The pack is stronger with a week where you had to move a date than with three perfect weeks — assessors read the recovery, not the perfection.
3
Capture one change note. Over three weeks something will change: a request, a discovery, a slip. Write the note the same day, get the decision recorded, and include it in the pack. If genuinely nothing changed in three weeks, say so explicitly in the reflection — and be suspicious, because usually it means a change was absorbed silently.
4
The stranger test. Give the whole pack to a colleague who knows nothing about the project. Five questions, timed at five minutes total: 
1. What is being built, and for whom?
2. What is the next visible result, and when?
3. What is most likely to go wrong, and who owns it?
4. What was decided that someone might question later — and why?
5. Is this project healthy? How can you tell?
Any question they cannot answer marks the artifact to fix. Question 5 is the real exam: a pack that cannot be assessed for health is a filing cabinet, not a delivery system.
5
Reflection (half a page, in the pack). Answer four things honestly: which cadence beat you skipped and why · which risk you saw coming and under-scored anyway · which change you almost absorbed silently · and what you will change in the next engagement. Written self-criticism is the fastest route from score 2 to score 3, and it is the section a reviewer reads first.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "Week 3's update never got sent." The single most common failure, and it is the point of the lab. Restart the three weeks — the evidence is consecutiveness, because that is what customers actually experience.
  • "There's no one to send updates to." There always is: your manager, the internal stakeholder, the team lead who inherits the work. Pick a real reader with real interest; writing to nobody produces artifacts nobody would accept.
  • "The logs are empty — nothing went wrong." Then the risks were never identified. Look harder at people and organisational risk: who is unavailable next month, who has not seen the thing yet, whose priorities are shifting.
  • "The customer never reads the updates." Send them anyway, and check the shape: are you leading with status and a decision, or with activity? Then ask once, directly: "is this format useful, or would you rather have five bullets?" The question itself demonstrates ownership.

✅ Done when

The pack contains a revised one-page plan, three consecutive real weekly updates, logs with dated maintenance across all three weeks, one change note and a written reflection — and a stranger answered all five questions, including whether the project is healthy. File it as your template; at score 2 this pack should take under 30 minutes a week to keep alive, and it becomes the backbone of every engagement you own afterwards.
