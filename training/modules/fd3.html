AI Solution Architecture

Translate business needs into secure, scalable and vendor-neutral technical architecture — a picture the customer's architect can review, a decisions log their security lead can audit, and a plan your delivery team can build against.

Why it matters for an FDE: The FDE is the one who draws the path from demo to real system and commits to a timeline.
Part I · What architecture means here

One page, plus a decisions log

Enterprise architecture, in the classical sense, produces a sixty-page document that nobody reads and that is stale before the first sprint ends. That is not what an FDE ships. Your architecture deliverable is two artifacts, and both are short:

The one-page picture

Every layer of the system, every external system it touches, every trust boundary it crosses — on a single sheet somebody can read in ninety seconds and argue with.

The decisions log

Five to eight ADRs: what we chose, what we rejected, why, and what it will cost us to reverse. The picture shows what; the log defends why.

The test for both is the same: a customer's architect, their security lead and you-in-six-months can each act on it without asking you a question. If the architect can't tell where the data crosses the network boundary, or security can't tell which component holds PII, or future-you can't remember why the vector store isn't the obvious one — the artifact failed, however beautiful it looked.

Architecture is the bridge from prototype to production

A prototype (FD2) proves the idea can work on a good day, with your API key, your laptop, ten cherry-picked documents and you standing next to it. A production system must work on a bad day, with the customer's identity provider, their data residency rules, their 4 a.m. traffic spike, and nobody watching. Architecture is the explicit, reviewable transformation between the two — and the moment where most AI engagements quietly die, because nobody owns the transformation.

The FDE's dual constraint

A staff engineer at a product company optimizes for one thing: the technically best system for a codebase they control. An FDE has two masters, and the second one is the hard one:

ConstraintWhat it asksHow it fails when ignored
Technically rightMeets the accuracy bar, the latency budget, the cost ceiling; observable; recoverable; not a security hole.A system that ships and then embarrasses everyone in month three.
Buildable hereRuns inside the customer's cloud/region, uses their identity provider, survives their change-approval board, fits their team's skills after you leave.A beautiful design that never gets deployed, because approval never came.
The FDE test for any design choice: "Can this be running in their environment, approved, in the number of weeks I just promised?" A design that fails this test is not a good design being blocked by politics — it is a bad design, because buildability is a real requirement, not an inconvenience. Politics, procurement and the change-advisory board are load-bearing walls (FD7).

Architecture in an FDE engagement typically starts the week the prototype lands well, runs 1–2 weeks with the customer's architect in the room, and produces the package you defend in a review. The discovery constraints (FD1) and the success metrics (FD4) are your inputs; the phased plan and its timeline are your outputs.

Now the useful part. For every layer there is a decision you must make, a default you should start from, and the constraint that overrides the default. Defaults are not laziness — they are how you move fast and stay defensible. The override column is where the engagement's real architecture lives.

LayerThe decisionDefaultWhat overrides it
① Surfaces Where does the output land in the user's day? Embed inside the tool they already use; no new app to log into. The host tool has no extension point, or IT forbids add-ins → thin web app with SSO. Adoption risk is the real driver (FD10).
② Gateway Who is the caller, which tenant, what may they see? Customer's existing IdP via OIDC; identity carried down to retrieval (PR13). Legacy on-prem auth, service accounts only, or a mandated API gateway product → adapt, but never lose per-user identity at the retrieval layer.
③ Orchestration Fixed pipeline or agent loop? Deterministic pipeline with LLM steps (AI1); use an agent loop only where the path genuinely varies (AI6). Truly open-ended tasks, or a variable number of tool hops → agent, with a step budget, a cost cap and a stop condition. Auditability requirements push back towards fixed pipelines.
④ Model layer Which models, hosted where, with what fallback? Two-tier routing: claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 for classify/extract, claude-sonnet-5 for reasoning; provider fallback behind one adapter (PR2). Residency or procurement mandates a specific cloud → same models via that platform (PR6). Air-gapped → open-weights (AI12) with a re-baselined accuracy bar.
⑤ Knowledge How does customer knowledge reach the model? Hybrid retrieval + rerank over a chunked, ACL-tagged index (AI4, AI5); incremental sync from source (FN5). Small, stable corpus → put it in context, skip the index. Highly structured data → SQL over vectors. Frozen jargon/format → consider adaptation (AI10) after retrieval is exhausted.
⑥ Tools How does the system act on other systems? Narrow, schema-typed tools behind an MCP-style boundary (AI8); writes idempotent and reversible (PR8). No API on the target system → nightly export/import, or RPA as a last resort with an explicit deprecation date. Any irreversible write → human approval gate, non-negotiable.
⑦ Async Sync request or background job? Anything over ~10 s or with multiple model calls goes to a queue with a status resource (PR3). Interactive chat needs streaming, not queueing. Bulk backfills need batch windows and rate-limit budgeting, not the interactive path.
⑧ State What is stored, where, for how long? Managed Postgres for runs/threads/feedback; object store for files; retention policy written down on day one. Residency, PII or retention rules (PR5) → in-region storage, field-level encryption, shorter retention, or no prompt logging at all.
⑨ Obs & evals How do we know it works — today and after every change? Trace every run with cost/latency/tokens (PR1); golden set + regression gate in CI (AI9). Sensitive content → trace metadata only, redact payloads. Never override this layer away: a design without evals is a demo with a longer runway.
⓪ Guardrails What must never happen, and what enforces it? Output schema validation, PII redaction, tool allow-lists, approval gates on writes (PR4). Regulated domains add review logging, refusal policies and red-team evidence. Guardrails are cross-cutting: they belong at the gateway, the orchestrator and the tool layer — not in one box.
The two layers juniors forget, and seniors never do: ⑨ observability/evals and ⓪ guardrails. They are invisible in a demo and decisive in production. If your architecture diagram has no eval loop and no guardrail boundary, a customer architect will spot it in the first two minutes of review — and will be right to distrust everything else on the page.

Trust boundaries: the version security actually reads

Draw the same picture a second time with only the boundaries marked, and you have the artifact the security lead cares about (PR5). Three questions answer 80% of a security review:

  • Where does customer data leave their perimeter? Name the exact hop, the region, the encryption, and whether it is retained by the receiving service.
  • Where is identity enforced? If authorisation happens only at the UI and the retriever queries an unfiltered index, you have built a data-leak machine with a chat interface.
  • What can the system do without a human? Every autonomous write is a boundary. List them; propose approval gates for the ones with consequences.
Part III · The decision method

Find the 5–8 decisions that matter; everything else is detail

A system has hundreds of choices. Only a handful are architectural — meaning: expensive to reverse, or constraining to everything downstream. Your job is to find those, decide them explicitly, and let the team make the rest. Two filters identify them:

Filter 1 · Cost of reversal

If changing it later costs more than a week — or requires re-approval from security, procurement or the customer's architecture board — it is architectural.

Filter 2 · Blast radius

If it constrains other components' options — auth model, tenancy model, sync vs async, data location — it is architectural even when it looks small.

For a typical enterprise AI engagement, the list lands almost every time on: model + routing, knowledge/retrieval store, integration pattern, auth & tenancy, sync vs async execution, eval & observability, plus one or two engagement-specific ones (residency, an unavoidable legacy system, an offline mode). That is your ADR set — and Lab 2.

Quality attributes: the six numbers that force every decision

Architecture without numbers is taste. Extract these six from the customer before designing — each one, once quantified, eliminates options for you. Ask for a number, and if you get an adjective, keep asking ("fast" is not a latency budget; "p95 under 4 seconds" is).

AttributeThe question that gets a numberThe decision it forces
Accuracy bar"What score on which measure makes this shippable — and who signs that off?"Model tier, retrieval depth, rerank yes/no, and how much human review stays in the loop (AI9).
Latency budget"At p95, what wait makes a user abandon the task?"Sync vs async, streaming, agent step budget, caching, model size (PR2, PR3).
Cost ceiling"What can this cost per document / per user / per month before it stops being worth it?"Routing strategy, prompt caching, context size, whether an agent loop is affordable at all (FD4).
Privacy & residency"Which data classes are involved, and where may they be processed and stored?"Cloud + region, provider, logging policy, redaction, sometimes the whole hosting model (PR5, PR6).
Availability"What happens to the business in the hour this is down? Is there a manual fallback?"Fallback provider, queue-and-retry, degraded mode, multi-region yes/no (PR3, PR11).
Auditability"If a regulator or a customer asks why the system said this, six months later — what must we show?"Trace retention, prompt/version pinning, citation requirements, deterministic pipelines over free-running agents (PR1).
Write them as a single line at the top of the architecture page. For example: "≥92% field accuracy on the 200-doc holdout · p95 ≤ 6 s · ≤ $0.04/document · EU-only processing, no prompt retention · 99.5% with manual fallback · 12-month trace retention." Every reviewer now argues about the right thing — the numbers — instead of about box shapes.

Framing trade-offs: one-way and two-way doors

Not all decisions deserve the same deliberation. Classify each one by the cost of reversal, then spend your scarce customer meeting-time proportionally. Deciding a two-way door slowly is how engagements lose their first month; deciding a one-way door quickly is how they lose their sixth.

DoorTypical examplesHow to handle
One-wayTenancy model · where data lives · the customer-facing API contract · the identity model · a deep dependency on one vendor's proprietary featureDecide with the customer's architect present, write an ADR, get explicit agreement. Budget real hours for these — they are worth a workshop each.
Heavy two-wayVector store choice · queue technology · orchestration framework (AI7)Decide fast behind an adapter interface. Note the migration cost in the ADR's consequences so nobody is surprised later.
Two-wayChunk size · prompt structure · which model tier for which step · rerank on/off · UI layoutDo not architect these. Set a default, measure with evals, iterate weekly. Bringing these to a review wastes the room's attention.

Vendor-neutrality by design

Customers ask "won't this lock us into one vendor?" and they are right to. The honest answer is not "no" — every system has dependencies — it is "here is exactly where the lock-in is, here is what it would cost to move, and here is the boundary that keeps the cost bounded." Neutrality is not achieved by avoiding good tools; it is achieved by placing adapter seams at the three or four places where substitution is plausible.

adapters.py — the seams that keep you honest 
from typing import Protocol, Sequence

# Seam 1 — model provider. The app never imports a vendor SDK directly.
class LLM(Protocol):
    def complete(self, messages: list[dict], *, tools: list[dict] | None = None,
                 max_tokens: int = 2048) -> "LLMResult": ...

class AnthropicLLM:
    # model ids live in config, never inline in business logic
    def __init__(self, client, model: str = "claude-sonnet-5"):
        self.client, self.model = client, model
    def complete(self, messages, *, tools=None, max_tokens=2048):
        r = self.client.messages.create(model=self.model, messages=messages,
                                        tools=tools or [], max_tokens=max_tokens)
        return to_result(r)          # normalise to OUR shape, not theirs

# Seam 2 — retrieval. Swapping pgvector → a managed vector DB must not
# touch orchestration code. AI5 compares the options.
class Retriever(Protocol):
    def search(self, query: str, *, k: int, principal: str,
               filters: dict | None = None) -> Sequence["Chunk"]: ...
    # NOTE: `principal` is mandatory in the interface on purpose —
    # it makes an ACL-blind implementation impossible to write by accident.

# Seam 3 — the systems of record. One class per external system, so
# "the ERP upgrade" is one file's problem, not the whole codebase's.
class OrderSystem(Protocol):
    def get_order(self, order_id: str) -> dict: ...
    def create_draft(self, payload: dict, *, idempotency_key: str) -> str: ...

# Seam 4 — telemetry. Vendor-neutral by standard, not by wrapper:
# emit OpenTelemetry spans; the customer points them at their backend.

Four seams, four files, roughly a day of work — and the answer to the lock-in question becomes a demonstration instead of a promise. State the residual honestly too: prompts tuned for one model family need re-evaluation on another (a week of eval work, not a rewrite), and a proprietary managed service you chose for speed is a named, priced dependency in the ADR. Customers forgive dependencies they were told about; they do not forgive the ones they discover.

The ADR: how a decision becomes durable

An Architecture Decision Record is one page, written at the moment of deciding, that survives you leaving. Its power is the "options considered" section — it proves the decision was made rather than defaulted into, which is exactly what an architecture review is testing.

ADR-0003 — worked example 
# ADR-0003 · Retrieval store for the policy-document assistant
Status: Accepted · Date: 2026-08-04 · Deciders: FDE (me), Customer
Architect (T. Nguyen), Security (M. Klein) · Supersedes: none

## Context
~180k policy pages across SharePoint (EU tenant) and a legacy DMS.
Per-user access rights differ by business unit and must be honoured at
query time — a leak here is a reportable incident. Corpus grows ~2%/mo;
freshness requirement is 24 h. Accuracy bar: ≥90% answer-groundedness
on a 150-question golden set. Budget: ≤ $0.05/query all-in. Data must
stay in the EU. Customer platform standard is Azure; team is 3 backend
engineers with Postgres experience and no vector-DB experience.

## Options considered
1) pgvector on the existing managed Postgres (EU region)
   + no new vendor, no new procurement, team already operates Postgres
   + ACL filtering is a normal SQL predicate on the same transaction
   - hybrid search needs manual BM25 wiring; rerank is a separate call
   - re-index of full corpus ≈ 40 min (acceptable at this size)
2) Managed vector DB (dedicated vendor)
   + best-in-class ANN, built-in hybrid + metadata filtering
   - new vendor: procurement + security review ≈ 6-8 weeks (measured
     against this customer's actual past reviews, not a guess)
   - EU region available but data leaves the customer's tenant
3) Azure AI Search
   + in-tenant, EU, hybrid + semantic ranker out of the box
   - per-index cost at this corpus size ≈ 2.4× option 1
   - proprietary query DSL → deepest lock-in of the three

## Decision
Option 1 — pgvector on managed Postgres, behind the `Retriever`
interface (see adapters.py). Hybrid search via Postgres FTS + vector,
rerank as a separate model call at top-50 → top-8.

## Consequences
+ Zero procurement delay; pilot can start in week 1.
+ ACLs enforced in the same query as retrieval — the security review's
  primary concern is answered structurally, not by policy.
+ Team can operate it after handover without new skills (FD10).
- If the corpus passes ~2M chunks or p95 retrieval exceeds 400 ms, we
  migrate. Migration estimated at 1.5 weeks behind the interface;
  re-embedding cost ≈ $900. Trigger + budget agreed with the architect.
- We own the hybrid-search wiring, incl. its own eval slice (AI9).

## Revisit when
Corpus > 2M chunks · p95 retrieval > 400 ms · a second use case needs
multi-modal retrieval · customer standardises on a vector platform.

Three details make that ADR good, and all three are learnable habits. It contains numbers from this customer (six-to-eight-week procurement, measured against their history, not folklore). It states the trigger to revisit, so the decision has an expiry rather than becoming dogma. And it prices the reversal — 1.5 weeks and $900 — which converts an argument about preferences into a conversation about a bounded, known risk.

Part IV · Sizing, phasing, committing

From a picture to a date you can defend

A diagram nobody can schedule is a poster. The FDE's architecture ends with a phased plan and a timeline they are willing to be held to — and the way to be held to a timeline safely is not to pad it secretly, but to decompose it, phase it, and put the uncertainty where everyone can see it.

Three phases, three different questions

The phasing discipline that matters: each phase must be independently valuable and independently killable. If the PoC's only output is "we learned things", you built a science project. If the pilot cannot be stopped without wasting everything, you did not phase — you sliced a waterfall. Write the exit criteria before starting the phase, and get them agreed; retrofitting exit criteria after a phase disappoints is how trust dies.

Estimating from the reference architecture

Top-down estimates ("feels like three months") are how FDEs get destroyed. Estimate bottom-up from the layers you just drew: every box on the diagram is a work item, and every arrow crossing an organisational boundary is a risk item. Then apply the heuristics.

HeuristicWhy it holds
The AI part is ~30% of the workPrompting and retrieval feel like the project; integration, auth, UI, ops and evals are the other 70%. Teams that estimate only the AI part miss by 3×.
Every external system = 1–3 weeksNot for the code — for credentials, sandbox access, rate limits, the undocumented field, and the one person who knows the schema being on leave (PR8).
Security review = calendar, not effortTwo days of your work spread over four to eight weeks of their process. Ask for their last three reviews' actual durations — measured, never assumed (PR5).
Eval set before build, alwaysBudget 3–5 days to build the golden set with the customer's expert. Skipping it doesn't save time; it moves the time into an unbounded tuning phase later (AI9).
Last 10% of accuracy = 40% of effortGetting to 80% is a week; 92% is a month of eval-driven iteration. Phase and price accordingly — and negotiate the bar in FD6 terms.
Customer-side tasks slip 2×Their people have day jobs. Any task on their critical path — data extract, SSO app registration, UAT — gets double the nominal duration in your plan, stated openly.

A risk with no trigger and no named owner is decoration. The trigger is what turns a risk into a decision on a specific date; the owner is who acts. Review the register in every weekly status — five minutes, and it does more for trust than any slide.

Committing to a timeline honestly

Customers hear a single number no matter how many you say, so choose which number to make single. The pattern that works: a committed date for a scoped phase, a forecast range for the rest, and the named dependencies that would move either.

"Pilot live with 20 users by 15 November — I'm committing to that, conditional on DMS read access by 12 September and the security pre-read happening the week of the 8th. Production hardening is a further 8–12 weeks; I'll narrow that range at the pilot exit review, once we know how the legacy documents behave."

One commitment. One range with a date on which it narrows. Two named dependencies with dates. This is FD6 applied to architecture — and the reason FDEs get to keep making commitments.

Two failure modes bracket this. Padding secretly buys you a comfortable first quarter and destroys your credibility when someone eventually benchmarks you. Refusing to commit ("it depends on many factors") reads as evasion, and gets the decision taken away from you. Commit to something small and real, early, and hit it — a met commitment is the currency that buys latitude on the big uncertain part.

Part V · Review & defend

The architecture only exists once it survives a room

You will present the same diagram to four audiences who want four different things from it. Preparing one deck for "the architecture review" and delivering it to all four is the most common way a good design gets rejected. Know who is in the room and lead with what they care about.

AudienceTheir real questionLead withWhat loses them
Customer architect"Does this fit our standards, and will I have to own it?"Where you used their existing platforms; the ADRs; the seams that let them substitute later.Introducing a new vendor they've never heard of, with no alternatives considered.
Security / compliance"Where does data go, and what can this thing do on its own?"The trust-boundary diagram, identity flow to the retrieval layer, the guardrails and the approval gates (PR4)."The model doesn't train on our data" as your only answer. Vague retention. No data-classification table.
Ops / platform"Who gets paged, and can we run this?"SLOs, failure modes and degraded behaviour, deploy/rollback, runbooks, the cost dashboard (PR11, PR7).A design with no answer for "what happens when the model API is down for an hour".
Your delivery team"Can I start Monday without guessing?"Interfaces and contracts, the phase-1 slice, what is explicitly out of scope, which decisions are already made.Boxes with no owner and no interface — the team will invent five incompatible answers.
Sponsor (bonus)"What am I buying, and when do I see value?"The phase picture, the exit criteria, the committed date, the KPI it moves (FD4, FD5).Any layer diagram at all. Sponsors do not want the blueprint; they want the plan.

Running the review meeting

−48 h — Send the package (diagram, one-line quality attributes, ADRs, scope, risks). Reviews where people read first are twice as productive; reviews where you present cold become QA sessions.
00–03 — State the goal explicitly: "I want two things today — agreement on six decisions, and your list of what I've got wrong." Inviting attack is what makes attack useful.
03–10 — The problem, the quality attributes, the phase plan. Never open with the layer diagram; open with what it must achieve.
10–20 — Walk the diagram once, top to bottom, following one real request end-to-end. A narrated request beats a tour of boxes, always.
20–35 — The decisions. One slide per ADR: options, choice, consequence, cost of reversal. This is the meeting; everything else is context.
35–45 — Risks and the timeline with its named dependencies. Ask directly for the two or three owners you need.
45–50 — Close on decisions: "Agreed as-is / agreed with changes / needs another session on X." Write the outcome in the ADRs the same day, and circulate.
The strongest move in an architecture review is naming your own weakest point before anyone else does: "The part I'm least confident about is the legacy DMS connector — here's the fallback if it doesn't hold." Reviewers who see you hunting your own weaknesses stop hunting them for you and start helping. Defensiveness costs you the room; pre-emptive honesty buys it.

When constraints change mid-engagement

They will. A cloud standard changes, a merger freezes procurement, security adds a control in week six, or the pilot reveals the accuracy bar was set for a different population of documents. The amateur response is to absorb it silently and slip; the FDE response is a short, boring ritual:

  1. Name it within 48 hours. Surprises age badly; a constraint disclosed late looks like a mistake concealed.
  2. Locate it on the diagram. Which layer, which decision, which ADR does it invalidate? Usually one — the picture makes the blast radius visible instead of rhetorical.
  3. Write a superseding ADR. Never edit the old one. "ADR-0007 supersedes ADR-0003" preserves the reasoning history, which is what a handover and an audit both need.
  4. Re-price, don't re-hope. New scope, new date, or dropped scope — pick one openly (FD6). "We'll try to absorb it" is a promise you will break.

Documenting for handover

The architecture package is most of the handover document, if you wrote it for a stranger from the start. Keep the diagram in version control as text (mermaid, not a screenshot in a slide deck), keep ADRs in /docs/adr/ next to the code, and make the eval suite and runbooks part of the repo. What the customer's team needs on day one after you leave is exactly what a good reviewer needed: what it is, why it is that way, and what would make it need to change (FD10).

The four anti-patterns

✗ Over-architecting for imaginary scale

Kubernetes, multi-region and a streaming bus for 40 internal users. You will spend the pilot budget on infrastructure nobody needed, and the accuracy — the thing that decides adoption — will be untouched. Design for 10× today's load, not 1000×; write down the trigger at which the next step happens (PR12).

✗ Ignoring the customer's existing stack

Proposing your favourite tools into an organisation with a platform standard, an approved-vendor list and a team that will inherit this. Every unnecessary novelty costs weeks of approval and a chunk of goodwill. Ask "what do you already run for this?" before every layer decision.

✗ Hidden lock-in

Building the core loop on a proprietary managed feature and never mentioning it. It surfaces during the renewal negotiation, and it surfaces as a betrayal. Name every dependency in the ADR, with a migration price attached.

✗ No evals or observability in the design

The system ships, quality drifts, nobody can prove whether last week's prompt change helped, and every incident is an argument. Evals and traces are load-bearing structure in an AI system — put them on the diagram as boxes with owners, not as a bullet in a "future work" slide.

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0
No exposure. Starts coding from the ticket; "architecture" means the folder structure. Cannot answer where customer data goes.
1
Awareness. Knows the nine layers and can name the default per layer; has read ADRs but not written one; estimates by feel.
2
Works with support. Produces a credible diagram and 3–4 ADRs for a familiar use case; needs a senior to catch missing security/eval layers, to price the reversal cost, and to run the review meeting.
3
Independent production (target). Self-check before claiming 3: take your last architecture and answer, without opening anything — what are the six decisions, what did you reject for each, and what does reversing the riskiest one cost in weeks and dollars? Any blank means you drew a picture, not an architecture.

In the field

Week three of an engagement at a European insurer. The prototype — a claims-document assistant — landed well; the sponsor wants "production by Q1". The customer's enterprise architect joins the first design session and opens with: "We standardise on Azure, and my team will own this after you leave."

The score-1 FDE hears an obstacle and starts negotiating for the stack they know. The score-3 FDE hears the two most valuable sentences of the engagement: the platform constraint and the identity of the future owner. Both go straight into the quality-attributes line and reshape three decisions before a line of code is written — same models, reached through the customer's own cloud (PR6); pgvector on the Postgres their team already operates instead of a new vector vendor; their existing queue rather than a new one.

Then the harder move: in the security pre-read, the FDE volunteers the weakest point — retrieval over documents whose ACLs live in a 12-year-old DMS with no per-user API. Security's answer was a control nobody had anticipated (a nightly permission snapshot with a documented staleness window), which cost six engineering days and would have cost six weeks if discovered during the go-live review. It became ADR-0006, with the staleness window as an explicit, signed-off risk.

The package went to review in week four: one diagram, six ADRs, a risk register with five rows, and a commitment — pilot live with 20 adjusters by 15 November, conditional on DMS access by 12 September. The architect's verdict, which the FDE quoted for the rest of the engagement: "This is the first vendor design I haven't had to redraw." Production followed in Q1. The team that owns it today has never called for help on the retrieval layer — because they chose it too.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Architecting before the numbers exist

Without an accuracy bar, a latency budget and a cost ceiling, every design is defensible and none is right. Get the six quality attributes quantified first — it takes one meeting and eliminates half your options.

✗ The diagram nobody can build from

Beautiful boxes, no interfaces, no owners, no data formats. Test it by handing the page to an engineer who wasn't there and asking what they'd build Monday morning.

✗ Deciding two-way doors in the review

Spending twenty minutes of a senior architect's time on chunk size while the tenancy model goes unexamined. Classify by cost of reversal before you build the agenda.

✗ Effort estimates presented as calendar dates

86 engineer-days is not "17 weeks" and is definitely not "9 weeks". Publish effort, waits and dependencies separately, then derive the window in front of the customer.

✓ Design with the customer's architect, not for them

Two whiteboard sessions where they pick two of the six decisions buys more approval velocity than any document. Co-authored architectures do not get rejected — they get defended by the co-author.

✓ Keep the diagram as text, in the repo

Mermaid next to the ADRs, versioned with the code. A screenshot in a slide deck is stale within a month and dead after handover; a diff-able diagram stays true and becomes the handover artifact for free.

Lab 1 · ~1.5 h · repeat monthly
Draw the reference architecture from memory

🎯 Objective

Make the nine-layer blueprint muscle memory, so that in a customer workshop — no notes, whiteboard, architect watching — you can draw a credible system in eight minutes and mark every layer with its choice and one alternative.

📦 Deliverable

One architecture.md containing a mermaid diagram plus a layer table with chosen option · alternative · why not the alternative for all nine layers. Under two pages.

🔬 Steps

1
Pick a use case you genuinely know — your FD2 prototype, a past client system, or an internal Golden Owl workflow. Imagined use cases fail this lab: you will invent convenient constraints.
2
Close this page. Set a timer for 8 minutes and draw the layers from memory on paper. Nine boxes, the arrows between them, and the two cross-cutting concerns. Do not look anything up — the gaps you produce are the lab's actual output.
3
Reopen Part II and diff. Missing layers, in order of how often people miss them: guardrails, evals, async, tenancy in the gateway. Note which you dropped — that is your personal blind spot, and it will be the same one next month unless you name it.
4
Write the quality-attribute line first — the six numbers. If you cannot fill one, write UNKNOWN — ask {name}. Unknowns are legitimate; silently inventing them is not. 
QA line: accuracy ≥__ on __-item golden set · p95 ≤__ s · ≤$__/unit
         data: __ region, retention __ · availability __ w/ fallback __
         audit: traces retained __ months
5
Now write it as mermaid, and fill the layer table. The alternative column is mandatory and is the whole point — a layer with no considered alternative is a default you fell into. 
| Layer            | Chosen                    | Alternative        | Why not |
|------------------|---------------------------|--------------------|---------|
| ① Surface        | Teams bot                 | standalone web app | adoption: they live in Teams |
| ② Gateway/auth   | OIDC via customer IdP     | service account    | need per-user ACLs downstream |
| ③ Orchestration  | fixed pipeline, 4 steps   | agent loop         | path is stable; audit needs determinism |
| ④ Model layer    | haiku extract → sonnet     | single sonnet call | 60% cost cut, same eval score |
| ⑤ Knowledge      | pgvector hybrid + rerank  | managed vector DB  | 8-wk procurement, team knows PG |
| ⑥ Tools          | 2 typed tools, dry-run    | direct DB writes   | reversibility + audit |
| ⑦ Async          | queue for >10 s runs      | all synchronous    | p95 blows the latency budget |
| ⑧ State          | Postgres + blob, 90 d     | keep everything    | PR5 retention limit |
| ⑨ Obs + evals    | OTel traces + CI gate     | manual spot-check  | no regression detection otherwise |
| ⓪ Guardrails     | schema + PII + approvals  | prompt-only        | writes touch customer records |
6
Narrate one request end-to-end, out loud, in under 90 seconds: "a user asks X → gateway resolves tenant and identity → orchestrator assembles context → retriever filters by their ACLs → sonnet drafts → validator checks schema → response with citations → trace written." Any hesitation is a hole in the design, not in your delivery.
7
Adversarial pass with Claude — paste your diagram and table: 
You are a skeptical enterprise architect at the customer. Here is a
proposed AI system architecture and its layer decisions. Do three
things, briefly:
1. Name every layer or concern that is MISSING or under-specified.
2. Find the two decisions most likely to be wrong given the stated
   quality attributes, and say what you would choose instead.
3. Ask the five questions you would ask in a review that this
   document cannot currently answer.
Be specific and blunt. Do not compliment the design.
Fix what lands. Ignore what doesn't apply — but write one sentence saying why, because "doesn't apply" is itself a decision.

🧯 Common failures

  • Boxes without interfaces. "Retrieval" is not a design; "search(query, k, principal) → chunks with source + ACL tag" is.
  • The demo architecture in disguise. No gateway, no tenancy, no evals — you drew your prototype and relabelled it.
  • Alternatives invented to look thorough. If you would never have chosen it, it is not an alternative; find the one a competent architect would actually propose.

✅ Done when

The 8-minute memory draw contains all nine layers plus guardrails and evals, the table's "why not" column has a real reason on every row, and you can narrate a request end-to-end without pausing. Repeat monthly with a different use case; the drill takes 20 minutes once it is genuinely internalised.
Lab 2 · ~2 h
The ADR set

🎯 Objective

Convert your Lab 1 diagram into six defensible decisions. The skill being trained is not writing — it is considering a real alternative seriously and then pricing the reversal, which is what separates an architecture from a preference.

📦 Deliverable

Six files in /docs/adr/: 0001-model-and-routing.md, 0002-knowledge-and-retrieval-store.md, 0003-integration-pattern.md, 0004-auth-and-tenancy.md, 0005-async-and-queueing.md, 0006-eval-and-observability.md. One page each, no exceptions.

📋 The template

# ADR-{nnnn} · {decision in a noun phrase, not a verb}
Status: Proposed | Accepted | Superseded by ADR-nnnn
Date: YYYY-MM-DD · Deciders: {names + roles, incl. customer side}

## Context
The forces: the quality attributes that bear on this decision (with
numbers), the customer constraints, the team's skills, the timeline.
Written so a stranger in 12 months understands the situation without
asking anyone. 5-10 lines.

## Options considered
1) {Option} — + benefits · - costs · effort/procurement reality
2) {Option} — ...
3) {Option} — ...        # at least two REAL alternatives

## Decision
What we chose and the boundary/interface it sits behind.

## Consequences
+ What becomes easy or safe.
- What becomes hard, expensive, or newly owned by us.
- Cost of reversal: {weeks} + {$} + {what must be re-approved}.

## Revisit when
The measurable trigger that should reopen this decision.

🔬 Steps

1
Start with the Context section of all six before writing any Decision. Doing all contexts first stops you from reverse-engineering justifications for choices you already made — the single most common ADR failure.
2
ADR-0001 · Model & routing. Which tier does which step, what triggers escalation, what is the fallback when the provider is unavailable, and where do model ids live in config. Concretely: claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 for classification/extraction, claude-sonnet-5 for reasoning and tool use — with the eval evidence for that split, not the assumption (PR2). 
# routing.yaml — the decision, made operational
steps:
  classify_intent:   { model: claude-haiku-4-5-20251001, max_tokens: 256 }
  extract_fields:    { model: claude-haiku-4-5-20251001, max_tokens: 1024,
                       escalate_if: "confidence < 0.75" }
  reason_and_draft:  { model: claude-sonnet-5, max_tokens: 4096 }
budgets:
  per_request_usd: 0.05          # hard cap, enforced in orchestrator
  max_tool_calls:  8
fallback:
  on: [overloaded, timeout_5xx]
  action: retry_with_backoff -> secondary_provider_via_adapter
3
ADR-0002 · Knowledge & retrieval store. Walk the Part III decision tree and record where you exited it. Must address: chunking strategy, hybrid vs vector-only, rerank, freshness/sync, and — the one reviewers always probe — how per-user permissions are enforced at query time (AI4, AI5, PR13).
4
ADR-0003 · Integration pattern. For each external system: read path, write path, idempotency, dry-run/simulation mode, error semantics, and who owns credentials. If a system has no API, state the fallback and its expiry date explicitly (PR8, AI8).
5
ADR-0004 · Auth & tenancy. The most expensive one to reverse. Identity source, how the principal reaches the retrieval query, single- vs multi-tenant data separation, service-to-service auth, and secret storage (PR13, PR12).
6
ADR-0005 · Async & queueing. The sync/async boundary with a number (">10 s p95 → queue"), status/callback contract, retry and DLQ policy, and what the user sees while waiting (PR3).
7
ADR-0006 · Eval & observability. Golden-set size and who owns its labels, which metrics gate a release, what a trace contains (and what it must not contain under PR5), retention, and the dashboards ops will actually look at (AI9, PR1).
8
The reversal audit. Go through all six and fill "cost of reversal" with a number of weeks and dollars. Then rank them. The top two are your one-way doors — those are the ones that need the customer's architect in the room, and the ones you lead with in Lab 4's review.
9
Stress test. Paste all six into Claude: 
You are the customer's principal architect reviewing these six ADRs.
For each: (a) is the "options considered" section honest, or is it a
strawman built to justify a pre-made choice? (b) which consequence is
understated? (c) what would you have chosen instead, and what evidence
would change your mind? Then name the ONE decision here most likely to
be regretted in 12 months, and why.
Any ADR where the strawman critique lands must be rewritten — that is the lab working, not failing.

🧯 Common failures

  • Strawman alternatives. "Option 2: build our own vector database" is not a considered alternative; it is theatre.
  • Consequences that are all upside. Every real decision costs something. An ADR with no "-" lines has not been thought through.
  • No numbers in Context. Without the quality attributes the decision cannot be evaluated by anyone else — including future-you.
  • Writing ADRs after building. Then they are documentation, not decisions, and they will quietly rationalise whatever happened.

✅ Done when

Six one-page ADRs exist, each with at least two genuine alternatives, a priced reversal, and a revisit trigger — and the adversarial pass produced at most one rewrite. At score 3 you write this set in a working day alongside customer conversations.
Lab 3 · ~2 h
Phasing & estimate

🎯 Objective

Turn the architecture into a plan you would sign: three phases with exit criteria, a bottom-up estimate that separates effort from calendar, and a risk register whose rows have triggers and owners.

📦 Deliverable

plan.md — phase table, estimate worksheet, calendar dependencies, risk register, and one paragraph of committed language you could read aloud to a sponsor.

🔬 Steps

1
Phase decomposition. Split the target system into PoC / pilot / production. Rule to enforce on yourself: each phase must be independently valuable and independently killable. Write the exit criteria first, as measurable statements — no "we'll see how it goes". 
| Phase | Scope (in)                        | Explicitly out       | Exit criteria (measurable) |
|-------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| PoC   | 1 workflow · 1 team · real data   | SSO, write-back, UI  | ≥85% on 100-item golden set;
|       | manual ops OK; export-based data  | polish               | access paths confirmed by IT;
|       |                                   |                      | cost/unit measured on real docs |
| Pilot | SSO + real ACLs · 20 users        | 2nd use case, mobile | adoption ≥70% weekly active;
|       | traces + feedback · 2 write-tools |                      | KPI moved per FD4; security
|       |                                   |                      | review passed |
| Prod  | SLOs · on-call · CI eval gate     | multi-region, i18n   | customer team ships a release
|       | rollback · cost dashboard         |                      | unaided; handover signed (FD10) |
2
Bottom-up estimate. Walk your Lab 1 diagram layer by layer — every box is a line item with engineer-days and a confidence rating. Estimating from the diagram rather than from feel is the entire technique; it also makes the estimate reviewable, because someone can argue with a line instead of with your intuition.
3
Apply the heuristics from Part IV and check yourself: is the non-AI work at least ~2× the AI work? Did every external system get 1–3 weeks? Is there a line for building the golden set? Are customer-side tasks doubled? If your estimate contradicts a heuristic, write the reason down — sometimes it is right, but it should be deliberate.
4
Name the buffers, don't hide them. Two explicit buffer lines — data access and security review — with the reasoning attached. Hidden padding gets discovered and taxed; named buffers get respected and defended by the customer's own people.
5
Separate calendar from effort. List every wait on the customer's side, its expected duration (ask for their last three, don't guess), when it must start, and what it blocks. Then derive the window. This block is what makes a commitment survivable.
6
Risk register. Five to eight rows, five columns: risk · likelihood · impact · mitigation with a trigger · owner. Force at least two rows to have a customer-side owner — a register where the FDE owns everything is a fantasy about control. 
| ID | Risk | L | I | Mitigation + TRIGGER | Owner |
|----|------|---|---|----------------------|-------|
| R-01 | {access/data risk}     | H | H | {action}; TRIGGER: {date/metric} → {fallback} | {customer role} |
| R-02 | {security/compliance}  | M | H | pre-read wk2; TRIGGER: new control → ADR + rescope | FDE |
| R-03 | {accuracy on a slice}  | M | M | slice in golden set wk1; TRIGGER: <80% wk4 → scope out | FDE |
7
Write the commitment paragraph. One committed date for a scoped phase, one forecast range with the date on which it narrows, and the named dependencies. Read it aloud. If it contains "should", "hopefully" or "assuming everything goes well", rewrite it (FD6).
8
Pre-mortem. With Claude or a colleague: "It is six months later and this pilot failed. Write the three most likely post-mortems." Any cause that isn't already a row in your register gets added — pre-mortems reliably surface the organisational failure modes that technical people skip.

🧯 Common failures

  • Exit criteria that are activities. "Complete integration testing" is a task. "≥85% on the 100-item golden set" is a criterion.
  • A phase that cannot be killed. If stopping after the PoC leaves the customer with nothing usable and nothing learned that they can act on, re-cut the phases.
  • Effort presented as a date. The number one cause of an FDE missing a commitment they could have met.

✅ Done when

A colleague reading plan.md can state what happens at the end of each phase, what would stop it, and what the committed date depends on — without asking you anything.

🔬 The defence — a 30-minute review

1

3

🧯 Common failures

  • Reviewing with a friendly audience. A reviewer who says "looks good" gave you nothing. Brief them to attack, and thank them loudly when they land one.
  • Defending in the room. Every minute spent justifying is a minute not spent learning what is wrong.
  • A revision that changes nothing. If a 30-minute expert review produced no change, either the review was soft or you did not listen. Both are fixable; neither is acceptable as an outcome.
  • Scope with no "out" section. The most expensive omission in the entire package.
