Customer Discovery

Interviewing stakeholders and end users to find the real workflow, the real pain, the real data — and the one AI use case worth building. The skill that decides projects before any code exists.

Why it matters for an FDE: Choosing the wrong use case is failure from day zero — the FDE's decisive skill, and it is not code. A brilliant implementation of the wrong problem is still a failed engagement.
Part I · Mindset

Discovery is risk elimination, not requirements gathering

A junior engineer asks "what do you want built?" and writes it down. An FDE treats discovery as a hunt for the three risks that kill AI projects:

Value risk

Does anyone actually lose money, time or sleep over this? Or is it just an interesting idea?

AI-fit risk

Can a probabilistic system do this job acceptably — given what happens when it's wrong?

Data risk

Does the input the AI needs exist, in usable form, and will anyone actually let you access it?

Your deliverable is not a feature list — it is a use case where all three risks were examined and survived, written up so precisely that a teammate could scope the build from it. Discovery happens twice in the FDE lifecycle: a light version during pre-sales (FD11) and a deep version at engagement start — and it never fully ends: every demo you give afterwards (FD2) is another discovery probe.

Part II · The feasibility model

The AI feasibility triangle

AI fit: the counterintuitive corner

Customers assume AI fit means "is the AI smart enough?". It actually means "what is the cost of being wrong, and who catches it?" — because a probabilistic system will be wrong some percentage of the time, forever (FN2). The one-sentence question that cuts through any AI idea:

"When it's wrong 1 time in 20 — who catches it, and what does the mistake cost?"
A crisp answer ("the reviewer catches it in the same screen, costs 30 seconds") = green light. A long silence = you just saved everyone six months.
Strong AI fitWeak AI fit
Drafting anything a human reviews (emails, reports, code)Autonomous decisions with money/legal/safety consequences
Triage & routing with confidence thresholds and a human fallbackTasks demanding 100% accuracy with no review step possible
Search/summarization where the user verifies via citationsPrecise arithmetic over many records with no tool support
Extraction from messy documents, human-checked at low confidenceAnything where a silent error surfaces weeks later, unattributable

Part IV · Interview craft

Past behavior beats hypotheticals — always

People are polite and imaginative — a lethal combination for discovery. Ask "would AI help here?" and everyone says yes; ship it and nobody uses it. The fix (adapted from The Mom Test): ask about what happened, recently, specifically — never about opinions of your idea.

✗ Don't ask✓ Ask insteadWhy
"Would an AI assistant help?""Walk me through the last time you did this. Screen-share if you can."Behavior can't be polite; opinions can
"How much time would this save?""How many did you handle yesterday? How long did the worst one take, and why?"Real counts beat projected ones
"Is your data quality good?""Open one real example file with me right now."The single highest-value minute in AI discovery
"Do you have workarounds?""What have you already tried? Which spreadsheet / macro / intern absorbs this today?"Existing workarounds prove the pain is real and priced
"Is this a big problem for you?""If this disappeared tomorrow, what would you do with the time?"Vague pain inflates; displaced time is concrete

Running the session: a 45-minute shape

00–05 — Frame it: "I'm here to understand how the work actually happens — no idea to sell today. The messy parts are the useful parts."
05–25 — Follow the workflow: "walk me through the last one" → then probe: "where did that number come from?", "what happens if that field is empty?", "who do you ask when stuck?"
25–35 — Follow the pain: worst recent case, frequency, workarounds, the "5 whys" on anything that sounds absurd (it usually has a reason).
35–42 — Follow the data: open a real example together; note system names, owners, formats.
42–45 — Close: "who else should I talk to?" (snowball sampling) + permission to follow up.
  • Silence is a tool. After an answer, wait three seconds. The sentence that follows the pause is where the truth lives.
  • Follow the artifact. Whenever a document, spreadsheet or screen is mentioned — ask to see it. Artifacts don't perform for visitors; people do.
  • Note verbatim quotes. "I lose every Friday afternoon to this report" will power your problem statement, your demo script, and your exec readout. Paraphrase kills it.
  • Interview end users, not only executives. Execs describe the org chart's version of the process; the person doing it Tuesday knows the exceptions and the ugly export button everything depends on. The gap between the two stories is usually where the use case lives.
Part V · Ground truth

Shadowing and the data reality check

Interviews tell you what people think they do. A shadow session — 60–90 minutes sitting next to a user during real work — shows what they actually do: the Alt-Tab dance across five systems, the paper notebook of "special cases", the two minutes of judgment inside a "simple" task that your AI design must either replicate or hand back to a human. One shadow session per key role; take timestamps.

Part VI · Synthesis

From notes to a signed problem statement

Score the use-case list — multiply, don't average

Mine 8–15 candidates from your notes, score each 1–5 on four factors, and multiply: multiplication punishes the zero. A 5-5-5-1 use case is a demo nobody adopts; a 4-4-4-4 is a business. A worked example from a logistics engagement:

CandidateValueAI fitDataWorkflow fitScore
Booking-email → TMS extraction4445320
Customer-facing chatbot532390
Auto-quote generation523390
Weekly KPI report drafting2544160

Workflow fit — does the output land inside a tool people already use? — is the factor teams forget, and the one that predicts adoption. Present the scorecard to the customer: killing their pet idea with a transparent rubric preserves the relationship; killing it with your opinion does not.

The problem statement one-pager

Seven sections, numbers included — the full worked example lives in Lab 4:

  1. Context — team, workflow, systems (3 sentences max).
  2. Problem — quantified: "5 staff × ~90 min/day re-keying bookings; ~4% error rate causing ~$11K/mo in corrections."
  3. Proposed use case — what AI does, where the human stays in the loop, what happens on low confidence.
  4. Data map — inputs, systems, sample verified ✓, access path ✓.
  5. Success criteria — baseline → target, measured how, by when. Feeds FD4 and AI9 directly.
  6. Constraints & risks — security, compliance, integration, politics.
  7. Out of scope — written down, or scope creep starts day one.

Timebox: 1–2 weeks. Discovery that runs longer is usually avoiding the readout, not learning. The readout itself: 30 minutes — findings as quotes, the scorecard, the one-pager, and one explicit ask: "do we agree this is the problem worth a prototype?"

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0
No exposure. Waits for a spec; builds whatever the loudest person asked for.
1
Awareness. Knows the triangle, the question patterns, the stakeholder map; has observed a discovery.
2
Works with support. Runs interviews from a prepared guide; needs a senior to spot the real use case and to push back on bad ideas.
3
Independent production (target). Leads a 1–2 week discovery solo: interviews, shadowing, data checks, scored use-case list, problem statement the customer signs. Kills unviable ideas without losing the room.

Leads & coaches. Runs discovery for strategic accounts, turns one use case into a five-item roadmap, coaches interview technique, shapes pre-sales scoping (FD11).
Self-check before claiming 3: in your last three interviews, count your hypothetical questions ("would you…", "do you think…"). More than two per interview? You're still selling, not discovering.

In the field

Kickoff call. The CEO of a distribution company opens with: "We want a ChatGPT for our whole business — customers, suppliers, everything."

The score-1 response starts estimating a chatbot. The score-3 FDE says "great ambition — let me spend a week finding where AI pays back fastest", and interviews the ops floor. The accounts-payable clerk mentions, offhand, that three people spend every morning matching supplier invoices to purchase orders because item descriptions never match exactly.

Value: ~60 staff-hours/week. AI fit: fuzzy matching with human review of low-confidence pairs — ideal. Data: both document types already land in one database; a sample checked out on the spot. The one-pager quantified $210K/year; the CEO approved the pilot in that same meeting — and "ChatGPT for everything" became a roadmap slide funded by the credibility of the first win.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Solution-first discovery

Arriving with "we'll build a RAG chatbot" and collecting confirmation. You'll get polite nods, then no adoption. Arrive with questions; let the use case emerge.

✗ Only interviewing the sponsor

Executives describe the org chart's process; the adoptable use case lives with end users. Always get both stories and mine the gap between them.

✗ Taking "the data exists" on faith

"It's all in the system" has ended more AI pilots than any model limitation. No use case is scored until a real sample was opened and the access path confirmed.

✗ Skipping the skeptic

The person burned by the last failed project holds your best risk analysis, free. Interview them early — and quote them in the readout; converts make the strongest champions.

✓ Write quotes verbatim

"I lose every Friday afternoon to this report" is worth ten slides — in the problem statement, the demo script (FD2), and the exec readout (FD5).

✓ Timebox and ship the readout

Two weeks, then a 30-minute readout with one explicit ask. Discovery without a deadline becomes anthropology.

Lab 1 · ~1 h · repeat weekly
The interview flight simulator

🎯 Objective

Build interview reps safely. Claude roleplays a realistic stakeholder who withholds information from bad questions and rewards good ones — then grades your technique against a rubric.

🔬 Steps

1
Open a fresh Claude chat and paste persona A: 
You are role-playing Linh, Head of Operations at a mid-size freight
forwarding company (300 staff). Context you know but only reveal when
asked good questions:
- Your team of 5 manually re-keys booking data from customer emails
  into the TMS; ~90 min/person/day; error-prone in peak season.
- A failed "chatbot" project last year made you skeptical of AI.
- Data lives in Outlook shared inboxes + on-prem SQL Server; IT is
  protective; one Excel macro nobody understands is critical.
- You have real pain but will NOT volunteer it. Answer hypothetical
  or leading questions with polite, empty enthusiasm. Reward specific
  questions about past events with concrete details.

I am an FDE running a discovery interview. Stay in character until I
say "DEBRIEF" — then step out and grade me on the rubric I'll provide.
2
Interview for 20 minutes. Apply Part IV: workflow → pain → data; follow artifacts; three-second silences (in text: resist filling the gap with a second question).
3
Say DEBRIEF and paste the rubric: 
Grade me 1-5 on each, with the specific moments as evidence:
1. Hypothetical/leading questions asked (5 = zero)
2. Did I find the re-keying pain? How directly?
3. Did I find the failed-chatbot scar and handle it well?
4. Did I uncover the data reality (inboxes, SQL, macro, protective IT)?
5. Did I quantify anything (counts, minutes, error rates)?
6. Best question I asked; worst question I asked; the question a
   great interviewer would have asked that I missed.
Log your scores in a note — same rubric every week; the trend is the point.
4
Personas B and C (subsequent sessions):
  • B — Security lead, defensive: "Đức, IT Security Manager at a bank. Burned by vendors overpromising. Answers with risks and objections. Reveals the real constraint (data cannot leave the country) only if the interviewer asks about past vendor failures with genuine curiosity instead of countering objections."
  • C — CEO, over-eager: "Mai, CEO who read about AI agents on a flight. Wants everything, describes no concrete pain, changes topic to strategy. The interviewer's challenge: politely drag the conversation from vision down to 'show me one workflow' — grade them on whether they landed it."
Each persona trains a different muscle: extraction (A), objection navigation (B), scoping-down (C).
5
Reverse the drill once per persona: "Now YOU interview ME about my last project using best discovery practice." Steal every question that made you think.

✅ Done when

Three sessions logged, scores trending up, and your last session has zero leading questions. Keep the ritual weekly — seniors do reps too.
Lab 2 · ~2 h
Use-case mining & scoring

🎯 Objective

Practice the synthesis half of discovery: mine candidates from real knowledge, score them honestly, and survive a challenge — before doing it with a customer watching.

🔬 Steps

1
Pick an organization you genuinely know: Golden Owl itself, or a past client. (Real knowledge matters — the lab fails on an imagined company because you'll invent convenient facts.)
2
List 10 candidates from observed pain, not from "cool AI ideas". Prompt yourself per department: What gets re-typed? What gets searched for and not found? What report does someone dread? What decision waits on a person reading documents? Write each as: who + workflow + pain — "PM team re-summarizes client emails into Jira tickets every morning".
3
Score all 10 on Value / AI-fit / Data / Workflow-fit (1–5 each), then multiply. Use the worked logistics example in Part VI as your calibration anchor. Honesty rules: any factor you can't justify with a concrete fact gets max 2 — the rubric works only when unknowns hurt.
4
For the winner, write the error-cost sentence: "When it's wrong 1-in-20, ___ catches it, costing ___." If you can't fill both blanks concretely, the AI-fit score was fiction — rescore.
5
Defend it. 10 minutes with a colleague (or Claude as devil's advocate: "Attack this use case scorecard as a skeptical CTO: challenge every score, find the hidden assumption most likely to kill it"). If the #1 pick survives, take it into Lab 3. If it dies — the lab worked perfectly; pick #2.

✅ Done when

A 10-row scorecard exists with a defended winner and a written error-cost sentence. Time yourself — at score 3 this whole exercise takes under an hour with real interview notes in hand.
Lab 3 · ~1.5 h
The data reality drill

🎯 Objective

Run the 6-point data checklist end-to-end on your Lab 2 winner — and experience how much of "the data exists" dissolves under contact.

🔬 Steps

1
Fill the checklist header: use case, data inputs needed (list every one — an invoice-matching case needs invoices and POs and the matching history).
2
Existence + location: for each input, get an actual sample file/record — not a description. Note system, format, and owner as a role ("ERP admin team"), not a person's name.
3
Quality probe on 10 random samples — the heart of the drill. Build a mini table: sample #, complete?, consistent format?, fields that lie, surprises. Ten samples reliably surface: the free-text field with three date formats, the "mandatory" field that's empty 40% of the time, the attachment that's actually a photo of a screen.
4
Access path: write the concrete route AI would take to this data (API? DB replica? export job?) and who must approve it. If you're doing this on a real engagement: confirm with IT this week — the approval lead time is itself a discovery finding.
5
Volume, history, sensitivity: records/day, months of history available (enough for a golden set? AI9 needs 20+ real cases), PII/residency flags for PR5.
6
Verdict paragraph: "Data readiness: X/5 because …" — plus the one remediation that would raise it. This paragraph goes verbatim into the Lab 4 one-pager's data map.

✅ Done when

The checklist is complete with a filled 10-sample probe table and a one-paragraph verdict — and at least one "surprise" you did not expect when you started. (No surprises usually means the probe was too shallow.)
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~4 h + interviews
The discovery pack

🎯 Objective

Assemble the catalog evidence — "interview notes and a clear problem statement" — at a quality where a teammate who attended nothing can still answer: what hurts, how much, what we'd build, how we'll know it worked.

📋 Component 1 — Interview notes (3+ conversations)

Real interviews if you have an engagement; otherwise interview colleagues about internal workflows (real pain exists at Golden Owl too). Template per interview:

# Interview — {name, role}  ·  {date} · {45 min}
TRIGGER QUOTE: "..."                  # the verbatim sentence that matters most
WORKFLOW OBSERVED: ...                # steps as they described/showed, incl. tools
PAIN, QUANTIFIED: ...                 # counts, minutes, error rates, frequency
WORKAROUNDS: ...                      # the spreadsheet/macro/intern absorbing it today
DATA MENTIONED: ...                   # systems, formats, owners
POLITICS / CONTEXT: ...               # skeptics, history, constraints
FOLLOW-UPS: ...                       # who else to talk to, what to verify

📋 Component 2 — As-is workflow sketch

One diagram (mermaid or hand-drawn photo) of how the work happens today — including the ugly parts: the re-keying, the two systems, the judgment step. This sketch becomes the "before" in every future demo.

📋 Component 3 — Problem statement one-pager

Worked example to calibrate against (freight case from Lab 1's persona):

# Problem statement — booking data entry automation        2026-08-__
## Context
Ops team (5 staff) processes ~140 customer booking emails/day,
re-keying shipment details from unstructured emails into the TMS.
## Problem
~90 min/person/day of manual entry (37 staff-hrs/wk). Peak-season
error rate ~4% → downstream corrections ≈ $11K/mo. Quote: "In peak
season I stay late every day just doing entry." — T., senior op
## Proposed use case
AI extracts structured booking fields from inbound emails → prefills
TMS entry screen. Operator reviews & confirms (human-in-the-loop).
Below 90% field confidence → flagged for full manual entry.
Error cost when wrong 1-in-20: operator catches at review, ~20 sec.
## Data map
Inbound emails: Outlook shared inbox (Graph API, IT approval needed —
requested 08/xx) ✓ sample verified · TMS: SQL Server, write via
existing import job ✓ · 14 months email history available for evals
## Success criteria (baseline → target)
Entry time: 90 → ≤30 min/person/day · field accuracy ≥97% on
200-email holdout · operator adoption ≥80% by week 6
## Constraints & risks
On-prem SQL only (no cloud DB copy) · Excel macro in workflow must
stay · skepticism from 2024 chatbot failure — champion: Linh
## Out of scope (phase 1)
Auto-send confirmations · non-email channels (phone/Zalo) · pricing

🔬 Final steps

1
Draft all three components from your real notes (Labs 2–3 feed sections directly).
2
Get a signature. Present to the real stakeholder — or a senior colleague as proxy — with one question: "Would you sign off on the success criteria as written?" Revise once from their pushback. A one-pager nobody pushed back on hasn't been read.
3
The teammate test: hand the pack to someone who attended nothing; have them answer the four questions (what hurts / how much / what build / how measured). Any hesitation marks the section to rewrite.

✅ Done when

Pack passes the teammate test and carries a real sign-off. File it as your template — at score 3, you'll produce one of these per engagement in a week, and its success criteria will flow straight into FD2's demo script and AI9's first eval.
