Document Processing Pipelines

OCR, PDF/table extraction, layout understanding and low-quality scans (Docling, unstructured.io, LLM extraction).

Why it matters for an FDE: Documents are the most common form of enterprise data; extraction quality determines the quality of everything downstream.
Part I · Why & when

Documents are enterprise data #1

Ask a customer where their knowledge lives and they will say "the database". Ask their operations team and the answer changes: it lives in a SharePoint folder with 40,000 PDFs, an email inbox full of supplier invoices, a shared drive of scanned contracts, and an Excel file someone maintains by hand. Analyst estimates put ~80–90% of enterprise data in unstructured form, and the overwhelming majority of that unstructured mass is documents. Every AI engagement that touches "our data" eventually touches a PDF.

This module is the plumbing layer beneath AI4. AI4 tells you that ingestion is the unglamorous 60% of RAG and that you must eyeball ten parsed documents before trusting anything. PR9 is where you actually learn how to parse them, how to measure whether the parse was good, and what to do when it wasn't.

Where RAG and extraction projects actually die

Post-mortems on failed document-AI pilots almost never say "the model was not smart enough". They say some version of the following four things — all of which are extraction failures wearing a model-shaped costume:

What the user reportsActual causeHow you confirm in 5 minutes
"It can't find things that are definitely in there"Scanned PDF with no text layer → empty chunkslen(page.extract_text()) across the corpus; count pages under 50 chars
"The numbers it quotes are wrong"Table flattened into word soup; column alignment lostPrint the raw parsed text of one table page and try to read it yourself
"It answers from the wrong section"Headers/footers and page numbers polluting every chunkGrep parsed text for the footer string; count occurrences
"It works on some documents and not others"Mixed corpus; one document class parses to garbageGroup extraction quality by doc_type, not by average
The averaging trap. A corpus that is 85% clean digital PDFs and 15% scans will report a cheerful "93% extraction accuracy" while being completely useless for the 15% — which is invariably the subset the customer cares most about, because scanned documents are the old contracts nobody has re-typed. Always report extraction quality per document type, never as a single number.

The document zoo

Before you choose a tool, classify what you are up against. These seven species behave completely differently, and a pipeline tuned for one will faceplant on another:

1 · Clean digital PDFs

Exported from Word/LaTeX/a report generator. Real text layer, reading order usually sane. ~50ms/page with pypdf, $0. If your corpus is all this, you do not need a fancy pipeline — and saying so earns trust.

2 · Scanned PDFs

A photo of a page in a PDF wrapper. Zero extractable text. Needs OCR. Quality ranges from 300-DPI flatbed scans (easy) to a phone photo of a fax of a photocopy (brutal).

3 · Office files

DOCX/XLSX/PPTX. Structurally the easiest — the XML already has headings, cells, and slide order — yet teams routinely convert them to PDF first and throw all of it away. Don't.

4 · Images

JPEG/PNG/TIFF, often from a mobile capture app. Add skew, shadows, and perspective distortion to the OCR problem (AI11).

5 · Tables

The hard problem, treated on its own in Part II. Merged cells, spanning headers, multi-page continuation, no ruling lines. Where most pipelines quietly lose 30% of their accuracy.

6 · Forms

Key–value layouts where meaning lives in spatial adjacency. Plain text extraction gives you "Invoice Date 2026-03-14 Due Date 2026-04-13" and no way to know which date is which.

7 · Handwriting

Signatures, margin notes, filled-in forms. Classical OCR essentially cannot do this; vision-LLMs and specialised cloud OCR can, at 70–90% depending on the writer. Always route to human review.

8 · The mixed reality

Real corpora are all seven at once, often inside one file: a digital contract with a scanned signature page and an appendix table. Per-page classification beats per-document classification.

The quality ceiling, drawn

This is the single most useful sentence you can say in a document-AI kickoff: "whatever accuracy we get out of extraction is the hard ceiling for the whole system, so let's measure that first, before we talk about models." It reframes the project from a model problem into a data problem — which is what it is.

Part II · The extraction toolkit

Four rungs, and knowing which one you're on

The toolkit is a ladder, not a menu. Each rung costs more and handles more. Start at the bottom and climb only when a measurement forces you to — a habit that will save your customer five figures a year on a corpus that turned out to be 90% clean PDFs.

Rung 1 · Native text parsing (pypdf, pdfplumber, python-docx)

A digital PDF already contains its text — the parser just reads the content stream. pypdf is the fastest and dumbest; pdfplumber is slower but exposes per-character coordinates, which lets you reconstruct columns and detect headers/footers yourself. For Office files, python-docx / openpyxl / python-pptx give you structure for free. 

import pypdf
r = pypdf.PdfReader("contract.pdf")
chars = [len(p.extract_text() or "") for p in r.pages]
print(f"{sum(1 for c in chars if c < 50)}/{len(chars)} pages look like scans")
# <50 chars on a full page ⇒ no text layer ⇒ route to OCR

Cost: $0. Speed: 20–80ms/page. Accuracy on clean digital text: 95–99% of characters. Fails completely on scans, and mangles multi-column layouts and tables (it emits text in content-stream order, which is not reading order).

Rung 2 · Layout-aware parsing (Docling, unstructured.io)

These libraries run a layout detection model over the page — segmenting it into titles, paragraphs, lists, tables, figures, headers and footers — and then emit structured output (Markdown or a JSON element tree) with reading order preserved. IBM's Docling produces clean Markdown with real Markdown tables and is the current best free default; unstructured.io covers a wider file-type surface (email, HTML, EPUB, ~25 types) with a partition-into-elements API.

This rung is where you get section paths — the thing that makes structural chunking (AI4) possible. It is also where most engagements should live: layout-aware parsing typically lifts retrieval recall by 10–25 points over naive text extraction on documents with any real structure, for $0 in API spend and 0.5–3 seconds of CPU per page.

Where the vision-LLM figures come from, so you can redo the math for your own page size: a Letter page downscaled to a 1568px long edge is roughly 2,500 input tokens; a full-page transcription is roughly 800 output tokens. At claude-sonnet-5 rates ($3/MTok in, $15/MTok out) that's 2,500 × $3/10⁶ + 800 × $15/10⁶ ≈ $0.0195/page → ~$20 per 1,000 pages. At claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 rates ($1/$5) the same page is ≈ $0.0065 → ~$6.50 per 1,000. Prompt caching does not help here (every page is new pixels), but the Batch API does — see PR3.

The vision-LLM failure mode nobody warns you about: classical OCR that can't read a character emits ? or garbage — obviously wrong. A vision-LLM that can't read a character emits a plausible one. An invoice total of $1,340.00 smudged at the comma may come back as $1340.00 — or as $7,340.00, formatted perfectly, with no signal that anything went wrong. This is why Part III's grounding requirement (every extracted value must quote verbatim source text) is not optional decoration.

Rung 4 · Vision-LLM structured extraction

The fourth rung skips "text" entirely: you send the page image and a schema, and get back the fields you actually want. It collapses OCR + layout + field mapping into one call, which is why it is so seductive — and why it needs the most discipline. Multimodal mechanics (image encoding, resolution, token math, multi-page handling) live in AI11; here we care about the pipeline shape.

Tables: the hard problem, treated separately

Tables deserve their own section because they fail differently from everything else. Prose that parses imperfectly is still mostly readable — an embedding still matches, a human still understands. A table that parses imperfectly produces confidently wrong numbers. And numbers are exactly what a finance, procurement, or compliance user came for.

Borderless tables

No ruling lines — column boundaries exist only as whitespace. Line-detection parsers see one paragraph. Whitespace-clustering (pdfplumber's text strategy) or a layout model is required.

Merged & spanning cells

A header spanning three columns, or a row label spanning four rows. Flat text output silently duplicates or drops the span, shifting every value one column left.

Multi-page continuation

A 400-row table split across 9 pages, header repeated on each. Naive chunking produces 8 chunks of numbers with no column names attached — unretrievable and uninterpretable.

Nested / hierarchical rows

Indentation encodes a parent/child relationship (subtotals under categories). Losing the indent turns a sub-line into a peer of its own total — a classic double-count bug.

Three defensive habits: (1) never split a table across chunks — keep it whole or summarise it; (2) repeat the header row on every table chunk if you must split; (3) serialise tables to Markdown or CSV, not to prose. An LLM reads a Markdown pipe-table far more reliably than it reads a space-aligned dump, and the serialisation is trivially diffable against ground truth in Lab 3. 

# pdfplumber: two strategies, radically different results on the same page
import pdfplumber
with pdfplumber.open("financials.pdf") as pdf:
    page = pdf.pages[4]
    ruled  = page.extract_tables()                                  # needs lines
    loose  = page.extract_tables({"vertical_strategy": "text",
                                  "horizontal_strategy": "text"})   # whitespace
    print(len(ruled), len(loose))     # e.g. 0 3  → the table is borderless
Part III · Pipeline design

Classify → route → extract → validate → review

A production document pipeline is not one extractor. It is a router in front of several extractors, with a validator behind them and a human queue behind that. The router is what lets you spend $0 on the 70% of pages that are clean and $0.02 on the 5% that are genuinely hard — a 10–20× cost difference on a large corpus.

The classifier: cheap, deterministic, page-level

Resist the urge to use an LLM here. Ninety percent of routing decisions are answerable with three deterministic signals, at zero cost and microsecond latency: 

def classify_page(page) -> str:
    text = page.extract_text() or ""
    n_chars  = len(text.strip())
    n_images = len(page.images)
    area     = page.width * page.height
    img_area = sum(i["width"] * i["height"] for i in page.images)

    if n_chars >= 200:                       return "native"
    if img_area > 0.7 * area and n_chars < 50: return "scan"
    if n_chars < 50 and n_images == 0:       return "blank"
    return "mixed"                            # hybrid page → OCR + native, merge

Add a document-type classifier only when routing depends on semantics (invoice vs contract vs statement). That one can be an LLM call — but use claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 on the first page's text, not sonnet on all pages, and cache the result by file hash (PR2).

Structured extraction: schema + grounding + confidence

For field extraction — invoices, purchase orders, claim forms — the output is not text but a record. Three properties separate a demo extractor from a production one:

  • A schema, enforced by code. Pydantic + structured outputs means a malformed date or a string where a decimal belongs fails loudly at the boundary instead of silently entering the ERP.
  • Verbatim grounding. Every field carries the exact substring it came from. If that substring is not present in the source text, the value is a hallucination — and you can detect that mechanically, without a judge model. This one check catches the majority of vision-LLM invention.
  • Per-field confidence. Not one confidence for the document. The vendor name may be obvious while the tax ID is half-cut-off at the page edge; a single score averages away exactly the signal the review queue needs.
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field
from decimal import Decimal
from datetime import date

class Extracted(BaseModel):
    value: str | None
    quote: str | None = Field(description="EXACT substring copied from the document")
    confidence: float = Field(ge=0, le=1)

class Invoice(BaseModel):
    invoice_number: Extracted
    invoice_date:   Extracted
    vendor_name:    Extracted
    total_amount:   Extracted
    currency:       Extracted

SYSTEM = (
    "Extract the fields from this invoice. For every field:\n"
    "- `quote` MUST be an exact substring of the document, copied character for character.\n"
    "- If the field is absent or unreadable, set value=null, quote=null, confidence=0.\n"
    "- confidence reflects how legible and unambiguous the source text is.\n"
    "NEVER infer, compute, or complete a value that is not printed on the page."
)

Then the check that makes all of it real — three lines that convert "trust the model" into "verify the model": 

import re
def norm(s): return re.sub(r"\s+", " ", (s or "")).strip().lower()

def ungrounded(inv: Invoice, page_text: str) -> list[str]:
    hay = norm(page_text)
    return [name for name, f in inv.model_dump().items()
            if f["value"] and (not f["quote"] or norm(f["quote"]) not in hay)]
# any field returned here was invented, no matter how confident the model claimed to be

Two paths from the same parse

The same normalized element tree feeds two very different downstream shapes, and conflating them is a common design mistake:

Chunking-for-RAG path
  • Unit of work = a chunk; goal = retrievability
  • Keep section paths as chunk prefixes; keep tables whole
  • Lossy is acceptable — a slightly imperfect chunk still retrieves
  • Measured by recall@k (AI4)
Field-extraction path
  • Unit of work = a record; goal = correctness
  • Schema-validated, grounded, per-field confidence
  • Lossy is not acceptable — a wrong total is a wrong payment
  • Measured by per-field accuracy vs labels (Part IV)
Score-3 move: when a customer says "we want to chat with our invoices", ask whether the real job is answering questions or populating a system of record. Nine times in ten it is the second, and the correct build is a field-extraction pipeline with a review queue — not RAG. Naming that distinction in week one saves a month.
Part IV · Quality & scale

Measure it, clean it, run it at volume

Extraction eval: the numbers that matter

You cannot improve what you don't label. The good news: a 50-document labelled set is usually enough to make every subsequent decision evidence-based, and labelling 50 invoices takes one focused afternoon. Use these metrics, per field and per document type:

MetricDefinitionTarget to argue for
Field accuracyExact match after normalisation (dates → ISO, amounts → Decimal, whitespace collapsed)≥ 95% on critical fields; report per field
Null-vs-wrong splitOf the errors, how many were "didn't find it" vs "found the wrong thing"Wrong ≪ null. A miss is recoverable; a confident error is not
Grounding failure rate% of returned values whose quote is not in the source text< 1%. Above that, your prompt or your OCR is broken
Confidence calibrationAccuracy within each confidence bucket (0.9–1.0, 0.7–0.9, …)Monotonic. If the 0.95 bucket isn't more accurate than the 0.7 bucket, confidence is noise
Table cell accuracyCorrect cells ÷ total cells, after aligning to the ground-truth grid≥ 90% simple, ≥ 75% complex — and know which you have
Straight-through rate% of documents auto-accepted with no human touchThe business metric. This is what pays for the project (FD4)

Calibration deserves a moment. The whole review-queue design rests on the assumption that low confidence predicts errors. Verify it once, explicitly: bucket your eval predictions by confidence and print accuracy per bucket. If the curve is flat, your threshold is arbitrary and you are routing documents to humans at random.

Low-quality scan preprocessing

Before you upgrade to a more expensive OCR engine, try fixing the pixels. Preprocessing is cheap, local, and on genuinely bad scans routinely moves Tesseract accuracy by 10–25 points — sometimes making the expensive rung unnecessary:

Upscale to 300 DPISmall type rendered at 150 DPI or belowLargest single win — often +10–15 points

import cv2, numpy as np, pytesseract

def prep(path):
    img  = cv2.imread(path, cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
    img  = cv2.resize(img, None, fx=2, fy=2, interpolation=cv2.INTER_CUBIC)  # ~150→300 DPI
    img  = cv2.fastNlMeansDenoising(img, h=10)
    return cv2.adaptiveThreshold(img, 255, cv2.ADAPTIVE_THRESH_GAUSSIAN_C,
                                 cv2.THRESH_BINARY, 31, 10)

raw  = pytesseract.image_to_string(cv2.imread("scan.png"))
fixed = pytesseract.image_to_string(prep("scan.png"))
print(len(raw), len(fixed))   # character count is a crude but fast proxy — then diff vs truth

Do this arithmetic before the architecture review, not after. A worked example for 100,000 pages/month, with routing:

Two further levers: the Batch API halves token cost for anything that can wait (overnight backfills are the canonical case — PR3), and idempotent, resumable jobs keyed by file hash mean a re-run after a crash costs nothing for pages already done. Backfills of a 2M-page archive are normal; design for restart from the first line of code.

The human review queue is part of the product

No document pipeline reaches 100%. The design question is not "how do we avoid human review" but "how do we make each human minute worth the most". Four decisions define a good queue:

  1. Route by field, not by document. If four fields are confident and one is not, show the reviewer one field — not a blank form. This is the difference between 20 seconds and 90 seconds.
  2. Show the evidence. Render the source page with the grounding quote highlighted. A reviewer who must hunt for the value in a 12-page PDF is doing the extraction themselves.
  3. Make thresholds a config value, not a constant. The business will want to trade straight-through rate against error rate — quarterly, and during month-end close.
  4. Every correction is a label. Write reviewer edits back as ground truth. Six months of review is a free, perfectly-distributed eval set — and the input to any fine-tuning conversation (AI10).

In the field

Logistics customer, ~8,000 supplier invoices a month, three people keying them into the ERP. The ask: "automate invoice entry with AI."

The score-1 move is to wire every PDF straight into a vision model and demo it on the three cleanest invoices in the folder. It looks spectacular. It also costs $1,900/month at full volume and nobody knows its error rate.

The score-2 FDE spends the first day sampling instead. Result: 62% of invoices are digital PDFs from four big suppliers with near-identical layouts, 31% are scans, 7% are photos from a driver's phone. She labels 40 invoices, builds the routed pipeline, and measures: field accuracy 97.4% on the digital majority, 88% on scans, 71% on phone photos. Confidence is monotonic across buckets, so a 0.90 threshold gives a 64% straight-through rate with a measured 0.4% error rate on auto-accepted documents.

The report she brings to the steering meeting has three numbers on it: $150/month in extraction cost, 64% of invoices needing no human at all, and 4.1 review-hours per 1,000 documents versus 25 today. Nobody argues about the model. They argue about whether to raise the threshold to 0.85 next quarter — which is exactly the conversation you want a customer to be having.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Demoing on the three cleanest files

The customer's folder is sorted by name, and the first files are always the nicest. Sample randomly, and deliberately include the ugliest scan you can find — in the demo.

✗ Reporting a single accuracy number

"93% accurate" hides the 15% of documents at 40%. Break it out by document type and by field, always. The averaged number is the one that gets you fired in month four.

✗ Converting DOCX/XLSX to PDF first

You are deleting perfect structure and then paying a model to guess it back. Parse Office formats natively — the headings, cells, and slide order are already there.

✗ Trusting vision-LLM output without grounding

It will fill in a plausible digit for an illegible one and never tell you. Require a verbatim quote for every field and reject anything not found in the source text.

✓ Route before you extract

A 20-line deterministic page classifier in front of four extractors is the highest-ROI code in the whole pipeline — typically 10–13× cheaper than sending everything to the strongest model.

✓ Turn review corrections into labels

Wire the review UI to write ground truth on day one. Six months later you have a real-distribution eval set that cost nothing — and the strongest possible evidence of improvement.

Extractor A — pypdf (rung 1). The zero-cost baseline: 
import time, pypdf, pathlib

def parse_pypdf(path):
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    r = pypdf.PdfReader(path)

Lab 2 · ~3 h
Structured field extraction with grounding

🎯 Objective

Turn documents into records, not text — with a schema the code enforces, a verbatim quote behind every value, and a per-field confidence you have actually verified is meaningful. Then score it against labels you wrote yourself.

📦 Setup

1
Label 20 documents first. This is the unskippable part, and it takes about 45 minutes. Create labels.jsonl — one line per document, the truth as a human reads it: 
{"file":"inv_0001.pdf","invoice_number":"INV-2026-00318","invoice_date":"2026-03-14","vendor_name":"Acme Logistics Ltd","total_amount":"1340.00","currency":"EUR"}
{"file":"inv_0002.pdf","invoice_number":"9921-B","invoice_date":"2026-02-28","vendor_name":"Nordwind GmbH","total_amount":"88.50","currency":"EUR"}
Normalisation rules, decided now and written down: dates as YYYY-MM-DD, amounts as plain decimals with no thousands separator, vendor name exactly as printed. Include at least 4 documents you find hard (a scan, a two-page invoice, one with a credit note, one in another language). A label set of only easy documents produces a number that lies to you.

🔬 Steps

2
Define the schema in extract.py — value + quote + confidence, per field: 
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field
import anthropic, base64, io, json, pathlib, re
from pdf2image import convert_from_path

client = anthropic.Anthropic()

class Field_(BaseModel):
    value: str | None = None
    quote: str | None = Field(default=None,
        description="EXACT substring copied from the document, character for character")
    confidence: float = Field(default=0.0, ge=0.0, le=1.0)

class Invoice(BaseModel):
    invoice_number: Field_
    invoice_date:   Field_    # normalized to YYYY-MM-DD in `value`
    vendor_name:    Field_
    total_amount:   Field_    # plain decimal, no separators
    currency:       Field_    # ISO 4217

SYSTEM = """Extract invoice fields from the page image.

For EVERY field:
- `quote` must be an EXACT substring visible on the page, copied character for character.
- `value` is the normalized form: dates as YYYY-MM-DD, amounts as plain decimals
  (no thousands separators), currency as an ISO 4217 code.
- If a field is absent or illegible, set value=null, quote=null, confidence=0.
- `confidence` reflects how legible and unambiguous the printed source is,
  NOT how plausible the value seems.

NEVER infer, compute, or complete a value that is not printed on the page.
A missing field is correct. An invented field is a failure."""
3
Extract with structured outputs. The schema is enforced at the API boundary, so a malformed response fails loudly instead of silently: 
def png_b64(pdf, page=1, dpi=200):
    img = convert_from_path(pdf, dpi=dpi, first_page=page, last_page=page)[0]
    img.thumbnail((1568, 1568))
    buf = io.BytesIO(); img.save(buf, format="PNG")
    return base64.standard_b64encode(buf.getvalue()).decode()

def extract(pdf_path) -> tuple[Invoice, float]:
    msg = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens=1500, system=SYSTEM,
        output_config={"format": {"type": "json_schema",
                                  "schema": Invoice.model_json_schema()}},
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": [
            {"type": "image", "source": {"type": "base64",
             "media_type": "image/png", "data": png_b64(pdf_path)}},
            {"type": "text", "text": "Extract the invoice fields."}]}])
    u = msg.usage
    cost = u.input_tokens*3/1e6 + u.output_tokens*15/1e6
    text = next(b.text for b in msg.content if b.type == "text")
    return Invoice.model_validate_json(text), cost
4
Add the grounding check — the single highest-value 6 lines in this module. You need the page's text to check against; use whatever rung 1–3 gives you: 
import pypdf
def page_text(pdf_path):
    return (pypdf.PdfReader(pdf_path).pages[0].extract_text() or "")

def norm(s): return re.sub(r"\s+", " ", (s or "")).strip().lower()

def ungrounded_fields(inv: Invoice, src: str) -> list[str]:
    hay = norm(src)
    bad = []
    for name, f in inv.model_dump().items():
        if f["value"] and (not f["quote"] or norm(f["quote"]) not in hay):
            bad.append(name)
    return bad
On scans page_text returns nothing, so grounding can't be checked from the text layer — in that case run Tesseract on the same image and check against that. Note the asymmetry in your notes: grounding is cheap on digital documents and costs an OCR pass on scans. That is a real architectural constraint, and score-3 engineers surface it early.
5
Score against your labels — per field, split into null-vs-wrong: 
FIELDS = ["invoice_number","invoice_date","vendor_name","total_amount","currency"]
labels = [json.loads(l) for l in open("labels.jsonl")]

stats = {f: {"ok":0, "null":0, "wrong":0} for f in FIELDS}
buckets = {}   # confidence calibration
total_cost = 0.0

for lab in labels:
    inv, c = extract(f"docs/{lab['file']}")
    total_cost += c
    d = inv.model_dump()
    for f in FIELDS:
        got, want = d[f]["value"], lab[f]
        conf = d[f]["confidence"]
        hit = norm(got) == norm(want)
        stats[f]["ok" if hit else ("null" if got is None else "wrong")] += 1
        b = round(min(conf, 0.999) * 5) / 5          # 0.0/0.2/.../1.0
        buckets.setdefault(b, [0,0]); buckets[b][0] += hit; buckets[b][1] += 1

n = len(labels)
for f in FIELDS:
    s = stats[f]
    print(f"{f:16s} acc {s['ok']/n:5.1%}   null {s['null']/n:5.1%}   WRONG {s['wrong']/n:5.1%}")
print(f"\ncost/doc ${total_cost/n:.4f}")
print("\nconfidence calibration:")
for b in sorted(buckets):
    hit, tot = buckets[b]
    print(f"  conf ~{b:.1f}: {hit}/{tot} = {hit/tot:.0%}")
Expected shape on a decent corpus: invoice_number acc 95.0% · total_amount acc 90.0% · vendor_name acc 85.0%, and a calibration curve that rises. If the calibration curve is flat, stop and fix that before anything else — a review queue built on meaningless confidence is worse than no queue, because it creates false assurance.
6
Run one controlled experiment. Change exactly one variable and re-score. Good candidates, in order of expected payoff:
  • Render at 300 DPI instead of 200 (more input tokens — is it worth it?)
  • Send the Docling Markdown as text alongside the image (belt and braces)
  • Swap claude-sonnet-5 for claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 and compare accuracy against the ~3× cost saving
Record all three rows. The haiku comparison in particular is a conversation you will have on every engagement, and having your own number for it is worth a great deal.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Quotes never match the source → the model is normalising inside quote. Strengthen the prompt ("character for character, including punctuation and spacing") and check your norm() isn't too aggressive.
  • Every confidence comes back 0.95 → the model is pattern-matching the field rather than judging legibility. Ask it to state why in a scratch field, or calibrate by bucketing on a hard subset.
  • Dates come back in the source's format → your normalisation instruction is losing to the visual. Add one worked example of a reformat to the system prompt.
  • Amounts wrong by a factor of 1000 → European vs US separators. Say explicitly which convention the document uses, or extract the raw string and parse in Python.

Lab 3 · ~2 h
Table extraction drill & cell accuracy

🎯 Objective

Measure the thing almost nobody measures: cell accuracy. You will build ground truth for three real tables, run three extractors against them, and produce a number you can defend in a room full of finance people.

📦 Setup

1
Pick three tables of escalating nastiness from your corpus and hand-type each into a CSV under truth/:
  • truth/easy.csv — ruled borders, single header row, no merges
  • truth/medium.csv — borderless, or a spanning header
  • truth/hard.csv — merged cells, nested rows, or a page break mid-table

🔬 Steps

2
Write the scorer first, so you can't unconsciously tune the extractors to a moving target: 
import csv, re

def load(path):
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        return [[c.strip() for c in row] for row in csv.reader(f)]

def norm(c):
    c = re.sub(r"[\s,]", "", (c or "")).strip().lower()
    return c.rstrip(".0") if re.fullmatch(r"-?\d+\.\d+", c) else c

def cell_accuracy(pred, truth):
    """Align on the truth grid; missing predicted cells count as wrong."""
    total = sum(len(r) for r in truth)
    ok = 0
    for i, trow in enumerate(truth):
        prow = pred[i] if i < len(pred) else []
        for j, tcell in enumerate(trow):
            pcell = prow[j] if j < len(prow) else ""
            ok += norm(pcell) == norm(tcell)
    shape_ok = (len(pred) == len(truth) and
                all(len(pred[i]) == len(truth[i]) for i in range(min(len(pred), len(truth)))))
    return ok / total, shape_ok
Note the second return value. Shape correctness is as important as cell correctness: an extractor that drops a column gets a decent cell score while producing data that is completely unusable, because every value is now under the wrong header.

Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~3 h
End-to-end pipeline & benchmark report

🎯 Objective

Produce PR9's catalog evidence — "extraction pipeline benchmarked on real documents" — as one coherent package: a routed pipeline, a benchmark report broken out by document type, and a review-queue design with thresholds you can justify.

🔬 Steps

1
Wire the router. Deterministic, page-level, zero cost: 
# pipeline.py
import pypdf, pathlib, time, json

def classify(page) -> str:
    text = (page.extract_text() or "").strip()
    imgs = getattr(page, "images", []) or []
    if len(text) >= 200:                 return "native"
    if imgs and len(text) < 50:          return "scan"
    if not imgs and len(text) < 50:      return "blank"
    return "mixed"

def route(pdf_path):
    r = pypdf.PdfReader(pdf_path)
    kinds = [classify(p) for p in r.pages]
    return kinds, max(set(kinds), key=kinds.count)   # dominant page kind
Run it over your whole corpus and print the distribution. That histogram is the first slide of your report — customers are consistently surprised by their own numbers.
2
Assemble the extractors behind the router, each instrumented for time and cost. The instrumentation is not optional garnish — it is the entire point of the exercise: 
EXTRACTORS = {}          # kind -> callable(pdf) -> (text_or_record, cost_usd)

def timed(fn):
    def wrap(*a, **kw):
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        out, cost = fn(*a, **kw)
        return out, cost, time.perf_counter() - t0
    return wrap

EXTRACTORS["native"] = timed(lambda p: (parse_pypdf(p)[0], 0.0))
EXTRACTORS["scan"]   = timed(lambda p: (ocr_tesseract(p), 0.0))
EXTRACTORS["mixed"]  = timed(lambda p: (parse_docling(p), 0.0))
EXTRACTORS["form"]   = timed(lambda p: extract_vision(p))     # from Lab 2

def run(pdf_path, kind):
    out, cost, secs = EXTRACTORS[kind](pdf_path)
    return {"file": pathlib.Path(pdf_path).name, "kind": kind,
            "cost_usd": round(cost, 5), "seconds": round(secs, 2),
            "chars": len(out) if isinstance(out, str) else None}
3
Add validation + confidence routing. Three gates, in this order — cheapest first: 
CRITICAL = ["invoice_number", "total_amount", "invoice_date"]

def decide(record, src_text, threshold=0.90):
    d = record.model_dump()
    bad = ungrounded_fields(record, src_text)          # Lab 2
    if bad:
        return "review", f"ungrounded: {','.join(bad)}"
    missing = [f for f in CRITICAL if not d[f]["value"]]
    if missing:
        return "review", f"missing critical: {','.join(missing)}"
    low = [f for f in CRITICAL if d[f]["confidence"] < threshold]
    if low:
        return "review", f"low confidence: {','.join(low)}"
    return "auto", "ok"
Then measure the two numbers the business cares about, on your labelled set: straight-through rate (share auto-accepted) and error rate among auto-accepted documents. The second one is the one that matters — an 80% straight-through rate with 6% errors is worse than 60% with 0.3%, and your job is to make that trade explicit rather than to maximise one number.

for t in (0.70, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, 0.95):

Expected shape: thr 0.80 STP 78.0% err-on-auto 2.10% … thr 0.95 STP 41.0% err-on-auto 0.20%. That table is worth more than any accuracy figure, because it converts an engineering knob into a business decision.
