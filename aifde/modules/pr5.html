Data Privacy & Compliance

GDPR/SOC2/HIPAA awareness, data residency, zero-retention agreements, tenant isolation and audit logs — the vocabulary and the controls that let an AI pilot survive contact with a customer's security team.

Why it matters for an FDE: Enterprise deals live or die in the security review — an FDE must be able to answer the customer's questionnaire.
Part I · Why compliance is an FDE deal-gate

The security review is the real go/no-go

You built the prototype in two weeks. The business sponsor loves it. The pilot metrics are good (FD4). And then a spreadsheet arrives — 180 rows, columns for Answer, Evidence, Owner — and the deal stops moving for six weeks. That spreadsheet is a security review, and in enterprise AI it is the single most common place a working pilot dies. Not because the answers were bad, but because nobody could answer them, or the answers arrived three weeks late, or the architecture made an honest answer impossible.

The FDE is very often the person in the room who can answer. The security analyst asks "where does the prompt text physically go?" and the account executive looks at you. The legal counsel asks "does Anthropic train on our data?" and the customer's CISO looks at you. You will not be the company's Data Protection Officer — that is a real role with real legal accountability — but you are the person who knows what the system actually does with the bytes, and that knowledge is what the review is buying.

Where compliance touches the engagement

The arrow is the whole lesson: compliance is a design input, not paperwork you attach at the end. Data residency is a deployment decision made in week one. Redaction is an architectural layer. Audit logging is a schema. Retrofitting any of them means re-architecting in front of an unhappy customer, which is exactly the moment you have the least credibility to ask for time.

You do not need to be a lawyer. You need to know which acronym implies which engineering obligation, so that when it appears in a call you can say something useful instead of "I'll check".

Part II · The data lifecycle lens

Follow the data — every hop is a compliance question

Almost every question on a security questionnaire is really the same question asked six ways: where does our data go, who can see it, how long does it stay, and can we get it deleted? So the FDE's core artifact is a data-flow map. Not an architecture diagram of boxes and arrows — a map annotated with what class of data crosses this arrow, where it lands physically, how long it lives there, and who can read it.

The five questions that matter

Training, retention, and what "zero retention" means

These three are constantly conflated. Keep them separate in your head and you will sound like the most competent person on the call:

  • Training — does the provider use your inputs/outputs to improve models? For major commercial APIs (Anthropic, and the enterprise tiers of other vendors), the default answer for API traffic is no. This is a contractual statement, so the artifact is a link to the terms or trust center, not your word.
  • Retention — does the provider store the request at all, and for how long? Many providers keep a short window for abuse monitoring. Zero-data-retention (ZDR) configurations remove that window and are typically arranged for enterprise accounts. Note the trade-off: some features that need server-side state (long-lived caches, some agent/session products, certain models) may be unavailable under ZDR. That trade-off is exactly the kind of thing an FDE should surface early rather than discover in week eight.
  • Logging you do — the one people forget. Your own traces, your error tracker, your analytics warehouse, your CI eval fixtures. In practice this is where more real prompt text sits than at the provider. Mask at the source (PR1).

Routing through a cloud platform changes the shape of all three. On Amazon Bedrock or Google Vertex AI, the request stays inside the customer's cloud account boundary and region, billing flows through their existing cloud agreement, and their existing cloud DPA/BAA often already covers it. For a customer who has spent two years getting comfortable with AWS, "it's a Bedrock call in your own account, in your own region" answers about fifteen questionnaire rows at once (PR6).

PII minimization: the cheapest control you own

The strongest privacy argument is we never sent it. Before any legal instrument, ask the engineering question: does the model actually need the customer's name, national ID, card number, and full address to do this task? Usually it needs the shape of the data, not the identity. Three techniques, in increasing order of cost:

Minimization

Don't put the field in the prompt at all. Select columns explicitly instead of SELECT * into a template. Free, and it removes the risk instead of managing it (FN5).

Reversible pseudonymization

Replace values with stable tokens ([[EMAIL_1]]), keep the mapping in your own store, restore in the response. The model reasons fine over tokens; the provider never sees the value. This is Lab 2.

Irreversible masking

Hash or drop. Correct for logs and traces, wrong for the live prompt when the answer must contain the value back. Different layers, different choices.

Know the limits

Detection is never 100%. Free-text notes hide identity in phrasing ("the patient from the Tuesday fire"). Report a measured recall number, never "we remove all PII" (FD6).

The right-to-deletion problem, honestly. Deleting a row is easy. Deletion becomes hard when the data has been transformed: an embedding derived from a paragraph containing a name, a summary written into a cache, a fine-tuning set (AI10), a 90-day backup. The workable design is lineage: every derived artifact stores the subject/source ID it came from, so "delete subject X" is a query and not an archaeology project. Decide this at ingestion or pay for it forever (AI4).

Paper instruments: DPA, BAA, SCCs

You will not draft these, but you must recognise them and know what they imply technically. A DPA (Data Processing Agreement) is the contract between a data controller (the customer) and a processor (you) describing purpose, duration, security measures, sub-processors, and deletion on termination — so your sub-processor list must be accurate. A BAA (Business Associate Agreement) is the HIPAA equivalent, and the practical rule is stark: PHI may only flow to parties covered by a BAA. SCCs (Standard Contractual Clauses) are the mechanism that legitimises EU→non-EU transfers when no adequacy decision applies — which is why "can we run inference in-region?" is worth engineering effort: it can remove the transfer entirely instead of papering it.

Part IV · Running a security review

The questionnaire genre — and how to win it

Security questionnaires come in recognisable shapes: the SIG (Standardized Information Gathering) family, CAIQ (Cloud Security Alliance's Consensus Assessments Initiative Questionnaire), the customer's own homegrown spreadsheet, and increasingly an AI addendum bolted on the end. They differ in vocabulary; the sections are nearly always the same:

1 · Data handling

What classes of data, collected how, stored where, encrypted how, retained how long, deleted when. Your data-flow map (Lab 1) answers this whole block.

2 · Access & identity

Who can reach production data, MFA, least privilege, offboarding, key rotation, separation of environments.

3 · Sub-processors & vendors

The named list, with regions, purposes, and each one's own attestations. Incomplete lists are the classic finding.

4 · Incident response

How you detect, who is notified, in what timeframe, and how you communicate. Ties directly to your runbooks (PR11).

5 · AI-specific

Model training on customer data, prompt retention, hallucination handling, human oversight, evaluation evidence (AI9), guardrails (PR4).

6 · Business continuity

Backups, RTO/RPO, provider outage behaviour — where a fallback model story is a genuinely good answer (PR3).

Answering honestly and still winning

  • Evidence over adjectives. "We take security very seriously" scores zero. "Prompts are redacted before egress; here is the redaction test report showing 96% recall on our labelled set" scores. Every answer should point to an artifact: a config, a trace, a test result, a policy, a link.
  • Never guess. A wrong "yes" discovered later is a trust event, not a correction. Mark it pending — owner: legal and move on. Reviewers accept unknowns; they do not accept surprises.
  • Use trust centers. Providers publish their compliance posture, sub-processors, and certifications. Linking the source is stronger than paraphrasing it, and it does not go stale in your document.
  • Answer the intent, not the words. A row written for a SaaS CRM ("do you support SAML SSO for admin console access?") may not map to your embedded deployment. Answer what they're protecting against, and say why the literal control doesn't apply.
  • Escalate deliberately. Legal questions (liability, indemnity, jurisdiction) go to legal. Certification questions go to whoever owns the SOC 2. Your lane is what the system does with data — own it completely and hand off the rest fast.

The FDE's compliance one-pager

Long before the formal questionnaire, someone will ask "so… is this safe?" in a meeting. Have a one-pager ready. It pre-empts half the review and makes the other half faster: 

# AI feature — data & privacy summary            v1.2 · 2026-08-__

WHAT DATA           support tickets (may contain customer name, email, order id)
CLASSES             PII: name, email · no payment data · no health data
WHERE IT GOES       app (eu-central-1) → redaction gate → Bedrock (eu-central-1)
TRAINING            provider does not train on API inputs/outputs  [link]
RETENTION           provider: zero-retention path enabled
                    our traces: 30 days, prompt fields masked
                    app logs: no prompt text (enforced by log filter)
DELETION            by subject id across tickets, traces, embeddings ≤ 7 days
                    backups expire ≤ 35 days
SUB-PROCESSORS      AWS (hosting + Bedrock, EU) · Langfuse self-hosted (EU)
ISOLATION           tenant_id enforced in data layer; ACL filter before ranking
AUDIT               every request logged: user, tenant, prompt version, model
KNOWN LIMITS        PII detection recall measured at 96% on our test set;
                    free-text notes may contain unstructured identifiers
OPEN ITEMS          DPA countersignature (legal) · pen-test scheduled Q4

Note the KNOWN LIMITS line. Including it is counter-intuitive and it is the reason the document works: it signals that the rest of the page is measured rather than aspirational.

Part V · Living with it

Compliance is a state, not an event

Passing the review is the start. Systems drift: someone adds a new sub-processor for error tracking, someone enables verbose logging to debug an incident and never turns it off, someone points the eval harness at production traffic and commits the fixtures. Three habits keep the state true:

  • Re-run the data-flow map when the architecture changes. New store, new vendor, new region → update the map and the one-pager the same day. It takes twenty minutes; reconstructing it under audit takes days.
  • Test the controls, don't assert them. A cross-tenant leak test and a redaction test in CI (PR7, PR10) turn a claim into a continuously verified fact. This is the single biggest jump from score 2 to score 3.
  • Know the incident path before the incident. If prompt text with PII lands in a log store it shouldn't, who do you tell, how fast, and how do you purge it? Write those three lines now (PR11).
A note on scope. This is a supporting skill: you are not becoming the compliance owner. The bar is that you can map your own system's data flows, apply the standard controls, fill a questionnaire with defensible answers under review, and know precisely where your competence ends. That boundary — stated out loud — is itself a professional signal.

In the field

Insurance customer in the EU. Eight-week pilot, users happy, sponsor ready to sign. Then the security questionnaire arrives — 140 rows — and the account exec forwards it with "can you take a look?"

The score-1 move is to answer from memory and hope. The score-2 FDE spends the first afternoon building the data-flow map instead of answering anything, and finds three things nobody had noticed: the error tracker was capturing full request bodies (prompts with policyholder names, retained 90 days), the eval fixtures in the repo were real customer tickets, and inference was running in us-east-1 because that was the default in the tutorial they started from.

Three fixes — a log scrubber, synthetic fixtures, and switching to an in-region endpoint — take four days. The questionnaire then answers itself, with evidence, and the "AI-specific" section gets a link to the eval report from AI9. The reviewer's written comment: "most complete vendor response we've had this year."

The lesson is not that the FDE knew privacy law. It is that they knew where the bytes went — and they looked before they answered.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Answering from memory

Every wrong answer is discovered eventually, and it costs more trust than the delay would have. Verify in the config, then answer.

✗ Forgetting your own logs

Teams obsess over the provider's retention while their error tracker stores full prompts for 90 days. Audit your side first — that's where the data actually is.

✗ "We remove all PII"

Detection is statistical. Say "we detect and tokenize these classes, measured at X% recall on our test set" — a claim you can defend under follow-up questioning.

✗ Compliance as a final phase

Residency, isolation and lineage are architectural. Bolting them on after the pilot means re-ingesting everything, in public, on the customer's clock.

✓ Ask for the questionnaire early

In discovery, ask "what will your security review need from us?" Getting the spreadsheet in week one turns a six-week blocker into a design constraint.

✓ Keep an evidence library

One folder: architecture diagram, data-flow map, retention table, sub-processor list, redaction report, leak test output. The next questionnaire takes hours instead of weeks.

4
Lint the map mechanically. A tiny checker catches the classic gaps and turns the map into something you can re-run after any architecture change: 
# check_dataflow.py — run: python check_dataflow.py dataflow.yaml
import sys, yaml

REQUIRED = ["name", "kind", "classes", "location", "retention", "readers"]

def main(path):
    doc = yaml.safe_load(open(path))
    known = {c["id"] for c in doc["data_classes"]} | {"UNKNOWN"}
    problems = []

    for hop in doc["hops"]:
        for field in REQUIRED:
            if field not in hop:
                problems.append(f"{hop.get('name','?')}: missing '{field}'")
        for c in hop.get("classes", []):
            if c not in known and not c.endswith("_redacted"):
                problems.append(f"{hop['name']}: undeclared class '{c}'")
        if "UNKNOWN" in hop.get("classes", []):
            problems.append(f"{hop['name']}: data class UNKNOWN — investigate")
        if hop.get("kind") == "store" and hop.get("retention", "").strip() in ("", "forever", "n/a"):
            problems.append(f"{hop['name']}: store with no retention limit")
        if hop.get("kind") == "egress" and "trains_on_data" not in hop:
            problems.append(f"{hop['name']}: egress without a training statement")

    # residency consistency: flag stores outside the declared home region
    home = doc.get("home_region", "eu-central-1")
    for hop in doc["hops"]:
        loc = hop.get("location", "")
        if "aws" in loc or "us-" in loc or "eu-" in loc:
            if home not in loc and hop["kind"] != "ingress":
                problems.append(f"{hop['name']}: outside home region ({loc})")

    if problems:
        print(f"❌ {len(problems)} finding(s):")
        for p in problems: print("  -", p)
        sys.exit(1)
    print("✅ data-flow map complete and consistent")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main(sys.argv[1])
Expected on a first run: ❌ 3 finding(s) or similar. That is a successful lab, not a failure.
5

# scrub request bodies before they reach any external error tracker
SENSITIVE_KEYS = {"prompt", "messages", "input", "body", "content", "email"}

def scrub(event):
    def walk(node):
        if isinstance(node, dict):
            return {k: ("[redacted]" if k.lower() in SENSITIVE_KEYS else walk(v))
                    for k, v in node.items()}
        if isinstance(node, list):
            return [walk(v) for v in node]
        return node
    return walk(event)

# wire as the SDK's before_send hook, then re-raise a test error
# and confirm in the tracker UI that no prompt text appears

Lab 2 · ~1.5 h
The redaction gate

🎯 Objective

Build a reversible PII redaction layer that sits between your application and the model, then measure what it misses — so you can state a defensible recall number in a questionnaire instead of an adjective.

🔬 Steps

1
Detector + reversible vault. Create redact.py. The design goal is that the model sees stable placeholders it can reason about, and only your process can map them back: 
import re, json
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

# Order matters: match the most specific patterns first.
PATTERNS = [
    ("EMAIL",  re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.-]+")),
    ("PHONE",  re.compile(r"\b(?:\+84|0)(?:[ .-]?\d){8,10}\b")),
    ("CARD",   re.compile(r"\b(?:\d[ -]?){13,19}\b")),
    ("NATID",  re.compile(r"\b\d{12}\b")),
    ("IBAN",   re.compile(r"\b[A-Z]{2}\d{2}[A-Z0-9]{10,30}\b")),
    ("IPV4",   re.compile(r"\b(?:\d{1,3}\.){3}\d{1,3}\b")),
]

def luhn_ok(digits: str) -> bool:
    d = [int(c) for c in digits if c.isdigit()][::-1]
    if len(d) < 13: return False
    total = 0
    for i, n in enumerate(d):
        if i % 2 == 1:
            n *= 2
            if n > 9: n -= 9
        total += n
    return total % 10 == 0

@dataclass
class Vault:
    """Reversible token map. Lives in YOUR process, never leaves it."""
    fwd: dict = field(default_factory=dict)   # value -> token
    rev: dict = field(default_factory=dict)   # token -> value

    def token_for(self, kind: str, value: str) -> str:
        if value in self.fwd:
            return self.fwd[value]
        n = sum(1 for t in self.rev if t.startswith(f"[[{kind}_")) + 1
        tok = f"[[{kind}_{n}]]"
        self.fwd[value] = tok
        self.rev[tok] = value
        return tok

def redact(text: str, vault: Vault | None = None):
    vault = vault or Vault()
    out = text
    for kind, rx in PATTERNS:
        def sub(m):
            val = m.group(0)
            if kind == "CARD" and not luhn_ok(val):
                return val                      # order ids, not cards
            return vault.token_for(kind, val)
        out = rx.sub(sub, out)
    return out, vault

def restore(text: str, vault: Vault) -> str:
    for tok, val in vault.rev.items():
        text = text.replace(tok, val)
    return text

# measure.py — run: python measure.py
import json
from redact import redact

rows = [json.loads(l) for l in open("pii_testset.jsonl")]
tp = fn = fp = 0
misses = []

for r in rows:
    red, vault = redact(r["text"])
    caught = set(vault.fwd.keys())
    expected = set(r["expect"])

    tp += len(caught & expected)
    missed = expected - caught
    fn += len(missed)
    fp += len(caught - expected)
    if missed:
        misses.append((r["text"], sorted(missed)))

recall = tp / (tp + fn) if (tp + fn) else 1.0
precision = tp / (tp + fp) if (tp + fp) else 1.0
print(f"recall    {recall:.0%}   ({tp} caught / {tp+fn} expected)")
print(f"precision {precision:.0%}  ({fp} false positives)")
print("\nMISSES — these go in the 'known limits' section:")
for text, m in misses:
    print(f"  - {m} in: {text[:60]}")
A typical first run: recall 82% · precision 94%. Do not tune until it says 100% — you would only be overfitting to seven lines. Tune until the structured classes are reliably caught and the misses are all genuinely hard.

  • Phone regex swallowing order numbers → tighten the boundary, add the negative case to the test set, re-measure.

# lint_answers.py — run: python lint_answers.py answers.yaml
import sys, re, yaml

VAGUE = re.compile(
    r"\b(we take .* seriously|industry[- ]standard|best practice|robust|"
    r"military[- ]grade|state of the art|fully secure|100%|all pii)\b", re.I)

def main(path):
    doc = yaml.safe_load(open(path))
    rows = [r for s in doc["sections"].values() for r in s]
    problems, stats = [], {"answered": 0, "partial": 0, "pending": 0, "n/a": 0}

    for r in rows:
        stats[r.get("status", "pending")] = stats.get(r.get("status"), 0) + 1
        ans = (r.get("answer") or "").strip()

        if not ans:
            problems.append(f"{r['id']}: empty answer")
        if VAGUE.search(ans):
            problems.append(f"{r['id']}: vague marketing language — be specific")
        if r.get("status") == "answered" and not r.get("evidence"):
            problems.append(f"{r['id']}: claims 'answered' with no evidence file")
        if r.get("status") == "pending" and not r.get("owner"):
            problems.append(f"{r['id']}: pending with no owner")
        if r.get("status") == "pending" and not r.get("due"):
            problems.append(f"{r['id']}: pending with no due date")
        if len(ans.split()) > 120:
            problems.append(f"{r['id']}: answer too long — reviewers skim")

    print(f"rows: {len(rows)}  " +
          "  ".join(f"{k}={v}" for k, v in stats.items()))
    if problems:
        print(f"\n❌ {len(problems)} issue(s):")
        for p in problems: print("  -", p)
        sys.exit(1)
    print("\n✅ questionnaire is defensible")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main(sys.argv[1])
