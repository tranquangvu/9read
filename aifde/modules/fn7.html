AI-assisted Development

Use AI coding tools — Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot — and agentic workflows effectively and safely: right-sized task decomposition, ruthless review discipline, and secure handling of customer code. The multiplier that makes FDE delivery timelines possible.

Why it matters for an FDE: FDE speed expectations (working demo in days) assume AI-assisted development — engineers who master these tools deliver several times faster. The ones who don't aren't slightly slower; they miss the window in which the customer's attention, budget and champion are all still aligned.
Part I · The new development loop

Why FDE economics assume AI assistance

Do the math on a typical engagement. Kickoff Monday, discovery Tuesday, and the promise on the table is a working demo on the customer's data by Friday. Under manual development, that demo is two to three weeks of work: scaffold a service, wire auth against an SSO you've never touched, integrate a vendor API from patchy docs, build an eval-able prototype, put a presentable UI on it. Nobody scoped a three-week discovery week. The gap is closed by AI-assisted development — and the entire commercial model (team size, pricing, "land in days not quarters") is built on the assumption that you close it. This is why FN7 is a critical skill and not a productivity nicety: it is load-bearing for the job.

The honest framing matters as much as the enthusiasm. These tools are a multiplier on engineers who can specify, decompose and review — and a liability amplifier on engineers who can't. Everything in this module is about becoming the first kind.

The capability ladder: three tool classes, three contracts

"AI coding tools" hides three genuinely different kinds of software. Each has a different unit of work, needs different input from you, and fails in different ways. Most engineers who "tried AI and it didn't help" used one class with the workflow of another.

Where the gains actually come from — and where they don't

The multiplier is not uniform across work. It is very large where the solution is well-trodden and the bottleneck was typing and doc-reading; it approaches zero — or goes negative — where the bottleneck is deciding what to build:

✓ Big multiplier (3–10×)
  • Boilerplate & scaffolding: services, CRUD, config, CI files
  • Unfamiliar APIs/frameworks — the FDE's daily reality on customer stacks
  • Test suites, fixtures, mocks for existing code
  • Mechanical migrations & refactors (rename, port, upgrade)
  • Glue code, data wrangling, one-off scripts, demo UIs
✗ Small or negative
  • Novel architecture decisions — the model interpolates precedent; your problem may have none
  • Debugging that requires your mental model of the system's history and intent
  • Anything depending on tacit org knowledge that isn't written down anywhere
  • Hot-path performance work needing profiling and hardware intuition
  • Code you can't review — the speedup gets repaid as rework, with interest
Amdahl's law applies. If 60% of a task is AI-suitable at 5× and the rest is unchanged, the whole task speeds up ~2.1× — not 5×. Knowing this keeps your estimates honest and makes you the person who promises Friday and delivers Friday. Lab 1 gives you your own numbers.

Vibe coding is a real discipline, not a shortcut

"Vibe coding" — describing what you want and letting AI generate it — sounds like cheating. It isn't. Done well, it's the hardest form of software engineering because it shifts your effort from writing to designing, specifying, and verifying. The typing was never the hard part. Deciding what to type, in what order, with what contracts, guarded by what tests — that was always the job, and vibe coding surfaces it by removing the busywork layer that used to hide weak thinking.

The progression for an experienced developer is uncomfortable by design. Stage one: you prompt, it generates, you read the output and think "I would have done that differently." Stage two: you learn to specify well enough that it does it your way — faster than you could type it. Stage three: you realize the model sometimes does it better than your way, and you learn to recognize when to cede the implementation to it. That last stage is where the 5× lives. It requires taste, humility, and enough experience to know the difference between "different" and "wrong." Junior engineers struggle with stage one (they can't judge output they couldn't produce). Senior engineers struggle with stage three (they've spent a career trusting only their own hands). The FDE needs to reach stage three on a deadline — that's this module.

Claude Code under the hood — what happens when you press enter

Understanding the machinery makes you a better operator. When you type a task into Claude Code, here is the sequence, simplified but not caricatured:

Three things in this diagram are not obvious from using the tool. First, the loop is real — Claude Code runs in a tight agentic loop of read-think-act-observe, and each iteration costs tokens and time. A 15-step task (explore 3 files, write 4, run 2 commands, fix 2 failures, verify) can be 40+ API calls. Second, context is cumulative — every tool result, file read, and command output stays in the conversation, so a poorly scoped task bloats the context with irrelevant files and degrades later decisions (AI3 explains why this matters). Third, permissions are the safety boundary — Claude Code's permission system intercepts every filesystem write and shell command. A well-configured deny rule (Part IV) prevents disaster; defaults prevent nothing.

The single most valuable Claude Code habit: after every task, read the transcript (Claude Code saves them). Look for — what files did it read that it didn't need? What tool calls looped? What assumptions did it state in its thinking that turned out wrong? One transcript read per week will teach you more about prompt engineering than any course, because you're studying your own failure modes on your own codebase.

The timeline is not aspirational — it's measured from real engagement work. The speed doesn't come from the model typing fast; it comes from not doing the wrong thing first. The explore phase prevents architecture mismatch. The spec phase prevents scope creep. The plan-mode redirect catches design errors before they're code. The five-session discipline prevents context rot. The review catches what the plan missed. Every phase earns its place on the critical path, and skipping any one adds time — you just pay it later, in rework, at 2× the cost.

Task decomposition: the right-sized task

The single biggest predictor of agentic success is task size. Too big ("build the invoice-processing app") and the agent makes dozens of unreviewable design decisions for you. Too small ("rename this variable") and you're paying agent overhead for autocomplete work. The sweet spot is a reviewable PR's worth: one capability, touching a handful of files, verifiable by tests — 20 minutes to 2 hours of manual work. Decomposing to that grain is your architectural act; it's where the design thinking now lives.

Spec-first prompting means writing the acceptance criteria into the prompt before any code exists. Compare: 

# Weak — outsources every decision, invites plausible wrongness:
add rate limiting to the API

# Strong — a 12-line spec the agent can actually satisfy (and be checked against):
Add per-API-key rate limiting to the FastAPI service.
- Sliding window: 100 req/min per key; limit configurable via RATE_LIMIT_PER_MIN env var
- Use the existing Redis client in app/redis.py — do NOT add a new dependency
- On limit: HTTP 429 with JSON {"error": "rate_limited", "retry_after_s": <int>}
  and a Retry-After header
- Exempt /health and /metrics
- Add tests: under-limit passes, over-limit 429s, window resets, exempt paths
- Non-goals: no per-endpoint limits, no UI, don't touch auth middleware
Run the full test suite when done and show me the diff.

Acceptance criteria in the prompt do double duty: they steer generation and hand you the review checklist. Non-goals matter just as much — agents over-deliver by default, and unrequested "improvements" are where silent behavior changes hide (Part III).

Context provisioning: rules files and exemplars

The agent starts every session knowing nothing about your project's conventions. Two mechanisms fix that:

  • A project rules file — CLAUDE.md for Claude Code, .cursor/rules for Cursor — loaded automatically every session. Keep it short and operational: build/test commands, layout, conventions, hard "never do" rules. A useful skeleton: 
    # CLAUDE.md
## Commands
- test: `pytest -q`   · lint: `ruff check .`   · run: `make dev`
## Conventions
- Python 3.12, type hints everywhere; FastAPI + SQLAlchemy 2.x (async)
- Errors: raise domain exceptions from app/errors.py — never bare Exception
- New endpoints mirror app/api/orders.py (structure, auth, pagination)
## Never
- Never edit generated files in app/migrations/ by hand
- Never add dependencies without asking
- Secrets come from env vars only; nothing secret in code or fixtures
  • Exemplar files + "mirror this pattern." The highest-leverage prompt technique in existence: point the agent at your best example and say "read orders.py completely, then implement the same for refunds, mirroring its structure and quality bar exactly." One good exemplar beats three pages of style prose, because models are far better at imitating demonstrated patterns than following described ones. (Meta-note: the training site you are reading was built exactly this way — one hand-polished module page served as the exemplar, and every other page was generated against it with a "mirror this" instruction. You're looking at the technique's output.)

Running the loop: plan mode, checkpoints, honesty

  • Plan before code on anything non-trivial. Use plan/review-before-apply modes (Claude Code's plan mode, Cursor's diff review) so the first artifact you evaluate is a plan, not 400 lines of diff. Redirecting a plan costs one sentence; redirecting an implementation costs a rewrite.
  • The iterate-vs-takeover judgment. A practical two-strike rule: if two rounds of corrective feedback haven't converged, stop prompting. Either your spec is ambiguous (fix the spec), the task is in the "small or negative" column (do it yourself), or the agent is anchored on a wrong approach (start a fresh session with a better framing). Grinding through strike five is the most common way to lose your multiplier.
  • Keep the agent honest. Make verification part of the task: "run the tests and paste the output", "show me the full diff", "list the assumptions you made". Agents will report success optimistically; insist on evidence, not claims. If it says tests pass, the test output should be in the transcript.
  • Fresh sessions are cheap. Long agent sessions accumulate context rot just like long chats (FN2): stale assumptions and abandoned approaches linger in context and pollute later steps. One task, one session, with the durable knowledge promoted into CLAUDE.md — this is applied AI3 Context Engineering.

CLAUDE.md as infrastructure — the complete template

The Part II skeleton was a starter. Here is a production CLAUDE.md, annotated section by section. This file compounds: every hour invested in it pays back on every task, for every engineer on the project, forever. Treat it like an incident postmortem or an architectural decision record — version it, review changes to it, and never let it go stale. 

# CLAUDE.md — Production template for FDE engagements

## Build, Test, Lint, Run (ALWAYS keep these current)
# Claude Code reads this section to know how to verify its own work.
# If these commands drift, the agent's self-check becomes meaningless.
- install: `poetry install`                     # or pip install -e ".[dev]"
- test:    `pytest -q --cov=app --cov-report=term-missing`
- lint:    `ruff check app/ tests/`
- format:  `ruff format app/ tests/`
- run:     `uvicorn app.main:app --reload --port 8000`
- typecheck: `mypy app/`

## Architecture overview (3–5 bullets — enough to orient, not a novel)
- FastAPI 0.115+ on Python 3.12, async SQLAlchemy 2.x, PostgreSQL
- Auth: OIDC via middleware/app/middleware/auth.py; injects current_user
- Config: pydantic-settings from app/config.py; env-var driven (12-factor)
- Pattern: Router → Service → Repository; services own business logic
- Key files: app/main.py (entry), app/deps.py (DI), app/config.py (settings)

## Conventions (the "house style" — every PR must match)
- Every endpoint uses async def; every DB call is awaited
- Errors: raise domain exceptions from app/errors.py — never bare HTTPException
- Logging: structlog via app/logging.py; always include correlation_id
- New routes: mirror the structure of app/api/orders.py — it's the exemplar
- Naming: GET /things/:id → get_thing; POST /things → create_thing
- Migrations: Alembic via `alembic revision --autogenerate -m "…"`

## Exemplar files (mirror these for new work — the best docs are code)
- REST endpoint: app/api/orders.py     # complete CRUD pattern
- Service layer: app/services/orders.py
- Repository:   app/repos/orders.py
- Test file:    tests/test_orders.py    # full coverage pattern
- Config schema: app/schemas/orders.py  # pydantic v2 style

## Never (hard constraints — the agent will be told these are non-negotiable)
- NEVER edit files in app/migrations/versions/ by hand — use alembic
- NEVER add a dependency without explicit approval — no "helpful" pip installs
- NEVER change the auth middleware — it was reviewed by security; reroute, don't rewrite
- NEVER commit secrets, .env files, or credentials — even in test fixtures
- NEVER use bare Exception or print() — use app/errors.py and structlog
- NEVER change the database schema without a migration file
The exemplar trick, repeated because it's that important: "Mirror app/api/orders.py for the new refunds endpoint — same structure, same error handling, same test pattern" is the single most effective prompt in the entire Claude Code workflow. Models imitate far better than they follow instructions. One good exemplar eliminates three pages of prose conventions. If you only invest in one thing in your CLAUDE.md, make it the exemplar file list.

Permissions that protect without crippling

Claude Code's permission system sits between the agent and your machine. Every filesystem write, every shell command, every network request passes through it. The defaults are permissive — the agent can do most things after asking you — but production FDE work demands a configured safety net. Here is the configuration that ships in every FDE engagement workspace: 

# .claude/settings.json — production safety configuration for FDE engagements
{
  "permissions": {
    // Deny: secrets, credentials, sensitive customer data — ALWAYS blocked
    "deny": [
      "**/.env",
      "**/.env.*",
      "**/secrets/**",
      "**/credentials/**",
      "**/*-key.json",          // GCP service accounts
      "**/*.pem",               // private keys
      "**/.aws/**",             // AWS credentials
      "**/customer-data/**"     // engagement-specific PII dir
    ],
    // Allow: safe operations the agent does constantly — approve silently
    "allow": [
      "Bash(git diff:*)",
      "Bash(git log:*)",
      "Bash(git status:*)",
      "Bash(pytest:*)",
      "Bash(ruff:*)",
      "Bash(ls:*)",
      "Bash(cat:*)",
      "Bash(head:*)",
      "Bash(grep:*)",
      "Bash(find:*)",
      "Read(**)"
    ],
    // Default for anything not in deny or allow: ASK
    "defaultMode": "ask"
  }
}

The deny list is the safety net. The allow list is the ergonomics — without it, every git diff and ls triggers a permission prompt, and prompt fatigue is a security failure mode (you'll start approving everything). The defaultMode: "ask" means anything genuinely dangerous — writing files, running commands that modify state, making network calls — still requires your approval.

Never disable permissions entirely. Claude Code in "yolo mode" (no permission prompts) is convenient and dangerous — it's the equivalent of running curl | bash on generated code. On customer code, every write and every shell command must be visible to you. The five-second pre-prompt check (Part IV) plus configured permissions is the minimum viable safety posture.

The multi-session workflow for large features

One Claude Code session per task is the rule (Part II). But what about a feature that takes 8 hours and touches 15 files? You don't do it in one session — that's context rot. You don't do it in one giant spec — that's the "build the dashboard" anti-pattern. The professional pattern: one spec, decomposed into session-sized subtasks, with state passed through the filesystem:

  1. Write the architecture spec — one page: data model changes, new routes, integration surface, acceptance criteria. This is your work; the agent doesn't write architecture.
  2. Decompose into 3–7 subtask specs — each a ~15-line prompt in its own TASKS/01-*.md file, each producing a reviewable, commit-able chunk. Subtask 3 should never depend on subtask 2 being "mostly right" — each is independently verifiable.
  3. Session per subtask, fresh context each time. The CLAUDE.md and exemplar files carry the durable knowledge. The subtask spec carries the local intent. Nothing from session 2 pollutes session 3.
  4. Integration session last. After all subtasks are merged, one final session: "Run the full test suite. Trace every new code path end to end. List anything that doesn't match the architecture spec." This catches the cross-subtask bugs — mismatched assumptions, duplicate helpers, inconsistent naming.
When to break the "fresh session" rule: if a subtask reveals that the architecture spec was wrong (new constraint discovered, API limitation found), stop all subtasks, update the architecture spec, and restart from the affected subtask. Continuing with a known-wrong spec is the sunk-cost fallacy in code form.
Part III · Review discipline

You own every line

This is the section that separates professionals from passengers. When AI-assisted code breaks production, leaks data, or misbills a customer, git blame prints your name — "the AI wrote it" is not a sentence that exists in any postmortem, contract, or courtroom. The professional stance: an AI diff is an untrusted contribution from a prolific stranger, reviewed to the same bar as a human PR — and to a different checklist, because AI code fails differently than human code. Human bugs cluster around typos, off-by-ones and forgotten cases. AI failure modes are subtler: the code is idiomatic, well-commented, plausible — and wrong in ways designed (by the training objective itself) to look right.

The AI-code failure catalog

Learn these five patterns cold — they account for the large majority of AI-introduced defects you'll catch:

The review checklist for AI diffs

  1. Diff scope: does it touch only what the spec required? Every out-of-scope hunk is either reverted or consciously accepted — never skimmed.
  2. Run it: tests pass on your machine/CI, not in the agent's claim. Read the test output for warnings.
  3. API reality check: every unfamiliar method/argument verified against current docs or the actual library source — this is minutes, not hours.
  4. Behavior deltas: error handling, defaults, timeouts, ordering, logging — did any change that the spec didn't ask for?
  5. Security pass: injection, secrets, authz on new endpoints, input validation at trust boundaries.
  6. Tests actually assert: open the tests; would they fail if the feature broke? (See below.)
  7. Explain-back: can you explain every hunk to a reviewer? Any hunk you can't explain, you don't commit.

Tests as the guardrail — if they're real

The strongest agentic pattern available today: have the agent write the tests first, review the tests (small, readable, high-leverage), then let it implement until they pass. You review 40 lines of intent instead of 400 lines of implementation, and the agent gets an objective target instead of "make me happy".

But verify the tests assert. AI-generated suites are notorious for tests that exercise code without checking it — green by construction: 

# Looks like a test, tests nothing — passes even if rate limiting is deleted:
def test_rate_limiting():
    resp = client.get("/api/items", headers=KEY)
    assert resp is not None        # always true

# A real test — pins observable behavior to the spec:
def test_over_limit_returns_429_with_retry_after():
    for _ in range(100):
        assert client.get("/api/items", headers=KEY).status_code == 200
    resp = client.get("/api/items", headers=KEY)
    assert resp.status_code == 429
    assert resp.json()["error"] == "rate_limited"
    assert int(resp.headers["Retry-After"]) > 0

Cheap verification habit: mutation spot-check — break the implementation on purpose (flip a comparison, comment out the limit check) and confirm the suite goes red. Thirty seconds, and it has caught more hollow AI test suites than any linter. Systematic quality measurement of AI outputs is AI9's territory; this is the developer-loop version.

Commit hygiene with an agent: commit at every reviewed checkpoint, one logical change per commit, message stating intent ("add per-key rate limiting; 429 + Retry-After per spec") — not "AI changes". Never git add -A on an agent's working tree without reading git diff first; agents leave debug scripts and stray edits behind. Small reviewed commits are also your undo button when strike two hits and you want to roll back to the last good state.

The adversarial review — using Claude to review Claude

One Claude Code session wrote the code. A different Claude Code session can review it — and will find things the first session missed. This is not redundancy; it's independent verification. The reviewer session has no stake in the code being right; it starts from "prove to me this is wrong" rather than "confirm this looks good": 

# In a fresh Claude Code session — NOT the session that wrote the code:
Review this diff adversarially. Your job is to find problems, not to
confirm correctness. For each file changed:
1. Does any change go beyond what the spec required? Flag every out-of-scope hunk.
2. Identify every API call, method, or argument you're not 100% certain exists in
   the current version of that library. I'll verify each against real docs.
3. Examine error handling: what exception paths are now silently swallowed?
4. Check for: deprecated patterns, security regressions, race conditions, missing
   rollbacks, timezone assumptions, encoding assumptions.
5. Read every test — is there at least one assertion per acceptance criterion?
   Mark any test that would pass even if the feature were deleted.

Be maximally skeptical. False positives are fine; false negatives are not.
Output a table: file:line | severity (high/med/low) | what's wrong | fix suggestion

The adversarial review typically surfaces 1–3 additional findings beyond your own review — not because you're bad at reviewing, but because the model has a different failure-mode distribution than you do. It catches different things. The combination of human review (judgment, context, "this doesn't feel right") plus adversarial AI review (exhaustive, pattern-matching, never tired) is significantly more effective than either alone.

Why a separate session matters: within the same session, the model is anchored on its own previous output — it has a strong bias toward confirming its own work. Starting fresh breaks that anchor. Give the reviewer session only the diff and the spec; don't feed it the original session's thinking or intermediate output. Independence is what makes the review adversarial rather than ceremonial.

When to throw it away — recognizing unrecoverable generations

The two-strike rule says: after two rounds of corrective feedback without convergence, stop and re-evaluate. But sometimes you shouldn't even get to strike two. Learn the signals that a generation is unrecoverable — cheaper to restart fresh than to fix:

🚫 Wrong abstraction chosen

The agent picked a class hierarchy when the problem wants composition. Fixing this means rewiring every method call — you're essentially rewriting from scratch. Throw it away; in the fresh session, specify "use plain functions, no classes" explicitly.

🚫 Anchored on a hallucinated API

The first version used s3.get_object_metadata(). Your correction said "that method doesn't exist, use head_object." The fix: it renamed the call but kept the same argument structure — also hallucinated. The hallucination contaminated the design. Restart with the real API docs inline in the prompt.

🚫 Over-engineered from the root

A factory, a strategy pattern, and a plugin system for what should be three functions. You can surgically remove each abstraction, but they're interconnected — removing one breaks the others. Restart with "simplest possible implementation; no abstractions until there are 3+ callers."

🚫 Diff has sprawled beyond the spec

The spec said "add rate limiting." The agent also refactored the middleware order, renamed three functions, and "improved" error messages across the codebase. The out-of-scope changes are tangled with the real ones. Restart with explicit non-goals and a diff-size limit in the prompt.

The common thread: throwing away is faster than fixing when the error is structural, not local. A typo fixes in one line; a wrong architecture fixes in a full rewrite. The skill is recognizing which one you're looking at within the first minute of review. Experienced developers have this instinct for human code; with practice it transfers to AI-generated code, but the failure signatures are different (see catalog above) and you need to calibrate on the new patterns.

Part IV · Safety rails

Security & client-code hygiene

An FDE's tools touch the most sensitive thing a customer has: their code and their data. One careless prompt can turn a delivery engagement into a security incident review. Four rules, non-negotiable:

1 · Secrets never enter a prompt

API keys, connection strings, customer PII, .env contents, tokens in pasted logs — once in a prompt, they've left your machine, may be retained per the tool's policy, and can resurface in transcripts and telemetry. Treat prompts like a public pastebin: scrub before pasting (sk-ant-… → REDACTED), keep secrets in env vars the agent references by name, and add deny rules so agentic tools can't read secret files at all (Claude Code supports permission deny rules for exactly this — e.g. denying reads of .env and secrets/**). If a real secret does leak into a prompt: rotate it. Not "probably fine" — rotate it.

2 · Know what each tool sends where

Every cloud-backed coding tool transmits code to servers — that's how they work. What differs is which code, to whom, and what happens to it afterwards:

  • Retention & training: consumer tiers of these tools may retain data and (for some vendors) train on it; enterprise tiers offer zero-data-retention and no-training commitments. The tier is a contractual fact — verify the actual plan in force, not the marketing page.
  • The FDE rule: before running any AI tool against a customer repo, check their AI tooling policy and get the tool + tier approved in writing. Many enterprises allow a specific approved tool on an enterprise agreement and prohibit everything else. "I used my personal Cursor account on their codebase" is a contract breach in the making, not initiative.

3 · License contamination awareness

Models trained on public code can occasionally reproduce distinctive licensed snippets near-verbatim. Practical hygiene, not paranoia: enable duplication filters where offered (Copilot's public-code filter); be suspicious of large, oddly specific verbatim-looking blocks (a complete nontrivial algorithm with someone else's naming conventions and comments) and ask the agent to reimplement from a spec instead of accepting them; know whether the customer has a stated policy on AI-generated code in their IP. When their lawyers ask — and in regulated industries they do — "we followed your approved tooling and policy" is the only good answer.

4 · The customer-laptop boundary

Where the code lives decides whose rules apply. On customer-issued hardware or VDI, only their approved tools with their accounts — installing your own tooling on their machine is a policy violation even with good intentions. On your laptop with their code, their data-handling terms still apply: approved tools only, code stays in the engagement workspace, deleted at off-boarding per contract. Never move customer code across the boundary to "work on it properly at home", and never mix Customer A's code into a session where Customer B's code is in context. When any of this is unclear — ask before running. The engineer who asks looks careful; the engineer who assumed looks like an incident report.

The five-second pre-prompt check: before every paste into any AI tool ask — secrets in this text? whose code is this? is this tool approved for that code? If any answer is "not sure", stop. This one habit prevents essentially every tooling-related incident an FDE can cause.

The customer security conversation — script and evidence

The Part IV rule says "get approval in writing." But how do you actually have that conversation with a skeptical security team? Here is the script, field-tested across real engagements. It works because it doesn't argue — it provides evidence and lets the security team apply their own standards:

Step 1 — Understand their existing policy. "Does your team already have an approved AI coding tool or a policy on AI-assisted development? We want to work within whatever framework you have — if there's an enterprise agreement with a specific vendor, we'll use that." Most enterprises already have something — an approved Copilot enterprise plan, a Claude Code enterprise agreement, a blanket prohibition. Find out which one before proposing anything.

Step 2 — Provide the vendor's security documentation. Don't summarize it — send the actual data-processing addendum, SOC 2 report, and the zero-retention policy page for the enterprise tier. Let their security team read the primary source. For Anthropic: the Claude Code enterprise agreement includes zero data retention and no training on customer code — these are contractual facts, not marketing.

Step 3 — Describe your safety configuration. Show them the exact permission settings (deny rules, command sanitization hooks, network restrictions) from your workspace config. The message: "Here are the controls we've configured. Here's what the agent cannot do. Here are the logs we keep. Here's our off-boarding procedure."

Step 4 — Offer a trial scope. "Start with one repo, two engineers, one week. Here's what we'll measure (cycle time, defect rate, security findings in review). Here are the stop conditions. After the trial, you decide whether to expand, modify, or discontinue." This de-risks the decision — they're approving a measured experiment, not a blank check.

Step 5 — Get it in writing, and keep it. An email from the security lead or CTO that says "tool X on enterprise tier Y is approved for repo Z under the configured controls" is sufficient. Save it in the engagement workspace. If the approval changes or expands later, get that in writing too. The paper trail is not bureaucracy — it's your protection when someone asks "who approved this?" six months later.

The most common failure mode: engineer gets verbal approval from their day-to-day contact ("yeah, use whatever makes you faster"), doesn't loop in security, and six months later the security audit finds unapproved tooling in the codebase. Verbal approval from a non-security stakeholder is not approval. The security team, not your project sponsor, owns the tooling policy — loop them in from day one.
Part V · Team practice & measurement

From personal skill to team capability

Share the assets, not the anecdotes

Individual AI skill compounds when it's committed to the repo. Version CLAUDE.md / rules files like code — review changes to them, because they steer every future generated line. Keep a docs/ai/ (or team wiki) with proven task-prompt templates ("integration scaffold", "test-first feature", "migration"), the exemplar-file map ("mirror orders.py for endpoints"), and a short "known failure modes in our stack" note. When someone finds a prompt that reliably produces good output for a recurring task, that's infrastructure — treat it like a shared library, not a personal trick.

PR etiquette

Follow the team's (and on engagements, the customer's) norms on declaring AI assistance — some teams want a commit trailer or PR note, some don't care; the non-negotiable part is that disclosure never lowers the bar. You answer every review question about the code as its author, because you are. Never submit for review what you haven't reviewed yourself first: pushing an unread agent diff converts your saved hour into your reviewer's wasted one, and it burns the trust that makes AI-assisted teams fast. If reviewers keep finding Part III catalog items in your PRs, your review discipline — not the tool — is the bug.

Measure speedup honestly

Perceived speedup is unreliable — engineers routinely feel faster with AI even on tasks where measured completion time got worse (the METR 2025 developer study found exactly this gap). So measure like an engineer: cycle time on comparable tasks — spec-ready to merged-with-review, in a simple log (task, type, estimate-if-manual, actual, issues caught in review). After a few weeks you know your multiplier per task type, which is the number that makes your demo-by-Friday commitments real rather than hopeful. Count the review-and-fix time in the total; a 30-minute generation plus a two-hour cleanup is not a win. Lab 1 seeds this log with a controlled A/B; Lab 4 extends it to a real project.

Avoid skill atrophy

Review quality depends on the very skills the tools tempt you to stop exercising. If you can no longer write the code, you can no longer judge the code — and judgment is the job. Deliberate countermeasures: regularly implement something non-trivial fully by hand (kata-style, or "manual Fridays"); when the agent surprises you, chase the why until you could have written it; keep debugging manual first — forming your own hypothesis before asking the agent is exactly the muscle production incidents will demand of you at 2 a.m. with the customer watching. The goal is a centaur, not a passenger.

Individual AI skill compounds when shared. The FDE's job is to leave the customer team faster than they were before you arrived — and the most durable way to do that is to build the AI tooling infrastructure that outlasts your engagement:

The FDE as AI tooling champion — owning the multiplier

On every engagement, exactly one person becomes the de facto AI tooling lead — the person others watch to see "how it's actually done." That person should be you, deliberately, not accidentally. The role means:

  • You set the bar for review quality. When your PRs consistently catch the five catalog failure patterns, the team's review bar rises — not because you lectured anyone, but because they see what "reviewed" looks like and match it.
  • You maintain the shared infrastructure. CLAUDE.md, the prompt library, the slash commands — someone has to own them. On a customer engagement, that someone is you. On your home team, volunteer explicitly: "I'll maintain the AI tooling docs; PR me changes."
  • You run the onboarding sessions. Thirty minutes with a new team member: here's CLAUDE.md, here's the exemplar map, here's how we do spec-first prompting, here are the five failure patterns to watch for on this codebase. One session replaces a week of trial and error.
  • You know when to slow down. The champion who says "this task shouldn't use AI — it's architecture-sensitive and the spec would take longer than the code" earns more trust than the one who AI's everything. Selectivity is credibility.
Measuring your impact as champion: track one number — the team's cycle time on comparable tasks before and after your tooling investments. A CLAUDE.md that saves every team member 10 minutes per task, on a team of six doing four AI-assisted tasks per day, saves four hours per day. That's half an FTE, and it's attributable to infrastructure you built. Put that in your engagement summary.
Part VI · From senior dev to FDE

Everything you know is still true — but the job changes around it

You already know how to code, design systems, review PRs, and ship software. The FDE transition is not about learning a new technical stack — it's about operating in a fundamentally different context. Here are the five shifts that turn a strong engineer into an effective FDE, and how AI-assisted development accelerates each one.

Shift 1: From "I'll estimate after I understand it" to "The demo is Friday"

In product engineering, you estimate after discovery. In FDE work, the demo date was set in the sales process before you were staffed. The customer's champion told their VP "we'll have something to show by Friday," and Friday is non-negotiable — not because anyone is unreasonable, but because the champion's credibility is on the line and your job is to protect it. This sounds terrifying to a product engineer. To an FDE with AI-assisted development, it's Tuesday:

Product-engineer instinct
  • "I need a week to spike the SSO integration before I can estimate it."
  • "Let me read the python3-saml docs and try a few things locally first."
  • Result: estimate comes Friday; demo comes next month.
FDE instinct (AI-assisted)
  • "Claude Code, explore this repo. Find the auth middleware and config pattern. Don't write anything." — 15 minutes to understand the integration surface.
  • "Here's the python3-saml quickstart. Generate a working SP metadata endpoint against our config pattern. Write the test first." — 45 minutes to a working endpoint.
  • Result: demo-ready by Thursday, with Friday as buffer for the things the spike revealed.

The AI doesn't make you faster at deciding — it makes you faster at knowing. You spike by having the agent explore and prototype in parallel with your reading, collapsing "I need to understand this before I can build it" into a single session. The decision quality doesn't drop; the time to reach the decision collapses.

Shift 2: From "I own this component" to "I own the outcome"

In a product team, success is shipping your piece: the API works, the tests pass, the PR is merged. In an FDE engagement, success is the customer using the system to do their job better — which means your scope is not the code. It's the code, the deployment, the monitoring, the runbook, the training session, the stakeholder update, and the follow-up next month. You are the integration point between the technology and the business result, and nobody else on the engagement has that role. AI-assisted development gives you the bandwidth to cover this expanded scope — the code that used to take all week now takes a morning, and the afternoons go to the human work: understanding the business problem, building trust, designing the adoption plan.

The FDE scope test: if your answer to "what did you deliver?" is a list of merged PRs, you're operating as a staff engineer, not an FDE. The FDE answer is: "The procurement team processed 40% more purchase orders in their first week with the system, with zero data-entry errors on the automated fields." PRs are the mechanism; business outcomes are the deliverable (FD4 owns the KPI framework).

Shift 3: From "the stack I know" to "the stack they have"

Every customer has a different stack. Last quarter's engagement was FastAPI + PostgreSQL + AWS. This quarter's is a ten-year-old Django monolith on Azure with a stored-procedure layer nobody fully understands. In product engineering, you'd advocate for the right tool for the job. In FDE work, the tools were chosen years ago by people who've since left — your job is to ship within their constraints, not to fix their architecture. Claude Code makes this shift survivable:

Without AI assistance

Week 1: reading Django docs, understanding the ORM, figuring out the stored-procedure conventions, making stupid mistakes because everything is new. Week 2: finally productive, but the demo was supposed to be Friday of Week 1.

With AI assistance

Day 1: "Explore this Django codebase. Map the ORM patterns, the stored-procedure conventions, and how auth works. Mirror the existing view pattern for a new endpoint that..." Productive by Tuesday afternoon, demo Friday.

The AI doesn't make you an expert in every stack. It makes you operational in any stack within hours instead of days. You still need to recognize when the AI's Django code is using 1.x patterns in a 4.x project (Part III failure catalog, pattern #2: deprecated-version code). But the ramp from zero to "can produce a reviewed, working PR" compresses from a week to a day. That compression is what makes the FDE business model viable across arbitrary customer stacks.

Shift 4: From "code is the artifact" to "the spec is the artifact"

When AI writes most of the implementation, your primary work product shifts from code to specifications. The spec — not the code — is where your expertise, judgment, and value are expressed. The code is a rendering of the spec, and can be regenerated when the spec changes. This inverts the traditional relationship between design and implementation. In traditional development, the design doc is a guide; the code is ground truth. In AI-assisted development, the spec is ground truth — if the code doesn't match, you regenerate the code, not the spec: 

# The FDE's real deliverable — a spec that survives code regeneration:
# SPEC: Invoice approval workflow — AI triage step

## Behavior
When an invoice arrives (POST /invoices from the ERP webhook):
1. Classify the invoice (PO-based, non-PO, utility, inter-company)
   using the existing classification model at app/ml/classify.py
2. If PO-based AND amount < $5,000 AND the PO exists in the ERP:
   → auto-approve (POST /approvals with source="ai-triage")
3. If PO-based AND amount >= $5,000 OR non-PO AND amount > $1,000:
   → route to manager queue (POST /queues/manager-review)
4. All others: route to AP-clerk queue (POST /queues/ap-review)
5. Every decision: log to app/audit.py with model_version, confidence,
   input fields used, and the decision path taken (for FD4 traceability)

## Acceptance criteria (test each independently)
- [ ] PO-based, $2,300, PO exists → auto_approved == true, queue untouched
- [ ] PO-based, $7,500, PO exists → queued manager-review, never auto_approved
- [ ] Non-PO, $45 → queued ap-review
- [ ] Utility invoice, any amount → queued ap-review
- [ ] ERP webhook with missing PO → HTTP 422, audit log has the error detail
- [ ] ERP webhook with malformed amount → HTTP 422, not 500

## Constraints
- Use existing deps only (app/ml/classify.py, app/audit.py, httpx for ERP)
- Match the service pattern in app/services/orders.py exactly
- Test file must follow tests/test_orders.py structure

## Non-goals
- No changes to the classification model itself (that's AI10's territory)
- No email notifications (separate feature)
- No UI — this is the API layer only
The spec-portability test: could you hand this spec to a different AI coding tool (or a different model) and get a working implementation? If yes, the spec is complete. If it depends on unwritten context in your head — "they'll know what I mean by 'route to queue'" — it's not a spec, it's a hint. Complete specs are the bridge from "I built it" to "anyone on the team can regenerate it."

Shift 5: From "I write code" to "I design systems and review code"

This is the identity shift underneath the other four. Your job title might still say "engineer," but your daily work has more in common with a technical architect and a staff engineer than with a mid-level developer. You spend your time on: understanding the business problem (FD1), decomposing it into right-sized specs (this module), reviewing AI-generated implementations (Part III), integrating with the customer's existing systems (PR8), measuring whether the thing you shipped actually solved the problem (FD4). The ratio is roughly:

This shift is uncomfortable. Senior engineers who've spent a decade mastering the craft of writing code are told that writing code is now 20% of the job. The value has migrated upstream (what to build, how to decompose it, how to verify it) and downstream (did it solve the problem, does the customer trust it, will they adopt it). The engineers who resist this shift — who insist on writing every line by hand because "that's the job" — will produce less, slower, with no compensating quality gain. The engineers who embrace it become the people who ship in days what used to take weeks, and who get called first for the hardest engagements. That's the FDE proposition. Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Accepting diffs you can't explain

If you can't explain a hunk to a reviewer, you don't own it — you're just closest to the keyboard when it breaks. Explain-back or revert.

✗ One giant prompt for the whole feature

"Build the dashboard" outsources thirty design decisions to statistics. Decompose to PR-sized specs; keep the architecture decisions yours.

✗ Customer code in unapproved tools

Personal accounts, consumer tiers, "just this once" — each is a potential contract breach and incident report. Approved tool, approved tier, in writing, first.

✗ Vibes-based speedup claims

Feeling 5× faster while measuring 1.2× (or slower) is documented and common. Log cycle times including review-and-fix; quote only numbers you have.

✓ Tests first, then implementation

Have the agent write tests, review those 40 lines hard (do they assert?), then let it code to green. Cheapest review leverage that exists — plus a 30-second mutation spot-check.

✓ Invest in CLAUDE.md and exemplars

An hour spent on rules files and a "mirror this file" exemplar map pays back on every task, for everyone on the team, forever. Version and review them like code.

✗ Letting stale context accumulate across sessions

"Just one more thing in the same session" past ~10 tool calls is where context rot sets in. The agent starts repeating itself, forgetting early constraints, and making decisions from polluted context. Fresh session per subtask; let CLAUDE.md carry the durable knowledge.

✓ Read your transcripts — one per week

The highest-leverage learning habit: open one Claude Code transcript per week and study your own failure modes. Which prompts worked? Where did the model misunderstand? What did you miss in review? Your transcripts are a personalized prompt-engineering course.

Assisted run. New timer, in assisted/, with your agentic tool. Feed it SPEC.md as a spec-first prompt (Part II shape: criteria + non-goals + "run the tests, show the diff"). Review the result with the Part III checklist inside the timed window — review time is delivery time. Record the same line.
4
Quality diff, both directions. Read both versions side by side and note: Which handles more edge cases? Which has better tests — and do the AI tests actually assert (run the mutation spot-check on both)? Which is over-engineered? Which would you rather maintain? Be specific — "assisted version validated dates with a regex that accepts 2026-13-45" is data; "AI code felt fine" is not.
5
Compute your multiplier and write 5 lines. 
speedup = manual_min / assisted_min          # include review time!
# typical first-run result: 1.5–3× on this task class — not 10×.
# the gap between this number and your expectation is the lesson.
Note where the assisted run gained (boilerplate, tests, docs lookup) and where it didn't (deciding validation rules). Compare against the Part I gains table — your data should roughly reproduce it.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • "Assisted was slower!" — legitimate result, keep it. Common causes: task too small for agent overhead, vague spec causing iteration loops, or unfamiliarity with the tool. Note the cause; re-run the lab in a month.
  • "I know the task too well for manual to be fair" — that bias favors the manual baseline, which makes an assisted win more convincing, not less.
  • One data point is a data point — resist generalizing to "AI is X" from n=1. That's what the ongoing log is for.

✅ Done when

You have two timed, spec-equivalent implementations, a written quality comparison, and one honest personal multiplier number — the seed row of your cycle-time log.
Lab 2 · ~2 h
Spec-driven build with Claude Code

🎯 Objective

Run the full Part II loop — spec → plan → implement → verify → review — on a task big enough to be interesting, and catch real defects with the Part III checklist. There will be findings; the lab fails only if your log is empty.

📦 Setup

1
Use a sandbox repo with existing structure (a side project, or a small FastAPI/Express starter you clone). Install and initialize Claude Code: 
npm install -g @anthropic-ai/claude-code
cd your-sandbox-repo && git checkout -b lab2-spec-build
claude   # then use /init to generate a starting CLAUDE.md
Edit the generated CLAUDE.md: add real test/lint commands, one exemplar-file pointer, and a "Never" section (Part II skeleton). Two minutes here changes everything downstream.

🔬 Steps

2
Write a ~15-line spec in SPEC.md for a feature that touches 3+ files — e.g. the rate-limiting spec from Part II, or "add CSV export to the reports endpoint". Must include: behavior, acceptance criteria (testable), constraints ("use the existing X, no new deps"), non-goals, and "write the tests first".
3
Plan first. In Claude Code, enter plan mode (shift+tab) and give it the spec. Read the plan as if reviewing a junior's design doc: does it touch files it shouldn't? Miss a criterion? Invent scope? Redirect in plan mode until the plan is right — count how many redirects it took (that's a measure of your spec, not the model).
4
Tests first, then implementation. Approve the plan; have it write the tests, and stop there. Review the tests hard: every acceptance criterion covered? Real assertions? Then let it implement to green and demand evidence: 
> write the tests for SPEC.md first; stop before implementing
… review the tests, fix/redirect …
> implement until the tests pass. run the full suite and lint,
> paste the output, then show me the complete diff
5
Formal review with the Part III checklist. Go hunk by hunk through git diff, checklist items 1–7, and log every finding: 
# review-log.md
| # | file:line | severity | catalog pattern        | what I found                     |
|---|-----------|----------|------------------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | api.py:84 | high     | silent behavior change | catch-all except now returns 200 |
| 2 | limits.py | med      | plausible-wrong API    | redis.expire_at() kwarg invalid  |
| 3 | test_…py  | med      | hollow test            | asserts response is not None     |
Verify every unfamiliar API against real docs, and run the mutation spot-check on the tests. Typical honest haul on a 300-line diff: 2–6 findings.
6
Close the loop. Feed the findings back as a numbered list; review the fix diff; commit in small, intent-described commits. Then promote anything durable you learned ("always use domain exceptions", the exemplar pointer that worked) into CLAUDE.md — that's the compounding step most people skip.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Agent ignores a constraint — constraints buried mid-paragraph get lost; make them a bulleted "Constraints/Never" block, and put repo-wide ones in CLAUDE.md, not the prompt.
  • Plan keeps missing the point after two redirects — two-strike rule: your spec is ambiguous. Rewrite the spec; don't argue with the plan.
  • Found zero issues in review — you reviewed too fast. Re-do items 3, 4 and 6 of the checklist slowly; hollow tests and silent deltas hide from skimming.

✅ Done when

Feature merged on your branch with tests you trust, a review log with real findings mapped to the failure catalog, and an improved CLAUDE.md — the complete professional loop, executed once end to end.
Lab 3 · ~1.5 h
Unfamiliar-codebase drill

🎯 Objective

Simulate the defining FDE moment: dropped into a codebase you've never seen, expected to be useful the same day. Use AI to build a mental map fast, then fix a real bug — with verification, because unfamiliar code is where plausible-but-wrong hurts most.

📦 Setup

1
Pick a mid-size OSS repo you have genuinely never read (5k–50k lines: a CLI tool, a Flask/Express app, a small library — not the Linux kernel). Two bug options:
  • Real: pick an open issue labeled bug + good first issue with reproduction steps.
  • Planted: have a colleague (or a separate agent session) introduce a subtle bug on a branch — an inverted condition, an off-by-one in pagination, a swallowed error — and tell you only the symptom.
git clone https://github.com/OWNER/REPO && cd REPO
git checkout -b lab3-drill
# get the test suite running BEFORE touching anything:
make test  # or pytest / npm test — a green baseline is your safety net

🔬 Steps

2
Map the repo — 20 minutes, timeboxed. Use the agent as a guide, not an oracle: 
> explore this repo. give me: (1) what it does, (2) architecture in
> 5 bullets, (3) the 5 files that matter most and why, (4) how a
> request/command flows from entry point to output, (5) how tests run
Then verify the map: open the 5 files and trace the flow it described yourself. Models sometimes describe the architecture a project should have. Sketch the flow on paper from what you actually saw — the sketch, not the transcript, is your mental model.
3
Reproduce before you fix. Write (or have the agent write, then verify) a failing test that captures the bug's symptom. No failing test → you don't understand the bug yet — keep investigating, not prompting.
4
Hypothesize manually, then use the agent to check. Form your own hypothesis of the cause from your map first (skill-atrophy countermeasure, live). Then: 
> the failing test is tests/test_export.py::test_unicode_filename.
> my hypothesis: the filename is encoded twice in export/writer.py.
> trace the actual code path and confirm or refute with line refs
Agreement is not confirmation — make it show you the lines. If it proposes a different cause, adjudicate by reading the code: someone is wrong, and it's your job to know who.
5
Minimal fix, full verification. Smallest change that makes the failing test pass; full suite green; whole diff through the Part III checklist — in a foreign codebase, be extra hostile to "improvements" beyond the fix, since you can't yet judge what's load-bearing. If it was a real issue: write it up as a proper PR-shaped contribution (and consider submitting it).
6
Debrief in 5 lines: time to working mental map, time to verified fix, where the agent's map was wrong, what you'd do differently on a customer repo Monday. That last line is the actual deliverable.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Test suite won't run — that is the drill; customer repos greet you the same way. Have the agent diagnose the setup failure; budget it separately from the 90 minutes.
  • Agent's map contradicts itself between answers — context is polluted; start a fresh session, feed it your verified sketch as ground truth.
  • Real-issue route stalls on maintainer context you can't know — switch to the planted-bug route; the skill being drilled is mapping + verified fixing, not OSS archaeology.

✅ Done when

From git clone to a verified fix with a green suite in ~90 minutes, with a hand-drawn architecture sketch to show for it — and you'd walk into a customer repo tomorrow without dread.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~1 week, ongoing
The real-project acceleration write-up

🎯 Objective

Produce the catalog evidence for FN7: "AI-assisted workflow demonstrably accelerating delivery on a real project" — a real task, run end-to-end with your full workflow, measured against an honest pre-registered estimate, written up so a skeptic would believe it.

📦 Setup

1
Pick a real task from real work — sprint backlog, internal tool, client deliverable (using only tools approved for that code — Part IV applies to your evidence too). Right size: 1–3 days of estimated manual work, some unfamiliar surface (new API, new framework corner), a verifiable done-state. Toy tasks convince nobody, least of all you.
2
Pre-register your estimate — before any AI touches the task, write down and timestamp: 
# estimate.md — committed BEFORE starting
task: integrate DocuSign webhooks into the order-approval flow
manual estimate: 2.5 days (20 h) — basis: last two webhook
  integrations took 2 and 3 days
done-state: events verified + persisted + status transitions tested,
  staging demo passes, PR approved
workflow planned: CLAUDE.md + spec-first prompts per subtask,
  tests-first, checklist review, small commits
A post-hoc "I would have taken a week" is the vibes-based claim this module exists to kill. Pre-registration is what makes your number evidence.

🔬 Steps

3
Execute with the full workflow, keeping a running log as you go (5 min/day, not a diary): decomposition into right-sized specs; plan-mode redirects; every review finding (catalog pattern + severity); actual time per subtask. The log is your raw data — unglamorous entries like "subtask 3: agent used deprecated SDK method, caught in review item 3, 15 min to verify + fix" are exactly what makes the write-up credible.
4
Ship it for real — through your normal quality gates: PR review by a human, CI, staging, demo. "Accelerating delivery" means delivered; a fast branch that never merged is not evidence of anything.
5
Write the one-page report: 
# AI-assisted delivery report — DocuSign webhook integration   2026-08-04
## Task & context
Real sprint item; order-approval flow; unfamiliar vendor API.
## Workflow used
Claude Code + repo CLAUDE.md · 5 spec-first subtasks · tests-first ·
plan-mode review · Part III checklist on every diff · small commits
## Result vs pre-registered estimate
| metric              | estimate (pre-reg) | actual        |
|---------------------|--------------------|---------------|
| elapsed dev time    | 20 h               | 7.5 h         |
| speedup             | —                  | 2.7×          |
| review findings     | —                  | 9 (2 high)    |
| post-merge defects  | (typical: 1–2)     | 0 to date     |
## Where the time went / came from
Won: scaffold, webhook signature verification, test fixtures, docs lookup.
Not won: modeling status-transition edge cases (whiteboard, human).
## Issues caught in review (top 3 of 9)
1. HIGH silent-change: retry wrapper swallowed signature failures → 200
2. HIGH security: webhook secret interpolated into a log line
3. MED  deprecated: SDK v2 event-parsing API; v3 in our lockfile
## Team practices adopted
CLAUDE.md additions (3 rules) · webhook-integration prompt template
committed to docs/ai/ · review checklist proposed at eng weekly
6
Extract the team practices doc — the report's last section, expanded into a half-page your team actually adopts: the rules-file diff, the reusable prompt template, the checklist. Evidence of acceleration plus evidence you make teams faster — that pair is what distinguishes a score-3 heading for 4.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Speedup came out modest (1.3–1.5×)? Report it honestly with the breakdown of where time went — an honest 1.4× with zero post-merge defects and a findings log is stronger evidence of mastery than a suspicious 6×.
  • Task mutated mid-week (real work does) — log the scope change and adjust the estimate explicitly in the report; silently comparing against the original estimate corrupts the number.
  • Client code with no approved AI tooling — run Lab 4 on an internal project instead; never let the evidence artifact create a Part IV violation.

✅ Done when

A skeptical engineering manager reads your one page and believes the number — pre-registered estimate, real shipped task, findings log, honest breakdown — and your team has adopted at least one practice from it. Keep the log running; your multiplier per task type is now a number you maintain, not a claim you make.
