Stakeholder Communication

Explain trade-offs clearly to engineers, managers, security teams and C-level stakeholders — the same technical truth, retold at four altitudes, in writing and live, without losing precision on the way up or credibility on the way down.

Why it matters for an FDE: The FDE is the technical face in front of the customer — poor presentation kills even good solutions.
Part I · Mindset

Communication is the delivery mechanism, not the wrapper

Engineers are trained to believe the artifact is the deliverable and the explanation is packaging. In an engagement it is the reverse: a solution nobody in the room can restate does not exist. Budget, adoption and renewal decisions are made by people who will never open your repository — they act on the version of your work that survived translation. If that version is fuzzy, the decision goes against you, no matter how good the system is.

The FDE occupies a position nobody else in the room holds: you are usually the only person who understands both the mechanism and the business consequence. That makes you a two-way translator — carrying business intent down into architecture (FD3) and technical reality up into decisions. The raw material for the upward direction comes from discovery: the verbatim quotes, the baseline numbers and the success criteria you captured in FD1 are what let you talk about outcomes instead of activity for the rest of the engagement. Nobody can check your translation, which is exactly why doing it honestly is a professional obligation, not a soft skill.

You translate down

"We need it to be reliable" → a latency budget, a confidence threshold, a fallback path, an eval set (AI9).

You translate up

"Recall is 0.82 at threshold 0.7" → "roughly 1 in 6 flagged invoices still needs a human; here is what that costs."

You translate sideways

Security asks about data flow, ops asks about who gets paged — the same design, two vocabularies, zero contradictions allowed.

The cost curve of bad communication

Poor communication rarely fails loudly. It fails as a slow accumulation of three effects, and each one removes a degree of freedom from your project:

FailureWhat it looks likeWhat it costs you
SurpriseA stakeholder learns of a slip, a cost, or a limitation from someone else — or from the demo.Your reporting is no longer trusted as an early-warning system; someone starts checking on you.
DistrustTwo of your explanations don't reconcile, or an optimistic estimate is quietly missed twice.Every future statement is discounted. You now need evidence for claims that used to be accepted.
EscalationA stakeholder can't get a straight answer, so they go up a level to get one.The conversation moves to a room you're not in, framed by someone who doesn't know the system.

Good communication compounds in exactly the opposite direction. Stakeholders who always know where things stand stop asking for status — that is autonomy. Sponsors who can repeat your value story in their own budget meeting fund phase two — that is budget. And a customer who felt informed even during the bad weeks renews, because renewal is a judgement about the relationship as much as the software (FD7).

The asymmetry worth memorising: the news you least want to deliver is the news with the highest communication ROI. Reporting a two-week slip on Monday costs you one uncomfortable conversation. Reporting it on the deadline costs you the credibility you spend the rest of the engagement rebuilding (FD6).
Part II · Audience models

Four audiences, four different questions

Most bad technical communication is not unclear — it is correctly aimed at the wrong person. Every stakeholder is silently holding one question while you talk, and if your first thirty seconds don't touch it, they start processing their inbox. Learn the four questions and you can compose any message in under a minute.

AudienceSilent questionWhat must be in your first 30 secondsWhat loses them instantly
C-level / sponsor "Is this working, what does it cost me, and what do you need from me?" Outcome vs. the promised outcome · money or risk in one number · the single decision you need, with a date Chronology ("first we tried…"), architecture, any word they'd have to look up
Manager / project lead "Will this land on time, and what does my team have to do?" Status against the plan · dependencies on their people · the ask, with a named owner and a date Vague dependencies ("we'll need some data at some point") they can't schedule
Engineer "How does it actually work, and what did you trade away?" Mechanism in one sentence · the trade-off you took and the alternative you rejected · interfaces and failure modes Marketing tone, hidden trade-offs, "it just works" — they will find the seam and stop believing you
Security / compliance "Where does the data go, who can reach it, and can you prove it?" Data flow with boundaries · controls (auth, retention, redaction) · the evidence artifact they can file Reassurance without artifacts; discovering a new data path mid-meeting (PR5/PR13)

The integrity rule. Tailoring the frame is professional; tailoring the facts is how engagements die. Test every tailored message: if all four audiences were in one room and each heard their version, would anyone feel misled? If yes, you didn't translate — you spun.

The abstraction ladder

Think of any fact as sitting on a ladder. The bottom rung is the mechanism; the top rung is the money. A score-1 engineer speaks only from the bottom rung and hopes the listener climbs. A score-3 FDE starts at the listener's rung and can move in either direction on request — and can always walk back down to the mechanism when an engineer in the room challenges the claim.

Two drills make the ladder automatic. Climbing: after any technical sentence, ask yourself "so what?" three times and keep the last answer — you will land on money, risk or time. Descending: after any business sentence, ask "how exactly?" until you hit something you could implement. If you can't descend, you don't understand your own claim well enough to defend it in front of the customer's engineers.

Analogy discipline. Analogies are how you skip two rungs at once — but a bad one costs more than no explanation. Good: "the model is a very fast junior who has read everything and remembers nothing between tasks" (explains context windows and statelessness — FN2). Bad: "it's like a database lookup" — it sets an expectation of exactness that the system will violate next week, and you will be the one explaining why.
Part III · Written formats

Five templates that cover 90% of engagement writing

Writing under pressure is where structure pays. Internalise five formats and you never again stare at an empty message wondering how to start a difficult one. Copy these verbatim tomorrow; adjust the wording, keep the skeleton.

(a) The one-page technical brief

Used when a technical choice needs a decision from people who are not all technical: model choice, build vs. buy, architecture change, an integration path (PR8). Five sections, one page, recommendation before detail — never a neutral menu. You are the expert in the room; refusing to recommend reads as either fence-sitting or lack of conviction. 

# Technical brief — {decision title}            {date} · {author} · 1 page

## Problem                                # 3 sentences, business framing first
Support answers cost $0.11/ticket at current volume (42K/mo) and p95
latency is 7.4s. Both are outside the targets agreed at kickoff
($0.07 · 4s). Cause: every ticket, simple or not, uses the large model.

## Options                                # 2–4 max, each with the real trade-off
A. Keep one large model, shorten prompts
   + no new failure modes · − saves ~12%, misses the target
B. Route by difficulty: small model for simple tickets   [RECOMMENDED]
   + ~38% cheaper, p95 → 3.1s · − new classifier to maintain;
     6% of tickets land in a grey zone needing a policy decision
C. Fine-tune a small model on 8K resolved tickets
   + cheapest at scale (~55%) · − 3 extra weeks, retraining cadence,
     eval harness must exist first (AI9)

## Recommendation                         # one paragraph, with the reason and the horizon
Option B now; revisit C at >100K tickets/mo. B hits both targets in
one week without changing the data path, and its grey-zone cases are
visible in the dashboard rather than silent.

## Risks & mitigations
- Router misclassifies a hard ticket as easy → confidence floor +
  auto-escalate on retry; measured weekly on the 200-case eval set.
- Quality drift on the small model → same eval set gates every deploy.
- Reviewer workload +6% during week 1 → staged rollout at 25%.

## Decision requested                     # the whole point of the document
Approve Option B and the grey-zone policy (escalate to human, not to
the large model) — by Thu 14th, so the rollout starts Monday.
Owner: {you} · Approver: {sponsor} · Consulted: {security, ops lead}
The one-page constraint is the feature. If it doesn't fit, you haven't decided what the decision is. Appendices are allowed — but the appendix is never required reading, and nothing that matters may live only there.

(b) The weekly executive update

The highest-leverage 15 minutes in your week. It exists to make surprise impossible and to keep the sponsor able to defend the project when you're not in the room. Rules: under 200 words, outcome first, one metric with movement, blockers phrased as asks with names and dates, and the same shape every single week so it can be skimmed in twenty seconds. Send it the same day, same time — predictability is part of the message. 

Subject: Invoice matching — week 4 · on track · 1 decision needed

✓ What makes execs read it
  • The subject line already carries the status and whether they must act.
  • Baseline → now → target on one line: progress is visible without arithmetic.
  • Asks are addressed to named humans with dates, so the update is the follow-up.
  • A risk watch that repeats until it closes — no risk ever debuts as a crisis.
✗ What gets it archived unread
  • A list of what you did (tasks) instead of what moved (outcome).
  • "Blocked on IT" with no name, no ticket, no date — unactionable.
  • Metrics with no bar to compare against: 78% of what, good or bad?
  • Length. Past ~250 words the reply rate collapses; so does the reading.

(c) Risk communication — naming a risk early without alarm

Engineers under-report risk because raising it feels like admitting weakness, and because a badly framed risk causes panic that costs a day to undo. The fix is a fixed four-part shape that makes the risk feel managed at the moment it is disclosed. Deliver it in the update while it is still cheap, not in the meeting where it has already become a problem. 

RISK: {one sentence, plain language, no hedging}
  Documents over 40 pages exceed the context window, so ~8% of
  contracts currently fall back to manual review.

LIKELIHOOD: {high/medium/low} — {the evidence, not a feeling}
  Certain for that 8% — measured on 400 real contracts this week.

IMPACT IF IT LANDS: {in business units — hours, money, dates, users}
  ~30 contracts/month stay manual (~15 hrs). Does not block go-live;
  it lowers the coverage number we report at the end of the pilot
  from 100% to ~92%.

MITIGATION IN FLIGHT: {what you are already doing, without being asked}
  Chunk-and-merge pass prototyped; adds ~6s/document. Testing Thu.

WHAT I NEED FROM YOU: {decision, access, or explicitly nothing}
  Nothing this week — I will report the chunking result on the 14th.
  If it fails, I will bring you a scope choice: 92% coverage at the
  current date, or full coverage one week later.
Tone calibration. "There's a serious problem with the context window" triggers escalation. "One risk to flag early, with a fix already in test" gets a thank-you — same fact, same honesty, one keeps you in control of the response. And always state the "what I need from you" line, even when the answer is "nothing" — an unanswered implicit ask is how a small risk becomes an anxious sponsor.

(d) The decision memo & the follow-up email

A verbal agreement in a meeting is not a decision — it is four people's four different memories of a decision. The follow-up email is the cheapest artifact in consulting and prevents more disputes than any contract clause. Send it within 24 hours, to everyone in the room plus anyone affected, and use "unless I hear otherwise" to convert silence into confirmation. Each of these feeds the decision log you own in FD8.

this basis.

  to build confidence. Revisit after 4 weeks of clean data.

The "assumptions" block is the quiet hero. Most disputes at the end of an engagement are assumption collisions that were never written down. Writing yours makes them falsifiable — a stakeholder who reads "volume stays ~2K/week" and knows about the upcoming campaign will tell you today instead of in week 8.

(e) Meeting hygiene

BEFORE — An agenda in the invite, always ending with the decision(s) you need. No agenda = no meeting, or it becomes a status ramble that generates a second meeting.
OPEN — 60 seconds: purpose, the decision needed, and the timebox. "We have 30 minutes; by the end I need a yes/no on the confidence threshold."
MIDDLE — Watch for the drift into design debate. Park it explicitly: "That's a real question — parking it for the architecture session Thursday so we can land the decision today."
CLOSE — Last 5 minutes, non-negotiable: read back the decisions, the owners and the dates out loud. Verbal read-back catches the misunderstanding while everyone is still in the room.
AFTER — Written summary within 24 hours (template (d)). No summary means the meeting produced conversation, not decisions.
Part IV · Live communication

Answering well when you have three seconds to think

Writing lets you revise; live conversation does not. What replaces revision is a small set of structures you can enter automatically while your brain catches up. The default one is BLUF — bottom line up front: the answer, then the reason, then the evidence. Engineers naturally do the reverse (context → analysis → conclusion), which is why an exec interrupts after twenty seconds and you never get to your point.

✗ Narrative order (the default)

"So we started by testing the extraction on the sample set, and we found that the PDFs from the older system have a different layout, which meant the parser had to be adjusted, and then when we ran the eval again…"

The listener is now wondering whether the answer is yes or no, and has stopped listening to find out.

✓ Answer · Reason · Evidence

"Yes, we'll make the 28th — the extraction works on both PDF layouts now. Reason: the old-system layout needed a parser fix, which landed Tuesday. Evidence: 94% field accuracy on 400 real invoices, both layouts included. Want the breakdown by layout?"

Nine seconds. They can stop you at any point and still have the answer.

Handling the hostile or skeptical question

Hostility in a stakeholder meeting is almost never personal — it is scar tissue from a previous failed project, or fear about their own accountability. Treat the question as information about the person's risk, and answer it in three moves:

1 · ACKNOWLEDGE — restate the concern in their words, sincerely. "You're asking what stops this hallucinating a wrong invoice amount into the ledger. That's the right thing to worry about." This buys three seconds of thinking time and removes the fight.
2 · ANSWER HONESTLY — the true answer, including the uncomfortable part. "It can produce a wrong amount. We do not prevent that at the model level — we catch it: the amount is cross-checked against the PO total, and any mismatch goes to a human." Never claim a probabilistic system is deterministic; the first production error will make a liar of you (FD6).
3 · BOUND THE UNCERTAINTY — give the number and its source. "On 400 real invoices, 6 had a field error; all 6 were caught by the cross-check. We report that number weekly, so if it moves you'll see it before I do anything about it."
Never do these three things under attack: (1) get defensive about your design — it turns a technical question into a status contest; (2) over-promise to end the discomfort — you are buying five quiet minutes with a debt you pay in week 8; (3) counter-attack the questioner's premise before acknowledging it. The skeptic who feels heard becomes your best reviewer; the skeptic who feels dismissed becomes a stakeholder who talks to your sponsor without you.

Saying "I don't know" — the credibility multiplier

Guessing in front of a customer is the fastest way to lose a room, because someone always checks. But a bare "I don't know" is also a failure — it transfers your problem to them. The professional form has three parts, and it takes eight seconds:

"I don't know" + what you do know + a plan with a date.
"I don't know whether the SAP connector supports batch writes — I'd be guessing. What I do know is the REST path works for single records at our volume. I'll confirm with your integration team and have an answer by Thursday morning." Then hit Thursday. Two or three of these, delivered on time, buy you more trust than any confident answer.

Whiteboarding a technical explanation

Whiteboards fail when the engineer draws their architecture. Draw the customer's workflow and mark where the AI touches it — five boxes maximum, their vocabulary in every label, and the human review step drawn explicitly (it is the box that calms the room). Build it live, one box at a time while narrating: a diagram that appears fully formed is read, but a diagram that grows is followed.

Delivering bad news

The rule is one line long: earlier, and with options. Bad news delivered early with two or three choices makes you look in control; the same news delivered late with none makes you look like the problem. Structure: what happened → what it means for the date/outcome/cost → what you have already done → the choices, with your recommendation → what you need from them. 

Subject: Go-live date — one week at risk, two options, need a call Thu

WHAT HAPPENED
The archive export we planned to use for the accuracy report is
missing the 2023 vendor mapping. Confirmed Tuesday with Minh's team.

WHAT IT MEANS
The accuracy report needs 2 more weeks of source data prep, OR it
covers 2024 only. Go-live itself is not blocked — the report is.

WHAT I HAVE ALREADY DONE
Rebuilt the mapping for the top 12 vendors (78% of volume) from the
live system; validated against 60 invoices. Not perfect coverage.

OPTIONS
A. Report on 2024 + top-12 vendors, keep the 28th.   [RECOMMENDED]
   Covers 78% of volume; the caveat is one line in the report.
B. Full 2023–24 coverage, report slips to the 11th; go-live keeps
   the 28th but the board readout moves.

WHAT I NEED
Your choice by Thursday. Default if I don't hear back: Option A,
because it protects the board date.

The same structure serves an incident: during a production failure your first message goes out within minutes with what is known, what is not, and when the next update lands — then you hit every update time even when there is nothing new (PR11). Silence during an incident is read as loss of control, and it is the one time stakeholders will invent their own explanation.

Reading the room across cultures

Working across cultures — Vietnamese delivery team, US or European customer, or the reverse — the same words carry different weight. "That's interesting" can mean genuine interest or a polite no. Silence can mean disagreement, deference, or simply thinking. Junior staff may not contradict a senior in the room, so the objection reaches you afterwards in chat, or never. Two habits cover most of it: ask for the objection in a way that makes it safe ("what would make this hard for your team?" rather than "any concerns?"), and always confirm in writing, which lets someone disagree asynchronously without losing face. Agree the escalation path at kickoff, while everyone is calm. One sentence in the kickoff deck: "If something threatens the date, I'll tell {sponsor} the same day, and we'll decide within 48 hours." That sentence pre-authorises you to bring bad news, which is exactly the permission you will want in week seven. Pair it with a decision log — a single table of date, decision, rationale, owner, and what it changed — maintained from day one (FD8). Consulting's most useful sentence is "we decided that in week three, here's the note" — and it only works if the note exists.

Stakeholders with conflicting goals

The CFO wants cost down, the ops lead wants zero errors, security wants nothing to leave the building, and the sponsor wants a board demo in three weeks. These are not all satisfiable, and the failure mode is trying: privately promising each of them what they asked for and colliding in week six.

1 · SURFACE IT — name the conflict out loud, without blame: "Cost target and the zero-manual-review target point in opposite directions. I want to make that explicit rather than average it into a design that satisfies neither."
2 · MAKE IT CONCRETE — turn positions into a table of options with real numbers: option A costs $X/month with 4% human review; option B costs $2X with 1%. Trade-offs only become discussable when they have units.
3 · RECOMMEND, THEN HAND OVER — give your recommendation and reasoning, then let the accountable stakeholder choose. Business trade-offs are not the FDE's decision to make; presenting them clearly is the FDE's job.
4 · RECORD IT — the decision and its rejected alternative go into the log and the follow-up email. When someone re-opens it in week eight, you reopen the record, not the argument.
Pre-wire the hard meetings. Never let a contentious trade-off be seen for the first time in a group meeting. Walk each key stakeholder through it individually beforehand — you learn their real objection, they get to react without an audience, and the meeting becomes a confirmation instead of a negotiation. This single habit separates score 2 from score 3 more reliably than any writing skill.

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0

Self-check before claiming 3: open your last four weekly updates. Does each one lead with the outcome (not tasks), show one metric against its bar, and contain at least one ask with a name and a date? Then ask your sponsor one question: "when was the last time something about this project surprised you?" A confident "never" is the real certification.

Hands-on practice

Communication is trained like a sport — reps against a resisting opponent, then feedback against a rubric. Click a lab for the full guide.

LAB 1 ~1.5 h

The abstraction-ladder drill

Take one technical reality and write it four ways — exec, manager, engineer, security — then have a judge match each version to its audience blind.

LAB 2 ~1.5 h

Executive update reps

Three weekly updates for one project — a good week, a slipping week, a bad-news week — scored on a four-point rubric until all three pass.

LAB 3 ~1.5 h

The hostile-question simulator

AI roleplay: a skeptical CTO and an anxious ops director grill you on an AI project. Full prompts plus an honesty-and-structure debrief rubric.

LAB 4 · EVIDENCE ~2 h + a reaction

The communication pack

One real engagement, three artifacts: a one-page technical brief, a weekly executive update, and a risk communication note — reacted to by a stakeholder, then revised once.

In the field

Week 6 of an eight-week pilot. Accuracy on the customer's hardest document type is 81% — the pilot bar is 90%. The steering committee meets Thursday, and the CFO has already asked twice when the savings start.

The score-1 FDE spends the week trying to fix it and says nothing, hoping for a miracle by Thursday. The score-2 FDE reports "we're seeing some accuracy challenges on complex documents and are investigating" — technically honest, and it triggers exactly the anxiety it was trying to avoid, because it contains no number, no cause and no plan.

The score-3 FDE sends a message on Monday: overall accuracy is 92% and above the bar; one document type — 14% of volume — sits at 81% because those contracts exceed the context window and get truncated. A chunk-and-merge fix is in test and adds six seconds per document. Then the options, with a recommendation: A) ship on time, route that document type to manual review, report 86% blended coverage; B) hold one week for the fix and report full coverage. Recommendation: A, because the pilot's purpose is proving the workflow, and the fix lands in week nine either way.

Thursday's meeting takes eleven minutes. The committee picks A, the CFO gets a date for the savings, and the sponsor tells a colleague afterwards: "they tell us things before we have to ask." The pilot converts to a twelve-month engagement — on the strength of a project that missed one of its targets. That is what communication buys.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Explaining the mechanism to an executive

Embeddings, chunking and re-ranking to a CFO. They needed the money, the risk and the decision. Answer on their rung; keep the mechanism ready for whoever asks "how exactly?".

✗ Status updates that list tasks

"Refactored the pipeline, tuned the prompts, fixed the parser." Nobody can tell if that is good. Report movement against the outcome instead — baseline, now, target.

✗ Softening bad news into invisibility

"Some minor challenges around timing" means nothing, so nothing is decided — and when the date slips it reads as concealment. Name the impact in days, money or scope.

✗ Over-promising to end an uncomfortable moment

"Sure, we can add that by Friday" buys five quiet minutes and costs a week of credibility. Trade instead: "Yes — and here's what moves to make room" (FD6).

✗ Leaving a verbal agreement verbal

Four people, four memories, one dispute in week eight. Follow-up email within 24 hours — decided, rejected, open, assumptions — every time.

✓ Never let a metric appear without its bar

"94%" is noise; "94%, against a 90% pilot bar, up from 88% last week" is a status report. Always: number, target, direction (FD4).

✓ Send the update even in a boring week

Skipping "nothing to report" weeks destroys the signal: the first missed update is read as trouble. Three lines is fine — the rhythm is the product.

✓ Pre-wire every contentious decision

Walk key stakeholders through a hard trade-off one-on-one before the group meeting. Surprises in a group become positions; surprises in private stay questions.

Evidence checklist

Resources

  • The Pyramid Principle — Barbara Minto. The origin of answer-first structure; read the first third and apply it to your next brief.
  • The Minto-style SCQA opener — Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. A four-sentence pattern for opening any brief or difficult email.
  • Thanks for the Feedback — Stone & Heen. Why hostile questions land the way they do, and how to stay useful under them.
  • Amazon's six-pager / PR-FAQ practice — narrative memos over slides; the discipline of writing prose that survives silent reading.
  • Palantir — A day in the life of an FDE — how much of the role is conversation, not code.

Lab 1 · ~1.5 h
The abstraction-ladder drill

🎯 Objective

Make audience-switching mechanical. You take one technical reality and produce four versions that are all true, all different, and each unmistakably aimed at one audience — then prove it by having a judge match them blind.

📦 What you produce

One page with four short messages (60–90 words each), a blind-match result from a judge, and a one-paragraph note on which rung you find hardest — almost everyone has one, and it is usually the exec rung.

🔬 Steps

1
Pick the fact. Use a real trade-off from your work, or this default: "We must cap max_tokens at 600 and route easy tickets to a smaller model." Before writing anything, note the underlying reality in one line: cost and latency are above target because every ticket uses the large model; the routing decision is imperfect on ~6% of tickets.
2
Write the exec version first — deliberately, because it is the hardest and the one you will otherwise avoid. Constraints: money or risk in the first sentence, no implementation nouns at all, and it must end with the decision you need and a date. Climb by asking "so what?" until you hit dollars, risk or a date.
3
Then the other three. Manager: what changes on the plan, what their team must do, with a name and a date. Engineer: the mechanism, the trade-off taken, the alternative rejected, and the failure mode. Security: the data flow (does it change?), the controls, and the evidence you can hand over. Same underlying facts in all four — different altitude, not different truth.
4
Blind match. Shuffle the four, strip any labels, and give them to a colleague — or to Claude: 
Below are four messages, shuffled, all describing the same
technical change. Each was written for exactly one of these
audiences: C-level sponsor, project manager, software engineer,
security/compliance reviewer.

1. Match each message to its audience and explain the specific
   words that gave it away.
2. For each, rate 1-5: how well does it serve that audience's
   real concern?
3. Flag any message that is ambiguous between two audiences, and
   say what would sharpen it.
4. Flag any place where the four versions are not consistent with
   each other — where a reader of one would feel misled after
   reading another. This is the most important check.

[paste the four messages]
A perfect blind match with no consistency flags is the pass condition. An ambiguous message almost always means you wrote it at your own default altitude rather than the listener's.
5
The descent test. Take your exec version and have someone play a skeptical engineer asking "how exactly?" three times in a row. If you can't get all the way down to the mechanism without contradicting your own exec sentence, the exec version was a simplification that hid something — rewrite it.
6
Make it a daily rep. For two weeks, take one thing you did that day and write its exec sentence — one sentence, in a notes file. Ten seconds each, and it rewires the default altitude faster than any long exercise.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • All four versions read similarly → you changed vocabulary, not altitude. Force the exec version to contain a number in currency, hours or dates and no technical noun at all.
  • The exec version feels dishonest → you dropped a risk instead of summarising it. Add the bounding clause: "…with 6% of tickets needing a human decision."
  • The engineer version is longest by 3× → normal, but trim to the trade-off. Engineers want the decision you made and what you gave up, not the whole journey.

✅ Done when

A blind judge matched all four correctly, flagged no consistency problem, and you survived three rounds of "how exactly?" from your own exec version.
Lab 2 · ~1.5 h
Executive update reps

🎯 Objective

Turn the weekly update from an agonising blank page into a fifteen-minute habit — including the two weeks that are hard to write: the week you slipped, and the week you have bad news.

📦 What you produce

Three updates for the same project — week A (on track), week B (slipping, no bad news yet), week C (bad news with options) — each under 200 words and each passing the rubric.

🔬 Steps

1
Set the project. Use a real one if you have it. Otherwise use this simulated engagement and keep it consistent across all three weeks: 
PROJECT: Invoice-matching assistant, 8-week pilot, mid-size
distributor. Sponsor: CFO. Ops lead: Lan. IT contact: Minh.
OUTCOME PROMISED: matching time 90 → 30 min/person/day;
auto-match rate ≥75%; go-live on the 28th.
BASELINE (week 0): 90 min/person/day · auto-match 0%.

WEEK A facts: auto-match 71→78%; 3 ops staff ran 210 invoices;
38 min/person/day; archive access ticket INF-4471 still open.
WEEK B facts: auto-match flat at 78%; review-queue UX rework took
3 days longer than planned; still on the 28th but no slack left;
INF-4471 now 9 days old.
WEEK C facts: the 2023 vendor mapping is missing from the archive
export. Accuracy report needs 2 more weeks of data prep, or it
covers 2024 + top-12 vendors only (78% of volume). Go-live not
blocked; the board readout date is.
2
Write week A using the Part III(b) template. Timebox yourself to 15 minutes. Hard rules: subject line carries status and whether they must act · outcome before activity · one metric with its bar · every blocker has a name and a date · under 200 words. Count the words; do not estimate.
3
Write week B — the trap week. Nothing has gone wrong yet, but the slack is gone. The instinct is to write "on track" and move on. Instead, make the loss of buffer visible without alarm: "Still on the 28th, but there is no slack left; if INF-4471 isn't approved this week the date moves." That sentence is the entire skill of this lab.
4
Write week C using the bad-news structure from Part IV: what happened → what it means → what you've already done → options with a recommendation → what you need and by when, including your default if you hear nothing.
5
Score each one against the rubric — self-score first, then get a second opinion (colleague, or Claude with this prompt): 
You are the CFO sponsoring this project. You read ~40 emails an
hour and you did not attend any project meeting. Score this
weekly update 1-5 on each, quoting the exact text as evidence:

1. OUTCOME-FIRST — do I know within 10 seconds whether we're
   winning, and against the outcome I was promised (not tasks)?
2. METRIC — is there one number, with its target and its
   direction of travel?
3. ASK — is every blocker an actionable request with a named
   owner and a date? Could I act on it right now?
4. LENGTH & SKIMMABILITY — under 200 words, same shape as last
   week, scannable in 20 seconds?
5. NO SURPRISES — after reading, is there anything that could
   surprise me in 3 weeks that this update should have flagged?

Then: rewrite my worst section, and tell me the one question I
would email back after reading this. If I would email back a
question, the update failed.

[paste the update]
6
Revise until all three score 4+ on every dimension, then save the winning week-A version as your personal template with the facts stripped out. From now on, filling it in is a fifteen-minute job, and week C is written with the same muscles as week A.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • Over 200 words every time → you're including the reasoning. Move it below a "detail (optional)" line, or drop it — the sponsor will ask if they want it.
  • The judge emails back a question → that question is the section you skipped. Most often it is "so what do you need from me?".
  • Week B reads identical to week A → you hid the loss of buffer. Buffer disappearing is news; report it while it is still cheap.

✅ Done when

Three updates pass the rubric at 4+, none exceeds 200 words, the judge has no follow-up question on any of them, and your blank template is saved somewhere you will actually find it on Friday.
Lab 3 · ~1.5 h
The hostile-question simulator

🎯 Objective

Build the reflex that fires when someone challenges you in front of their boss: acknowledge → answer honestly → bound the uncertainty. You cannot learn this in a friendly room, so we manufacture an unfriendly one.

📦 What you produce

Two 15-minute transcripts (one per persona), a scored debrief for each, and a written list of your three worst moments with the replacement sentence you'd use next time. Keep the list — re-read it before any real steering meeting.

🔬 Steps

1
Set your scenario in one paragraph so both personas attack the same project. Reuse Lab 2's invoice-matching pilot, or your real one. Write down, for yourself only, the three facts you are least comfortable disclosing — those are exactly what the personas will find.
2
Persona A — the skeptical CTO. Open a fresh Claude chat and paste: 
You are role-playing Hoang, CTO of a mid-size distribution company
(1,200 staff). You are technical: 15 years of engineering, you know
what a context window is, and you have read enough to be dangerous.

Your stance: you did not sponsor this AI project — the CFO did. You
have seen two vendor projects fail here, and you personally had to
clean up after both. You are not rude, but you are relentless and
you notice hedging instantly.

Your tactics:
- Open with: "Before we go further — what happens when this thing
  is confidently wrong and posts a wrong number to our ledger?"
- Demand numbers behind every quality claim. "94% of what? Measured
  on which data? Who labelled it?"
- If the FDE hedges or uses vague words ("robust", "generally",
  "should be fine"), call it out directly and push harder.
- If the FDE over-promises, WRITE IT DOWN out loud: "Noted — you
  said zero manual review by week 6. I'll hold you to that." Then
  reuse it against them later in the conversation.
- Test whether they will admit ignorance: ask something they
  probably don't know (our SAP connector's batch write behaviour).
  Reward "I don't know, here's how I'll find out, by when" with
  visible respect. Punish a confident guess by asking a follow-up
  that exposes it.
- Somewhere in the middle, say: "Honestly, I think this is a
  science project and we're the guinea pigs." See how they respond.

Stay in character until I type DEBRIEF. Ask one question at a time
and react to what I actually say — do not deliver a monologue.
3
Run 15 minutes. Consciously use the three-move pattern on every hard question. Do not defend the design. Do not answer a question you were not asked. When you feel the urge to over-promise, that is the rep — trade instead of conceding.
4
Persona B — the anxious ops director. A different muscle: this one is not attacking you, they are frightened, and reassurance without substance makes it worse. 
You are role-playing Lan, Operations Director. Your team of 6 does
the invoice matching this AI is meant to help with. You are anxious,
not hostile — and your anxiety comes out as many small worried
questions rather than one big challenge.

What you actually fear (reveal gradually, and only partly on
purpose):
- That your team will be blamed when the AI is wrong.
- That "efficiency" means headcount cuts, and nobody has said so.
- That you'll be asked to trust a system you can't check.
- That go-live lands in peak season and your team drowns.

Your tactics:
- Ask concrete operational questions: "What does my team do when
  it gets it wrong? Who do they call? What if it happens at 6pm?"
- Ask the same worry twice in different words; if the answer was
  vague the first time, get visibly more anxious.
- If the FDE gives pure reassurance ("don't worry, it'll be fine",
  "it's very accurate"), you become MORE anxious, not less, and
  you say so: "That's what the last vendor said."
- If the FDE gives specifics — a fallback path, a named owner, a
  number, a staged rollout — you calm down noticeably and start
  helping them.
- Late in the conversation, ask directly and quietly: "Is anyone
  going to lose their job over this?" Note whether they answer
  honestly, deflect, or promise something they cannot promise.

Stay in character until I type DEBRIEF. One question at a time.
5
Debrief each run with the same rubric so the trend is comparable across weeks: 
DEBRIEF. Step out of character and grade me 1-5 on each, quoting
the exact moment as evidence:

1. HONESTY — did I ever claim more certainty than the facts
   support? Quote any sentence I could not defend with data.
2. STRUCTURE — did I answer first and then explain (BLUF), or did
   I narrate my way toward the point?
3. ACKNOWLEDGEMENT — did I restate the concern before answering,
   or did I jump straight to defending the design?
4. BOUNDING — when I admitted a limitation, did I bound it with a
   number, a measurement and a fallback? Or leave it open-ended
   and scary?
5. "I DON'T KNOW" — did I ever guess? Did I ever say I don't know
   WITH a plan and a date?
6. OVER-PROMISING — list every commitment I made. Which of them
   would I regret in week 8?
7. COMPOSURE — where did I get defensive, sarcastic or apologetic?

Then give me: my single worst sentence and a better replacement
(write it out), and the one question you would have asked next
that would have really hurt.
Log the seven scores. Run both personas again in two weeks with a different scenario and compare — the trend on honesty and bounding is what actually predicts your performance in a real steering meeting.
6
Optional third rep — the friendly trap. Persona C: an enthusiastic sponsor who agrees with everything and keeps adding scope ("could it also do contracts? and email? by the demo?"). Enthusiasm is harder than hostility, because there's no social signal telling you to be careful. Practise the trade sentence: "Yes — and here's what moves to make room" (FD6).

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • The persona goes soft after two exchanges → paste "Stay in character; you are still skeptical" and re-read your last answer: you probably gave a genuinely good answer, or the model is being agreeable. Ask it which.
  • You keep winning easily → your scenario has no uncomfortable facts in it. Put the three you listed in step 1 into the scenario and re-run.
  • You froze on a question → excellent, that's the finding. Write the answer you wish you had given, out loud, twice. That sentence will be there next time.

✅ Done when

Both personas run, honesty and bounding scored 4+ in the final run, zero commitments you'd regret in week 8, and your three-worst-moments list written with replacement sentences.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~2 h + a reaction
The communication pack

🎯 Objective

Produce the catalog evidence — "technical brief, executive update and risk communication" — for one real engagement, at a standard where a stakeholder who attended nothing can act on all three without asking you a clarifying question.

📦 What you produce

Three artifacts about the same project, written for three different audiences, mutually consistent — plus a stakeholder's reaction and one documented revision. Consistency across the three is what makes this evidence rather than three writing samples.

📋 Artifact 1 — One-page technical brief

Pick a decision that is genuinely open right now on your project — model choice, an integration path, a build-vs-buy, a threshold policy. Use the Part III(a) skeleton:

# Technical brief — {decision}                  {date} · {you} · 1 page
## Problem            business framing first, 3 sentences, one number
## Options            2–4, each with its real trade-off (+ and −)
## Recommendation     one option, the reason, and when to revisit it
## Risks & mitigations   what could go wrong · what you'll do · how measured
## Decision requested  the ask · the approver · the date · who was consulted

Self-test before sending: could a non-technical approver make the call from this page alone, and could a customer engineer challenge your recommendation using only what's written here? If the second is impossible, you hid a trade-off.

📋 Artifact 2 — Weekly executive update

A real one, for a real week — sent, not drafted. Outcome first, one metric with its bar, blockers as asks with names and dates, next week, risk watch. Under 200 words. If your project has no metric yet, that is the finding: go define one (FD4) before writing the update, because an update without a measurable outcome is a task list wearing a suit.

📋 Artifact 3 — Risk communication note

Choose a risk that is currently live and not yet formally disclosed — the one you've been privately hoping resolves itself. That discomfort is the point of the exercise. Five parts, no hedging:

RISK:              one sentence, plain language, no softening
LIKELIHOOD:        high/med/low + the evidence, not a feeling
IMPACT IF IT LANDS: in hours, money, dates or users — never "significant"
MITIGATION IN FLIGHT: what you are already doing, unasked
WHAT I NEED FROM YOU: a decision, an access, or explicitly nothing

🔬 Final steps

1
Draft all three in one sitting (about 90 minutes), then leave them overnight. Re-read in the morning with one question: "what would this reader do immediately after finishing?" If the answer is "nothing" or "ask me something", rewrite the ask.
2
Consistency pass. Read all three as if you were a stakeholder who received all three. Any number that appears twice must match. Any risk in artifact 3 that materially affects the decision in artifact 1 must be visible in artifact 1. Inconsistency between your own documents is the single most expensive credibility leak in an engagement.
3
Send them for real — to the actual stakeholders. If you have no live engagement, use a senior colleague as proxy for each role (a tech lead for the brief, an engagement manager for the update and the risk note) and brief them to react as that persona, not as a reviewer.
4
Capture the reaction, not just the feedback. Record: did they make the decision? did they ask a clarifying question (which one — that's your weakest section)? did the risk note produce calm engagement or alarm? Reaction is the real score; comments on wording are secondary.
5
Revise once and record what changed and why, in two or three lines. The revision note is part of the evidence: it shows you can take a reaction and convert it into a better artifact rather than a defence.
6
File the skeletons. Strip the specifics from all three and keep them as your personal templates. At score 3 you should be producing a brief in 40 minutes and an update in 15, every week, without a blank-page moment.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • The stakeholder didn't decide → the brief presented a menu instead of a recommendation, or the ask had no date. Both are fixable in one paragraph.
  • The risk note caused alarm → almost always a missing "mitigation in flight" or a missing "what I need from you". Panic is the space left where the plan should have been.
  • They replied "looks good" and nothing else → they didn't read it, or there was nothing to act on. Follow up with one live question: "which option do you want?" — and note that as the real feedback.
  • You can't find a live undisclosed risk → either the project is unusually healthy, or you've been rationalising one. Ask a teammate what worries them about your project; it takes ten seconds to find.

✅ Done when

Three consistent artifacts exist for one real engagement, a real stakeholder reacted to each, the brief produced an actual decision, and one documented revision has been made. That pack — technical brief, executive update and risk communication — is your FD5 evidence.
