Pre-sales Support

Technical demos in sales cycles, RFP/RFI responses and architecture proposals with the sales team — the phase where the project you will later deliver gets its shape, its scope and its promises.

Why it matters for an FDE: A good FDE doubles deal win rates — and understanding pre-sales helps you take on feasible projects instead of impossible ones.
Part I · Mindset

The deal you help shape is the project you will deliver

Many engineers treat pre-sales as someone else's job — an interruption from real work, full of adjectives and optimism. That instinct is expensive, because every constraint that will define your next six months gets fixed here: the scope, the timeline, the accuracy expectation, the integration nobody checked, and the sentence in the proposal that says "the system will process invoices with 99% accuracy". Bad scoping now is a death march later — and you will be the one marching.

There is an upside too, and it is why this module exists at all. An engineer in the room changes the conversation: buyers of AI have been marketed at relentlessly and can smell it. A person who says "that part is hard, and here's why" is the most persuasive thing a vendor can put in front of a technical buyer. That is the mechanism behind the win-rate claim — not charisma, credibility.

You raise win rate

Specific, evidenced answers beat polished slides with technical buyers. Honesty about limits reads as competence.

You protect delivery

The assumptions section you write in week zero is what you point at in month four. Nobody else will write it.

You choose your projects

Seeing deals early means you can kill the unfeasible ones before they become your quarter.

The sales motion in plain terms — and where you plug in

Two stages deserve a warning. Qualification is where an AE decides whether the deal is real; if you are pulled into a demo for an unqualified prospect, you are being used as free consulting — say so politely and ask for the qualification answers first. Security review is where deals die quietly and late; the questionnaire that arrives in week eight could have been answered in week two, and the answers are yours to give (PR5, PR13).

Working with an AE: the contract between you

What the AE needs from youWhat you need from the AE

The safe form of every one of those is a conditional: "On the 200 invoices you send us, we'll measure field-level accuracy and report it before you commit to phase 2." You have promised a measurement, which you control, instead of a number, which you do not.

Part II · The demo

A sales demo is not a delivery demo

In FD2 you demo to a customer who has already bought in, using their real data, to drive a decision about what to build next. In a sales cycle you are demoing to people who owe you nothing, who are comparing you to two other vendors, and who will decide in the first five minutes whether this is about their problem or about your product.

DimensionDelivery demo (FD2)Sales demo (this module)
GoalGet feedback, steer the buildEarn the next meeting — usually a paid pilot or a POV

AudienceThe team you work with dailyMixed: a sponsor wanting outcomes, a skeptic wanting the failure mode, IT wanting the architecture

Fifteen minutes of discovery changes everything

The single biggest upgrade to any sales demo is refusing to give it cold. A short pre-call — even 15 minutes, even by email — turns a generic walkthrough into something that feels built for them, and it uses exactly the technique from FD1: ask about what happened, not what they want. 

# Pre-demo call — 15 minutes, 6 questions
1. "Walk me through how this work happens today — who touches it,
    in which system?"                        # gives you the demo's opening scene
2. "How many of these a day/week? How long does one take?"
                                             # gives you the value slide, in their numbers
3. "Show me — or describe — one real example. Can you send me 3?"
                                             # the highest-leverage ask on the call
4. "What have you already tried?"            # tells you what NOT to demo
5. "If we meet in 3 weeks and you're impressed, what did you see?"
                                             # they define your success criteria out loud
6. "Who's in the room, and what does each of them care about?"
                                             # lets you write one scene per person

Closing ask: "Send me 3 anonymized examples and I'll demo on those
instead of on our sample data."   ← if they say yes, the deal is real
The sample request is also a qualification test. A prospect who will not spend twenty minutes anonymizing three documents is not going to spend six figures. Their answer tells you more than the meeting will.

The 20-minute narrative

00–03 — Their world, in their words. One slide, no product: the workflow you heard on the pre-call, with their numbers. "Six people, about 140 emails a day, roughly 90 minutes each." You are proving you listened; you are also inviting the correction that saves the meeting.
03–06 — The moment. One end-to-end pass on their sample: input in, output out, in under three minutes. No architecture yet. The room should have one clear "oh" before minute six.
06–12 — The mechanism. Now open the box, at the level the room can absorb: where the data goes, where the human stays in control, what happens on low confidence, what it does when it does not know. This is where technical buyers decide whether you are real.
12–16 — The ugly case, deliberately. Run something it handles badly and show the flag, the fallback, the correction. Vendors hide this; showing it is the strongest credibility move available in a competitive cycle (FD6).
16–20 — The path. What a first phase looks like, what you'd need from them, when they'd see a number. End with one specific next step and a date — never "let us know what you think".
  • Never open with architecture. Value, then mechanism. A diagram in minute one signals that this is a technology purchase; a working output in minute four signals that it is a business one.
  • Narrate as the user, not the builder. "It's Tuesday morning, 40 bookings are waiting" beats "here we call the extraction endpoint".
  • Two paths ready. Live demo primary, recorded backup on your machine. Wi-Fi and vendor rate limits do not respect sales cycles.
  • Leave silence after the moment. Three seconds after the output appears. The room's first unprompted question tells you what the deal is actually about.

The customization dial: what you fake, what you build

Every re-skin costs time you do not have. The dial below is the honest version of "how far do we go for this opportunity" — the rule that keeps you out of trouble is simple: the AI behaviour must be real; the surroundings may be staged, as long as you say so.

Dial positionEffortUse whenSay this out loud
0 · Generic demo0 hEarly exploratory call, unqualified"This is our sample data — tell me where your case differs."
1 · Their data, real pipeline2–4 hThe default for any qualified deal"These are the three documents you sent. Nothing is pre-computed."
2 · Their data + their vocabulary/UI skin4–8 hCompetitive deal, decision within weeks"The screen is a mock-up of how it would sit in your ERP; the extraction behind it is live."
3 · Mock integration (fake system of record)1–2 dLarge deal, integration is the objection"This SAP screen is simulated — the API call it would make is what we'd build in phase 1."
4 · Real POV on their systems1–3 wksOnly when paid, or a very large deal with a signed planTreat as a delivery mini-project: success criteria in writing first (FD4)
The line you never cross: pre-computed output presented as live inference. If the model is not running, the sentence is "this is a recorded run" — every time. Getting caught staging AI results ends the deal and the reputation; being transparent about a mock UI costs nothing at all.

Handling live questions: the four answers to "can it do X?"

✓ YES — plus the shape

Never a bare yes; a bare yes sounds like every other vendor. "Yes — that's the same extraction path you just saw, pointed at a different document type. We'd need about 20 examples of that type to tune and measure it."

◐ YES, WITH WORK

"Yes, but that's a build, not a configuration — roughly two to three weeks, and it depends on whether your CRM exposes a write API. Can I confirm that and come back Thursday?" Size it, condition it, date it.

↷ NO, BUT

"Not that way — a model that files legally binding documents unsupervised isn't something I'd recommend. What works is drafting plus one-click approval, which removes about 80% of the time and keeps the accountability where your auditors expect it."

✗ NO

"No. That's outside what this does well, and I'd rather tell you now than in month three." A clean no is a credibility deposit you will spend later in the same meeting. Say it early and the room starts believing your yeses.

The phrase that buys time without losing authority: "Good question — I don't want to guess at it. Let me check and send you a precise answer by {day}." Then send it by that day. Two of those, delivered on time, are worth more than an hour of confident improvisation — and improvisation in a sales meeting is how impossible commitments enter a contract.
Part III · Paper

RFPs, questionnaires and the architecture proposal

An RFP (request for proposal) is a structured document a buyer sends to several vendors; an RFI is its lighter cousin, used to build a shortlist. Both are read by people scoring answers against criteria — often with weights you can ask for. Your job is not to write beautifully. It is to be specific, evidenced, and easy to score.

Most RFPs allow written clarification questions, and most vendors waste them on trivia. Use yours to make the un-answerable requirements answerable — "please confirm the definition of accuracy and the sample it will be measured on" is a question that helps the buyer, differentiates you as the serious bidder, and removes the clause that would have hurt you in delivery.

Answer patterns: specific, evidenced, no adjectives

Scorers cannot award points for enthusiasm. Every answer follows the same skeleton: direct answer → how it works → evidence → limit or condition. Four sentences beats four paragraphs.

✗ Before

"Our cutting-edge AI platform leverages state-of-the-art large language models to deliver best-in-class accuracy for intelligent document processing, ensuring your team can focus on high-value work while our robust, enterprise-grade solution handles the rest."

✓ After

"Yes. We extract 14 structured fields from scanned invoices using an LLM pipeline with per-field confidence scores. On a 500-document benchmark of a client's own invoices (Jan 2026), field-level F1 was 95.1%; methodology and the full confusion table are in Appendix C. Fields below a configurable confidence threshold — about 12% of volume at the default 0.85 — are routed to a human review queue that ships with the system. Handwritten totals are the weakest field type (≈ 88%) and are always flagged for review."

  • Answer the question asked, in the first sentence. Scorers read the first line of every answer and skim the rest.
  • Numbers with provenance. "95.1% field-level F1 on 500 client invoices, Jan 2026" is evidence; "high accuracy" is noise. If you have no number, say what you would measure and when.
  • Include one limit per major answer. Buyers who have run AI pilots trust the bidder who names a weakness; it is also how you avoid promising what delivery cannot do.
  • Mirror their vocabulary. If they say "matters", do not say "cases". Scoring is done by humans matching words to requirements.
  • Never leave a question blank. "Not supported today. Our alternative is X; a native implementation would be Y weeks if it is a requirement" scores higher than silence, which scores zero.

Build the answer library from day one

The second RFP should cost a third of the first. That only happens if every answer you write is saved in a reusable, dated, evidence-linked form — not buried in a submitted PDF.

The security questionnaire — where AI deals actually die

Enterprise security review is the stage least under sales' control and most under yours. The questions repeat across customers, so the library pays back fastest here. What matters is not having every control in place — it is answering with precision and never overstating.

The one rule for security answers: never claim a control you do not have. These answers become contractual representations, and the person who wrote them is you. The honest gap answer wins deals anyway: "We are not SOC 2 certified today; our audit starts in Q1. In the meantime we can offer {specific compensating controls} and a right-to-audit clause." Buyers hear that from almost no one.

The architecture proposal — a lighter FD3

A pre-sales architecture proposal is not the solution architecture you will build (FD3) — you do not know enough yet, and pretending otherwise is how fixed-price disasters begin. It is a credible reference shape plus the integration points, the phases, and — most importantly — the assumptions the price depends on.

Part IV · Scoping & risk

Estimating a project you have not scoped yet

Pre-sales asks you to estimate with a fraction of the information you would want. The professional response is not refusal and not false confidence — it is an estimate with its assumptions welded to it, and a phase 0 that converts the biggest unknowns into facts before anyone commits to a number that matters.

Run the feasibility triangle early, on thin evidence

The same three risks from FD1 — value, AI fit, data — apply here, just with worse information. Ask the three questions in the first technical call and grade your confidence rather than the answer:

The assumptions section is your protection

Every estimate is a function of things you have assumed. Write them as testable statements with a consequence attached, and read them aloud in the proposal meeting — an assumption nobody heard is an assumption that will be disputed later (FD6, FD8). 

## Assumptions — this estimate holds if:
A1 Sandbox access to {system} is granted within 10 working days of kickoff.
   → each week of delay moves delivery by one week.
A2 Customer provides 200 representative documents for benchmarking in
   phase 0. → without them, no accuracy commitment can be made.
A3 One named business owner is available 4 h/week for review sessions.
A4 Integration is via the documented {API}; no changes to {system} are
   required from our side.
A5 Volumes stay within {N}/day. Above that, an infrastructure review applies.
A6 Security review starts by {date} and uses the customer's standard
   questionnaire (not a bespoke audit).

## Exclusions — not included in this price:
· data cleansing of historical records      · changes to {system} itself
· licences for third-party models/tools     · 24/7 support (business hours only)
· migration of the legacy {X} workflow      · training beyond 2 sessions (FD10)

## Accuracy statement
We do not commit to an accuracy figure before phase 0. We commit to
MEASURING field-level accuracy on 200 of your own documents and to
reporting it, with the review-queue design sized from the result.
The reframe that makes assumptions welcome: present them as "here is what we need from you for these dates to hold" rather than "here is what we're not responsible for". Same content, opposite reception — one sounds like partnership, the other like a disclaimer.

Pricing awareness: what each model does to risk

You will not set the price, but you must understand what the commercial model does to your delivery life — because the AE will ask "can we do this fixed price?" and your answer shapes the next six months.

Time & materialsCustomerExploratory work, trusted relationship, unclear scope — i.e. most phase 0sRequires trust and visible progress; procurement often resists it
Fixed priceYouGenuinely known scope: an integration, a migration, a defined pipelineLethal when the deliverable is a quality level. "97% accuracy, fixed price, before we've seen the data" is a promise to lose money
Capped T&M / phased fixedSharedThe usual compromise: fixed phase 0, then T&M or a fixed phase 1 priced with real knowledgeOnly works if the phase gate is real and someone is willing to stop

The FDE's contribution to this conversation is one sentence, said early: "We can fix the price of anything whose scope we can freeze. Accuracy is not scope until we've measured it on their data — so let's fix phase 0 and price phase 1 when we know."

Walk-away signals — and how to raise them internally

✗ Hard walk-aways
  • No data — and none to be shared before signature
  • No sponsor — nobody with budget who feels the pain
  • Impossible accuracy demanded with no human review allowed
  • No access to end users during delivery
  • Fixed price + undefined quality target + fixed date
  • Procurement-only contact; every technical question routed through a form
◐ Yellow flags — bid, but reshape
  • "The board asked for AI" with no named workflow → propose discovery
  • Data exists but nobody knows its quality → phase 0 with a data probe
  • A failed previous AI project → gold mine, if you can interview them
  • Integration into a system nobody in their IT owns → scope the access path first
  • Timeline set by an external event → phase to protect the date, cut scope not quality

Saying "we shouldn't bid" to a salesperson with a quota is a communication problem, not a technical one. Do not lead with "no"; lead with what would make it winnable. A short written memo beats a hallway opinion, because it can be forwarded and it survives the pressure of the next call:

In the field

A manufacturing group runs a competitive evaluation for AI-assisted supplier-document processing. Three vendors, a 90-question RFP, and a 45-minute demo slot each.

Vendor A demos a polished generic product on their own sample invoices. Vendor B demos a platform: eleven features in forty minutes, architecture first. Golden Owl's FDE spends fifteen minutes on a pre-call the week before, asks for three real documents, and gets them — including one scanned at an angle with handwriting in the margin.

The demo opens with the prospect's own numbers ("four people, about 300 supplier documents a week, roughly six minutes each") and runs their three documents live. The angled scan comes out with two low-confidence fields, which the FDE deliberately leaves on screen: "this is what I want you to see — it doesn't guess, it flags. On your sample of three, that's the one a human would check." Then five minutes on the SAP write-back and its open question about the auth model, and a phase plan ending in a measured accuracy number before any production commitment.

The RFP response contained one sentence the evaluation committee later quoted back: "We do not commit to an accuracy figure before phase 0; we commit to measuring it on 200 of your own documents and reporting it whatever it says." Both other vendors had promised 99%. The deal was won on the answer that refused to promise.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ The eleven-feature demo

Showing everything you built proves you can build; it does not prove you understand them. One use case, all the way through, on their data.

✗ Answering "can it do X?" with "yes"

Bare yeses are what every vendor says, so they carry no information — and each one becomes a delivery obligation. Yes plus shape, size and condition.

✗ Accuracy numbers without a benchmark

Quoting another client's 96% as if it transfers to their data is the most common way engineers create an unwinnable delivery. Different documents, different number.

✗ Free consulting on an unqualified deal

Two weeks of custom POV for a prospect with no budget and no sponsor. Ask for the qualification facts before you build anything.

✗ Letting the proposal go out unread

Sales edits happen. Someone may "tighten" your conditional accuracy sentence into a commitment. Read the final document before it is sent, every time.

✓ Ask for three real samples

It transforms the demo, tests the data reality, and qualifies the prospect — one request doing three jobs.

✓ Show a failure on purpose

The flag-and-review moment is the most persuasive thirty seconds in an AI demo — for anyone who has been burned by a pilot before, which is most buyers now.

✓ Write the answer library as you go

Every question answered once, saved with its evidence and a review date. By the third RFP it turns a two-week scramble into two days.

🔬 Steps

1
Separate the three layers that change per customer from the engine that does not. This is the entire trick of the lab: 
demo/
  engine/              # never changes: pipeline, confidence gate, review UI
  profiles/
    logistics.yaml     # ← the ONLY thing you edit per prospect
    insurance.yaml
  data/
    logistics/*.pdf    # 5-8 documents, at least one ugly
  scripts/
    new_profile.sh     # scaffolds a profile from a template in 1 command
    warmup.sh          # pre-flight: keys, index build, one live call
profile: logistics
brand:      { name: "Acme Freight", logo: assets/acme.png, accent: "#0B5" }
vocabulary: { item: "booking", queue: "operations desk", actor: "operator" }
fields:     [shipper, consignee, container_no, eta, incoterm, weight_kg]
documents:  data/logistics/
narrative:
  pain:   "6 operators · ~140 booking emails/day · ~90 min each"
  before: "re-keyed by hand into the TMS"
  after:  "prefilled, operator confirms; low confidence goes to review"
ugly_case:  data/logistics/scan_angled_handwritten.pdf
2
Make the field list and vocabulary data-driven. If changing "container_no" to "policy_no" requires editing code in three files, the 30-minute target is impossible. Prompts should be templated from the profile (AI1), and every user-visible label should come from the vocabulary map.
3
Build in the ugly case as a first-class feature. Every profile must include one document that produces low confidence, and the UI must show the flag and the review path clearly. This is the demo's most valuable twenty seconds (Part II) — it cannot be an afterthought you hope to find on the day.
4
Write warmup.sh. Ten minutes before any demo it should: verify the API key and quota, rebuild the index, run one real inference end to end, and print a green/red summary. Most demo disasters are environmental, and they are all preventable by a script you run in the waiting room.
5
Record the fallback. A 3-minute screen recording of the perfect run, stored locally. If the network dies you say "let me show you the recorded version and we'll run it live afterwards" — calm, professional, meeting saved.
6
The re-skin drill — the actual test. Have a colleague pick an industry you did not build for and hand you 5 public sample documents. Start a timer. 
30-minute clock:
 0-5   run new_profile.sh · drop documents in data/{industry}/
 5-15  set field list + vocabulary + the 3 narrative lines
15-22  run the pipeline · check outputs · pick the ugly case
22-27  branding + a dry run of the 3-minute happy path
27-30  warmup.sh green · fallback recording exported
Then have the colleague do it for a different industry, using only your README. If they cannot, the asset is a personal toy, not a firm asset — fix the automation, not their understanding.
7
Write the one-page demo README: what this pattern proves, what it deliberately does not, the 30-minute re-skin procedure, and the three questions it always gets asked (with the answers). Store it with the asset.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • The re-skin takes 3 hours. Normal on the first attempt. Note exactly where the time went — it is almost always hard-coded field names or prompts. Fix those two and re-run the drill.
  • Quality collapses on the new industry. An honest and useful finding: it tells you the prompt was overfitted to your original documents. It also tells you what to say in a real demo — "we tune per document type; that tuning is phase 0 work."
  • You are tempted to hard-code an impressive output. Don't. Build the ugly case into the profile instead; a real low-confidence flag is more persuasive than a fake perfect run, and it cannot blow up in your face.
  • No public sample documents for the industry. Generate realistic ones with an LLM, then say so on the call: "these are synthetic samples in your format — send me three real ones and I'll rerun this."

✅ Done when

A colleague, using only your README, retargeted the demo to an unfamiliar industry and dataset in under 30 minutes and ran it end to end including the ugly case; warmup.sh exists and passes; a local fallback recording exists. Keep the profiles you build — they compound into a library that makes every future deal cheaper to chase.
Lab 2 · ~1.5 h
Mock sales demo under fire

🎯 Objective

Run a 20-minute technical demo to a hostile-but-fair prospect and handle five hard questions without over-promising, under-selling, or freezing. Recorded, scored, and repeated — the delta between run one and run two is the learning.

📦 What you need

  • Your Lab 1 demo, re-skinned to a scenario your colleague chooses (they may not tell you until 30 minutes before — that is realistic).
  • A colleague willing to play the prospect properly, briefed with the role card below.
  • A recording of screen + audio. Non-negotiable: the self-review is the lab.
  • A timer visible to you, not to them.

🔬 Steps

1
Do the pre-call first — 10 minutes, in character. Use the six questions from Part II. Your colleague answers as the prospect. This is part of the exercise: most engineers skip it in practice, and the demo that follows is measurably worse.
2
Give your colleague the role card: 
You are the prospect team in a 45-minute vendor call. Between you,
play three people:
 · OPS DIRECTOR — cares about time saved and their team's workload.
   Interrupts with "but what about when...?" using real edge cases.
 · IT ARCHITECT — sceptical, technical. Cares about integration,
   data flow, where our data goes, and what happens at scale.
 · SKEPTIC (burned by a failed AI pilot in 2024) — quiet, then asks
   the sharpest question in the room around minute 12.

Rules: be fair, not cruel. Interrupt at least twice. Ask at least
5 of the hard questions on the list. If the vendor gives a vague or
inflated answer, push once more: "can you be specific?"
End by stating out loud: would you take the next meeting, and why?
3
The five hard questions (your colleague picks at least five; you do not see which): 
1. "How accurate is it?"                     → benchmark, denominator, limits
2. "We tried this in 2024 and it failed. Why is this different?"
3. "Does our data leave our environment? Is it used for training?"
4. "Can it integrate with our on-prem SAP?"  → auth model, who owns it
5. "How long and how much?"                  → phases, assumptions, phase 0
6. "Can you guarantee 99%?"                  → the reframe to measurement
7. "What happens when the model provider changes the model?"
8. "Why you and not {big vendor}?"
9. "Can it also do {adjacent thing you don't do}?"  → NO, or NO-BUT
10. "Who else in our industry have you done this for?"  → honesty test
4
Run the 20-minute demo to the Part II narrative: their world → the moment → the mechanism → the ugly case → the path. Keep the timer discipline: if you are still on slide two at minute six, skip to the live run.
5
Score it immediately, both of you independently, then compare: 
1-5 each:
 · Opened with THEIR world, not our product
 · Reached a live output before minute 6
 · Named a limitation unprompted
 · Every number given was traceable to a measurement
 · Handled interruptions without losing the thread
 · Used the four answer patterns (yes / yes-with-work / no-but / no)
 · Said "I'll confirm by {day}" instead of guessing, at least once
 · Closed with a specific dated next step
PLUS from the prospect: "next meeting?" yes/no — and the one moment
that decided it.
6
Watch the recording with a notepad, specifically hunting for: promises you did not intend to make, numbers without provenance, and moments where you talked over an objection instead of answering it. Write down the three sentences you would change.
7
Re-run within 48 hours with a different scenario and a different colleague. The second run is where the pattern locks in. Log both scores; the trend is the evidence.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • You over-promise under pressure. The most common failure, and the reason for the recording. Adopt one rescue phrase and practise it until it is automatic: "I don't want to guess — I'll confirm that by Thursday."
  • You freeze on a question you cannot answer. Say what you know, name what you would check, give a date. Nobody expects an engineer to know their SAP instance; everyone expects a straight answer about what happens next.
  • The demo breaks live. Perfect practice. Switch to the fallback recording calmly, keep talking, and note how long the recovery took. Doing this once in a mock makes the real one a non-event.
  • Your colleague is too gentle. Ask them to re-run the last five minutes as the skeptic only. Being pushed twice on the same evasive answer is what trains precision.
  • You run to 35 minutes. Cut the mechanism section, not the ugly case. Overrunning in a real cycle costs you the Q&A, which is where the deal is actually decided.

✅ Done when

Two recorded runs exist, the second scoring higher on at least four criteria; you named a limitation unprompted in both; every number you gave can be traced to a measurement; and your prospect said yes to a next meeting for a reason they could articulate.
Lab 3 · ~2.5 h
Ten answers & the reusable library

🎯 Objective

Write ten RFP and security answers that a scorer could award full marks to and a lawyer could live with — then convert each into a dated, evidence-linked library entry so the next response costs a fraction of this one.

📦 What you need

  • A real system of yours to answer about — the labs from AI4, PR4 and PR13 give you real facts to cite. Answers about an imaginary system teach nothing, because the evidence step is where the work is.
  • Access to your actual model-provider terms (retention, training-on-data, sub-processors) — read them; do not assume.
  • A place for the library: a repo folder or wiki space with one file per answer.

🔬 Steps

1
Take these ten questions — they are the ones that recur across real enterprise RFPs and security reviews: 
 1. Describe your approach to extracting structured data from
    unstructured documents, including accuracy achieved to date.
 2. Is customer data used to train models? What is retained, where,
    and for how long?
 3. Describe tenant isolation: how is our data separated from other
    customers' data?
 4. What happens when the AI produces an incorrect result? Describe
    detection, correction and prevention of recurrence.
 5. Describe your controls against prompt injection and unauthorized
    data exfiltration via the AI system.
 6. What are the integration options with SAP / Salesforce /
    on-premise systems? Describe authentication.
 7. Describe your monitoring, alerting and incident response,
    including notification SLAs.
 8. How do you manage model changes and deprecations by your
    providers? What is the impact on us?
 9. Describe your data residency options, including any in-country
    or self-hosted deployment.
10. What are the resourcing, timeline and dependencies for a first
    production deployment?
2
Answer each in the four-part skeleton, 120–180 words: direct answer → how it works → evidence → limit/condition. Time-box to 12 minutes per answer; the library entry is where you polish, not the first draft.
3
Run the adjective purge. Delete every instance of: robust, seamless, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, best-in-class, enterprise-grade, powerful, intelligent, industry-leading. Then read what remains — if an answer lost its substance, it never had any, and that answer needs a real fact. 
Test each sentence: "could a competitor write the exact same words?"
  yes → it is marketing, cut or replace with a specific fact
  no  → it is evidence, keep it
4
Attach evidence to every claim, or downgrade the claim. Each number needs a source: a benchmark run with a date and sample size, a config file, a DPA clause, an architecture section. If the evidence does not exist, rewrite the answer as what you would measure and when.
5
Deliberately write two honest-gap answers. Pick two things you genuinely do not have (a certification, a native connector, an in-country deployment) and write the gap pattern: what is true today → the compensating control → the path and timeline → what we will not claim. These are the answers that build trust with technical evaluators, and the ones most engineers get wrong by bluffing.
6
Convert all ten into library entries using the format from Part III: question variants, category, last-reviewed date, owner, evidence links, confidence level, short answer, long answer, and a "do not say" line capturing the trap you nearly fell into.
7
Adversarial review. Have a colleague — or Claude — attack the set: 
"You are a security architect evaluating this vendor. For each answer:
 (1) what is the vaguest claim, and what evidence would you demand?
 (2) which answer would you push back on hardest, and with what
     follow-up question?
 (3) which answer, if any, sounds like it is hiding something?
Be specific and cite the sentence."
Rewrite the three weakest. Note that follow-up questions are gold: add them as question variants in the library so the next person is ready.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • You do not know the answer to a security question. That is the finding. Go read the provider's terms or your own config, and write down what is actually true. Half of pre-sales value is knowing your own system precisely.
  • Every answer is 400 words. Scorers skim. Cut to the four parts; move the depth to an appendix and reference it.
  • Answers drift from delivery reality. Cross-check each with the person who would deliver it — if that is you, ask "would I be comfortable if this sentence appeared in the contract?"
  • The library rots. Every entry has last_reviewed for a reason. Anything older than six months is marked stale before reuse — a confidently wrong security answer is worse than no library at all.

✅ Done when

Ten answers exist with zero banned adjectives, every claim carrying dated evidence, at least two written as honest gaps with compensating controls, all converted into library entries — and the adversarial review produced no "this sounds like it is hiding something" verdicts on the rewritten set.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~5 h
RFP response or architecture proposal

🎯 Objective

Produce the catalog evidence — "RFP response or pre-sales demo contribution" — as a complete, sendable technical document for a real or realistic opportunity, accompanied by the demo asset or recording you would bring to the same deal.

📦 Choose your opportunity

  • Best: a live Golden Owl opportunity — ask to own the technical sections, with a senior reviewing.
  • Good: a real public RFP. Government and university procurement portals publish AI/document-processing tenders continuously; pick one with a real requirements list.
  • Acceptable: a realistic scenario built from the industry you know best, with the requirements written by a colleague playing the buyer (they must invent at least two awkward requirements before you start).

📦 Component 1 — the technical response section

Not the whole RFP; the part an FDE owns. Minimum contents:

1  Understanding of the requirement      # their words, their numbers, 5 sentences
2  Proposed solution + reference architecture diagram
3  Approach to accuracy & quality        # benchmark plan, confidence gating,
                                          # human review design, eval regime (AI9)
4  Integration approach                   # table: system/direction/mechanism/owner/open Q
5  Security & data handling               # from your Lab 3 library entries
6  Delivery plan & phases                 # phase 0 → pilot → production → handover (FD10)
7  Team & ways of working                 # embedded model (FD7), cadence (FD5)
8  Assumptions, dependencies & exclusions # the protection section — never omit
9  Risks & mitigations                    # 5 rows, honest ones, with owners

📦 Component 2 — the demo contribution

Either the Lab 1 asset re-skinned to this opportunity with its profile committed, or a recorded 20-minute demo run for this scenario (Lab 2 counts if it was for the same case), plus the short deck: their world, the moment, the mechanism, the path.

🔬 Steps

1
Triage the requirements first using the Part III flow: read the evaluation criteria before anything else, tag every requirement MUST/SHOULD/NICE/WEIRD, and list the clarification questions you would submit. Include that question list in your deliverable — it demonstrates the judgment the document itself cannot show.
2
Do the bid/no-bid honestly. Write two paragraphs: would you bid, in what shape, and what would make you walk away? If your honest answer is no-bid, write it as the internal memo from Part IV — that is a legitimate and often stronger deliverable than a bid you do not believe in.
3
Draft sections 1–4 from your own real system knowledge. Every accuracy statement must be conditional on a phase 0 measurement; every integration row must name the open question rather than assume it away.
4
Assemble sections 5 and 8–9 from the library (Lab 3) and the assumptions template in Part IV. Read the assumptions section aloud once — if any of them would embarrass you to say to the customer's face, it is either badly worded or you do not actually believe the estimate.
5
Price the shape, not the number. You are not setting rates; you are producing effort bands per phase with the dependencies that move them: "phase 1: 6 weeks, 2 engineers, assumes A1–A4; each week of access delay moves the end date one week."
6
Two reviews before you call it done. A senior engineer reviews for deliverability ("would you sign up to build exactly this?"), and a non-technical colleague reviews for readability ("what are they buying, and what could go wrong?"). Both notes get incorporated.
7
Write your one-page reflection for the portfolio: which requirements you judged pre-wired and why, which promise you refused to make, and what your bid/no-bid recommendation was. At score 2 this reflection is what proves the judgment, not just the document.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • You have no real opportunity. Use a public tender — the requirement lists are genuinely awkward in the way real ones are, which is the point of the exercise.
  • Sales edits your conditional accuracy sentence into a commitment. This is the single most important thing to catch in a real cycle. Push back in writing, once, with the delivery consequence stated plainly — and keep the thread.
  • The requirements are impossible. Good. Write the reshape: which MUSTs you meet, which you meet differently, which you decline, and what phase 0 would resolve. A response that reshapes honestly outscores a response that claims everything, with any evaluator who has been burned before.
  • You cannot fill the assumptions section. That means you do not understand the estimate yet. Work backwards from the dates: for each one, ask "what has to be true for this to hold?" — those are your assumptions.
  • It balloons to 30 pages. Evaluators score sections, not weight. Keep each section tight and push detail into appendices you reference by name.

✅ Done when

A complete technical response section or architecture proposal exists with all nine parts including assumptions and risks; the demo contribution (profile, recording or deck) is attached; two reviews are incorporated; and a senior colleague answered yes to "would you deliver exactly what this document promises?". At score 2 you produce this with that senior's guidance — at score 3, you own the whole technical side of the deal and drive the bid/no-bid call yourself.
