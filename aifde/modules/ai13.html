Reasoning Models & Extended Thinking

When to spend thinking tokens and when they are pure cost — the effort control, the prompting differences, interleaved thinking inside an agent loop, and how to verify an answer whose reasoning you are not allowed to trust.

Why it matters for an FDE: every engagement now opens with "which model?", and the honest answer is a routing table with measured numbers behind it. An FDE who can show that effort=low matches effort=high on 80% of a customer's traffic just cut their inference bill without touching quality — and the 20% where it doesn't is the argument for spending more, made with evidence instead of vibes.
Part I · Why & when

Reasoning is a budget you spend, not a personality you buy

The industry talks about reasoning models as if they were a different kind of thing — smarter, more careful, the one you use when it matters. That framing produces exactly one engineering decision ("use the smart one") and exactly one bill (enormous). The useful framing is narrower and it is the whole of this module: a reasoning model spends serial compute before it answers, you choose how much, and on most of your traffic the answer does not change. Everything downstream — the effort control in Part II, the prompt rewrite in Part III, the routing table in Part V — follows from taking that sentence literally and then measuring it.

The thing that makes this an FDE skill rather than a research question is that the measurement is cheap. Twenty tasks, five effort levels, a scoring function you already own from AI9, and you have a curve that ends the argument in the room. Lab 1 builds that harness in two hours. Every number in this module comes from running it against one reference workload, and the numbers stay consistent from here to the memo in Lab 4.

What a thinking token actually buys

A non-reasoning model does a fixed amount of computation per output token. If the answer requires four dependent steps, the model has to do all four in the forward passes that produce the answer's first tokens — which is why chain-of-thought prompting works at all on those models: it converts internal serial work into externally visible tokens, giving the model more forward passes to do it in. AI1 owns that technique and it is still correct for Haiku-class models. A reasoning model does the same thing natively: it emits a thinking block first, and only then the answer. The block is not a courtesy explanation. It is the computation.

Three consequences follow immediately, and each one is a section of this module. Thinking tokens are billed as output tokens — at Opus rates that is $75 per million, which is why Part V exists. Thinking tokens are serial, so they land on your p99 latency, not on some background thread. And the thinking text is a byproduct of the computation, not a record of it, which is why Part VI tells you not to treat it as evidence.

That bar is drawn to scale from the reference workload used throughout this module: 4,200 input tokens, 600 visible output tokens, 3,100 thinking tokens at effort=high on claude-opus-5. The thinking block is 68% of the request cost and 84% of the output tokens, and it does not appear in any log your product team is looking at. The single most common finding in a cost review is that nobody instrumented it.

Boundary: the mechanics of the Messages API — request shape, streaming events, the tool-use loop — belong to AI2. This module assumes you can already make a call and reads only the fields that reasoning changes.

The four task classes where reasoning measurably wins

Reasoning pays when the task has dependent steps whose intermediate results are not in the prompt. That is the whole test, and it partitions real workloads more cleanly than any taxonomy of "hard" and "easy". Four families show up in engagements over and over.

  • Multi-step arithmetic over messy inputs — invoice reconciliation, unit conversion chains, pro-rated billing. Each step consumes the previous step's number, and one slip poisons everything after it.
  • Constraint satisfaction — shift rostering, shipment consolidation, seating plans. The model must hold a partial assignment in mind, detect a violation, and backtrack. Backtracking is precisely what a single forward pass cannot do.
  • Code debugging from a failing test — not "write a function", which reasoning barely helps with, but "here is a stack trace and three files, find the cause". The search over hypotheses is serial.
  • Ambiguous-spec triage — a support ticket that must be routed to one of nine queues and return the list of facts still missing. The second half is where the gains are: deciding what you do not know requires enumerating what you would need.

The four classes where it is pure cost

The mirror image of the test above: when every fact the answer needs is already in the prompt and the transformation is shallow, extra serial compute has nothing to do. It does not usually make the answer worse — it makes it later and dearer, and occasionally it talks itself out of a correct first instinct. These four classes are, in most engagements, the majority of traffic.

Pure cost (measured Δ ≤ 0.01)
  • Field extraction — invoice PDF to 12 JSON fields. 0.96 at low, 0.96 at high.
  • Classification — ticket to one of nine labels. 0.94 → 0.93; high is worse, inside noise.
  • Reformatting — Markdown to the customer's XML. 0.99 → 0.98.
  • Retrieval-grounded QA — answer from five supplied chunks. 0.88 → 0.89.
Why they flatline
  • No intermediate result is hidden — the answer is a projection of the input.
  • The bottleneck is recall of the input, which more thinking does not improve.
  • Errors are perceptual (a misread column), not logical.
  • On grounded QA the ceiling is your retriever, not your model — that is AI4's problem, not this one's.

The classification row is worth dwelling on, because it is the one people refuse to believe. Extra thinking on a nine-way label task produces a paragraph of deliberation about edge cases and then, roughly one time in forty, picks the edge case over the obvious answer. 0.94 → 0.93 is not a win you paid 2.8× for; it is a small regression you paid 2.8× for. If your intuition says this cannot be right, that is exactly the intuition Lab 2 exists to falsify on your own data.

The escalation ladder: reasoning is rung four

When output quality is bad, "turn up the effort" is the cheapest thing to type and almost never the right first move. There is a fixed order of things to try, ranked by cost-to-fix and by how much of the problem they actually address. Reasoning is fourth. Most teams jump to it from first, and then cannot tell whether it helped, because they changed the model at the same time as the prompt.

A reasoning model doing long division in its head at $75 per million output tokens is a worse calculator than decimal.Decimal, which costs nothing and is right every time. On the invoice reconciliation route, giving the model a sum_lines tool moved effort=low from 0.62 to 0.88 — within 0.03 of what effort=high achieved without the tool, at a third of the cost. Try rung 3 before rung 4 every single time.

Read those two comment lines as a negotiating position. Three points of accuracy for thirteen times the price is a trade a customer might well take on a dispute workflow and would never take on a dashboard — but it is now their decision, made against a number, which is exactly where you want it.

Score-4 move: when a customer asks you to "make it smarter", decline the effort bump until rungs 1–3 have been tried one at a time with the eval re-run between each. The deliverable is not a better model setting; it is a four-row attribution table saying which change bought which points. That table is what survives the next model release — the effort setting will not.
Part II · The control surface

Adaptive thinking and effort replaced every knob you learned

If you learned this API in the era of budget_tokens and sampling temperatures, the current surface will feel alarmingly small. That is the point. The model now decides how much to think per request; you supply a policy, not a number. Two fields carry the whole control surface, three familiar parameters now return HTTP 400, and one technique you have muscle memory for — assistant prefill — is gone entirely. This Part is the reference. Get it wrong and you will spend an afternoon debugging a 400 that says exactly what is wrong and that you will not read carefully.

The two fields, and nothing else

thinking turns reasoning on. output_config.effort says how much of it you are willing to pay for. There is no third field, no per-request token budget, and no way to force a specific thinking length — which is a feature, because the old budget_tokens made you guess a number for a distribution you had never measured.

How thinking blocks arrive when you stream

Above roughly 16,000 max_tokens the non-streaming endpoint will reject the request, because a single response can now take minutes to generate and no sensible HTTP timeout covers it. Since a high-effort reasoning request routinely needs 8,000+ tokens of headroom and an xhigh one needs far more, streaming stops being an optimisation and becomes the default calling convention for this module. The SDK makes it painless, provided you know that thinking arrives on its own delta type.

get_final_message() is what you keep — it is a normal Message with the thinking blocks, their signatures, and the usage totals, and it is the object you append to messages for the next turn. Do not reconstruct a message from the deltas you rendered; you will lose the signature and the following turn will 400. The streaming mechanics themselves — event taxonomy, reconnection, error frames — belong to AI2.

28 seconds of silence is a product bug, not a model bug. Users abandon at around 8 seconds of no feedback. Either render a thinking status off thinking_delta, or drop the route to effort=medium where p50 is 4.8 s. What you must not do is ship xhigh behind a static spinner and then be surprised by the drop-off funnel.

Carrying thinking blocks across turns

A thinking block carries a cryptographic signature. When you send the conversation back — which you do on every tool result and every follow-up turn — the API verifies that the thinking you are replaying is the thinking it produced, unmodified. This is what makes it safe for the model to condition on its own earlier reasoning. It also means you may not edit, truncate, summarise or re-order thinking blocks, and the "trim old turns to save tokens" helper that every codebase grows will break the moment it touches one.

Occasionally a block comes back with type redacted_thinking and an opaque data field instead of readable text. That is a safety-system redaction, it is normal, and the handling is identical: pass it through untouched. Code that filters on b.type == "thinking" and silently drops everything else will break on the first redaction, which in practice happens weeks after launch on a request nobody can reproduce.

Breaks the signature chain
  • Filtering blocks by type when appending an assistant turn.
  • Re-serialising through your own dataclass and losing unknown fields.
  • Summarising old turns with a second model call.
  • Reordering blocks so tool_use comes first.
Safe
  • Appending msg.content as-is.
  • Dropping whole turn pairs from the front of the list.
  • Storing the raw JSON and rehydrating it verbatim.
  • Logging a hash of the thinking text for debugging, not the text.

One wrapper you will reuse in every lab

Everything above collapses into about forty lines that you should write once and import everywhere. It streams unconditionally, checks stop_reason before touching content, separates thinking from answer, and — the part people forget — records what it cost. A wrapper that does not emit a usage record is why nobody can forecast the bill in Part V.

reasoning.py — the module-wide helper, used by all four labs 
import anthropic, time, json, dataclasses
client = anthropic.Anthropic()

PRICES = {  # USD per 1M tokens (input, output)
    "claude-opus-5":   (15.00, 75.00),
    "claude-sonnet-5": (3.00,  15.00),
    "claude-haiku-4-5":(1.00,  5.00)}

@dataclasses.dataclass
class Result:
    text: str; thinking: str; blocks: list
    in_tok: int; out_tok: int; seconds: float; usd: float
    model: str; effort: str; stop: str

def ask(prompt, *, model="claude-opus-5", effort="high",
        system=None, tools=None, fmt=None, max_tokens=32000):

    if fmt: cfg["format"] = fmt
    kw = dict(model=model, max_tokens=max_tokens,
              thinking={"type": "adaptive"}, output_config=cfg,
              messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}])
    if system: kw["system"] = system
    if tools:  kw["tools"]  = tools

        for _ in s: pass
        msg = s.get_final_message()
    secs = time.perf_counter() - t0

    if msg.stop_reason == "refusal":
        raise RuntimeError("refusal")
    if msg.stop_reason == "max_tokens":
        raise RuntimeError(f"truncated at {max_tokens}; raise the ceiling")

    pin, pout = PRICES[model]
    return Result(
        text="".join(b.text for b in msg.content if b.type == "text"),
        thinking="".join(b.thinking for b in msg.content
                       if b.type == "thinking"),

        out_tok=msg.usage.output_tokens, seconds=secs,
        usd=msg.usage.input_tokens/1e6*pin + msg.usage.output_tokens/1e6*pout,
        model=model, effort=effort, stop=msg.stop_reason)

Two design choices are deliberate. max_tokens=32000 by default, because running out of room mid-thought wastes the whole request — you pay for every thinking token generated before the cut-off and get nothing. And it raises on max_tokens rather than returning a partial, because a truncated reasoning answer is confidently wrong in a way that a caller three layers up will not notice.

Part III · Prompting reasoning models

Your best non-reasoning prompt is the wrong prompt here

The prompts you are proudest of were tuned against a model that could not think before answering. Every technique in them — the step-by-step scaffold, the eight worked examples, the "first do X, then do Y" procedure, the prefilled opening brace — exists to compensate for that. Point the same prompt at a reasoning model and you are not just wasting the effort; you are actively constraining a model that would have found a better decomposition than the one you hand-wrote. The measured penalty on the reconciliation route is 0.17 accuracy and 2,300 wasted thinking tokens per request.

AI1 owns prompting technique and remains correct for Haiku-class models, where all of the above still works. This Part owns only the delta: what to delete, what to write instead, and how to prove the rewrite helped.

"Think step by step" is now redundant-to-harmful

There are two distinct failure modes and it is worth separating them, because they call for different fixes. The redundant case is a bare "think step by step" with no procedure attached: the model was going to think anyway, the instruction adds nothing, and it costs you eight input tokens. Harmless, but it clutters a prompt that people will keep editing.

The harmful case is the one that costs accuracy: a prescribed procedure. "First list every line item. Then compute the subtotal. Then apply the fuel surcharge. Then compare to the booked rate." That is a plan, and the model will follow it — including on the 30% of invoices where the surcharge is embedded in the line items and applying it again double-counts. You have replaced the model's search over decompositions with your single guess at one, and your guess was written from three examples you happened to look at.

Delete from an AI1-era prompt
  • "Let's think step by step" / "Take a deep breath".
  • Numbered procedures the model should derive itself.
  • <scratchpad> / "show your working" tags.
  • Six or eight worked examples kept for their reasoning.
  • Assistant prefill — it is a 400 now anyway.
  • "Be careful", "double-check", "be thorough" — effort is the control for this.
Keep, and make sharper
  • The goal, stated once, unambiguously.
  • Every constraint and every edge rule — these get more valuable.
  • Domain facts the model cannot know (your surcharge policy).
  • The output contract, as a schema.
  • What to do when the input is insufficient.
  • One example, for shape only.

Part IV · Reasoning inside the loop

Interleaved thinking is where agents get their judgment

A single-shot reasoning call thinks once, at the start, about a problem it can see in full. An agent does not have that luxury: the useful information arrives as tool results, one at a time, and the decision that matters is what to do after the third result contradicts the plan you made after the first. Interleaved thinking means the model gets a thinking block after every tool result, not just at the top of the trajectory — and that is the difference between an agent that executes a plan and an agent that revises one.

The loop itself, its patterns, checkpointing and human-in-the-loop approval belong to AI6; the tool schemas, descriptions and error contracts belong to AI8. This Part owns three things only: how thinking is wired into the loop, what it costs, and how to stop it thinking forever.

Without it, the model commits to a tool sequence early and executes it, because the only place it did extensive reasoning was before it had seen any data. With it, each result gets evaluated against the goal before the next call is chosen. On a 60-trajectory benchmark over the reference workload's shipment-exception agent — six tools, real API, ground-truth resolutions — the shape of the difference is consistent.

The loop, in runnable code

The wiring is unremarkable and that is the point — the only thing reasoning changes about AI6's loop is that msg.content now contains thinking blocks you must append verbatim, and that you must stream. Everything else is the standard tool-use cycle.

import anthropic, json
client = anthropic.Anthropic()

TOOLS = [
  {"name": "get_booking",
   "description": "Booking record: incoterm, free time, service level, rate.",
   "input_schema": {"type": "object",
     "properties": {"ref": {"type": "string"}}, "required": ["ref"]}},
  {"name": "get_events", # …},

  "to the customer, and quantify it. Call tools to get facts; never "
  "assume a tariff or a free-time allowance. Stop as soon as you can "
  "justify a number — do not gather evidence you will not use.")

def run_agent(task, effort="low", model="claude-sonnet-5"):
    messages = [{"role": "user", "content": task}]
    trace = []
    while True:
        with client.messages.stream(
            model=model, max_tokens=32000,
            thinking={"type": "adaptive"},
            output_config={"effort": effort},
            system=SYSTEM, tools=TOOLS, messages=messages,
        ) as s:
            for _ in s: pass
            msg = s.get_final_message()

        if msg.stop_reason == "refusal":
            return {"status": "refused", "trace": trace}

        # ← the one rule that matters: the WHOLE content list, unedited.
        #   Thinking blocks carry signatures; dropping one 400s the next call.
        messages.append({"role": "assistant", "content": msg.content})
        trace.append({"turn": len(trace) + 1,
                      "think_words": sum(len(b.thinking.split())
                                         for b in msg.content
                                         if b.type == "thinking"),
                      "out_tok": msg.usage.output_tokens})

        calls = [b for b in msg.content if b.type == "tool_use"]
        if not calls:
            answer = "".join(b.text for b in msg.content if b.type == "text")
            return {"status": "done", "answer": answer, "trace": trace}

        results = []
        for c in calls:
            try:
                out = TOOL_IMPL[c.name](**c.input)
                results.append({"type": "tool_result", "tool_use_id": c.id,
                                "content": json.dumps(out)})
            except Exception as e:                  # errors are DATA, not crashes
                results.append({"type": "tool_result", "tool_use_id": c.id,
                                "is_error": True, "content": str(e)})
        messages.append({"role": "user", "content": results})

[61, 44, 38, 52] is the signature of a healthy interleaved trajectory: a substantial opening think, shorter ones as facts arrive, and a slightly longer one at the end where the model assembles the answer. A trace reading [210, 8, 6, 5, 4, 4, 4, 4] is an agent that planned once and is now grinding — usually because the system prompt handed it a procedure, exactly as in Part III. Log think_words per turn from day one; it is the cheapest agent health metric there is and it costs one len(split()). Wire it into your traces alongside the spans from PR1.

The 400 that will find you: a well-meaning refactor adds content=[b for b in msg.content if b.type in ("text","tool_use")] to "clean up the history". It passes every test where the loop ends in one turn, and fails on every multi-turn trajectory in production with "messages.1.content.0: thinking block signature is invalid". Put a test in CI that runs a two-tool trajectory end to end — it is the only test that catches this class of bug.

The task budget and the wrap-up guard

A reasoning agent's failure mode is not crashing; it is not landing. It gathers a ninth piece of evidence, reconsiders, gathers a tenth, and produces a beautifully reasoned nothing while your latency budget and your spend cap both burn. Two mechanisms fix this and you need both: a hard budget that terminates the loop, and a soft guard that tells the model to land the plane before the hard budget fires. The soft one is what gets you an answer instead of a truncation.

budget.py — hard ceiling plus the wrap-up nudge at 70% 
import time, dataclasses

@dataclasses.dataclass
class TaskBudget:
    max_turns: int = 12
    max_usd: float = 0.60
    max_seconds: float = 90.0
    warn_at: float = 0.70          # the wrap-up fires here

    def __post_init__(self):
        self.turns, self.usd, self.t0 = 0, 0.0, time.perf_counter()
        self.warned = False

    def consumed(self):        # fraction of the TIGHTEST dimension
        return max(self.turns / self.max_turns,
                   self.usd / self.max_usd,
                   (time.perf_counter() - self.t0) / self.max_seconds)

    def charge(self, msg, model):
        pin, pout = PRICES[model]
        self.turns += 1
        self.usd += (msg.usage.input_tokens/1e6*pin
                     + msg.usage.output_tokens/1e6*pout)

    def exhausted(self):
        return self.consumed() >= 1.0

WRAP_UP = (
    "BUDGET NOTICE: you have used {pct:.0%} of the budget for this task. "
    "Make no further tool calls unless one is strictly required to avoid "
    "stating something false. Answer now with what you have, and state "
    "explicitly what you could not verify.")

# inside the loop, immediately after appending the tool results:
budget.charge(msg, model)
if budget.exhausted():
    return {"status": "budget_exhausted", "trace": trace}
if not budget.warned and budget.consumed() >= budget.warn_at:
    budget.warned = True
    messages[-1]["content"].append(          # onto the tool_result turn
        {"type": "text", "text": WRAP_UP.format(pct=budget.consumed())})

print(run_agent(hard_task)["status"], budget.turns, f"${budget.usd:.3f}")
done 7 $0.412

And note it does not say "stop"; it says answer now and say what you could not verify. That phrasing is worth more than the guard itself, because it converts a silent truncation into a caveated answer a human can act on.

When reasoning changes the tool choice

The most valuable thing interleaved thinking does is not picking a better tool — it is declining to call one. A non-thinking agent handed six tools tends to use them, because each tool description is an invitation. A thinking agent asks whether the call will change the answer, and on the shipment-exception benchmark it answers "no" often enough to cut 2.5 calls off the mean trajectory. Here are the three divergences that account for most of the gap.

Tool errors are where non-thinking agents are worst, because recovering means forming a hypothesis about why the call failed, and that is exactly the serial inference a thinking block affords. This is also why AI8's rule that error strings must be actionable pays double here: "no booking for NL-88413; try the consignment reference, format NLC-#####" gets recovered from; "404" does not.

Neither ingredient works alone: a thinking model with terse errors recovers 44% of the time, an actionable error with no thinking recovers 31%. You need both to reach 78%, which is the sort of interaction that makes single-variable A/B tests on agent design so misleading.

Effort belongs to the step, not to the agent

Teams set one effort level for the whole agent and then argue about it. That is the wrong granularity. A trajectory contains a hard decision or two and six mechanical ones, and paying high for the mechanical ones is most of the bill. Because effort is a per-request field, you can vary it turn by turn on the same conversation — the history carries over untouched.

tiered.py — cheap by default, expensive only when the loop stalls 
def effort_for_turn(turn, trace, budget):

    if turn == 1:

    return "low"

Part V · The economics

Thinking tokens are the line item nobody forecasts

Every pilot proposal contains a cost estimate, and on a reasoning workload almost all of them are wrong by the same factor and in the same direction. The estimate is built from the visible answer — "600 tokens out, so about four cents" — because the visible answer is the only thing anyone has looked at. The thinking block is five times larger, bills at the same rate, and appears in no log. This Part turns that into a number you can defend, then into a routing table, which is the actual deliverable of this module. PR2 owns cost engineering across a whole system; what belongs here is the one line item reasoning creates.

How thinking tokens bill

Three rules, and each one is a mistake someone makes in their first month. Thinking tokens bill as output tokens, at the full output rate — $75 per million on claude-opus-5. They are already inside usage.output_tokens, so if you were summing that field you were counting them; if you were computing cost from the length of the string you rendered, you were not. And you pay for thinking on requests that fail — a max_tokens truncation, a downstream timeout, a schema violation you retry — because the tokens were generated before anything went wrong.

cost.py — the only honest way to price a reasoning request 
PRICES = {  # USD per 1M tokens: (input, output, cache_read, cache_write)
    "claude-opus-5":    (15.00, 75.00, 1.50, 18.75),
    "claude-sonnet-5":  (3.00,  15.00, 0.30, 3.75),
    "claude-haiku-4-5": (1.00,  5.00,  0.10, 1.25)}

def price(usage, model):
    pin, pout, pread, pwrite = PRICES[model]
    fresh = usage.input_tokens                     # uncached input only
    read  = getattr(usage, "cache_read_input_tokens", 0) or 0
    write = getattr(usage, "cache_creation_input_tokens", 0) or 0
    return (fresh/1e6*pin + read/1e6*pread + write/1e6*pwrite
            + usage.output_tokens/1e6*pout)   # ← thinking is IN HERE

# The split you actually want for a forecast — there is no API field for it,
# so measure it once against a no-thinking control run of the same prompts.
def split_output(msg):
    think = sum(len(b.thinking) for b in msg.content if b.type == "thinking")
    ans   = sum(len(b.text)     for b in msg.content if b.type == "text")
    frac  = think / max(think + ans, 1)          # by characters ≈ by tokens
    return round(msg.usage.output_tokens * frac), msg.usage.output_tokens

Caching helps less than you expect, and here is why

Prompt caching is the reflex answer to any cost problem, and on a reasoning route it is a much weaker lever than on a normal one — for a structural reason worth stating plainly: caching applies to input tokens, and on a high-effort route input is 18% of the bill. The 68% that is thinking is output, generated fresh every time, and no cache will ever touch it.

It is still worth doing, and the mechanics have one reasoning-specific trap. The cache is a prefix match: everything before your breakpoint must be byte-identical. Put the breakpoint after the stable material — system prompt, tool definitions, the rate card — and never after a turn that contains a thinking block, because thinking text differs on every request and a breakpoint below it caches nothing at all while still charging you the write premium.

Part VI · Verification

Trust the answer, never the trace

The thinking block is the most seductive artifact in this whole API. It is fluent, it is specific, it names your fields and your business rules, and it appears to show exactly how the conclusion was reached. Product managers want to display it. Compliance teams want to archive it. Engineers debug against it. All three are mistakes, and the reason is the same: the trace is text the model generated, and like every other token it generated, it is a claim rather than a record. This Part says what to do instead, and the answer is not "trust nothing" — it is a specific, cheap set of checks that get you to 96% caught for one and a half percent of the route cost.

Why the trace is not evidence

There are three distinct problems and conflating them is how people talk themselves back into trusting it.

  • It is not causal. The thinking text is generated by the same process that generates the answer. It is strongly correlated with the computation, and that correlation is why it is useful for debugging — but "the model wrote that it checked the incoterm" is not the same as "the model checked the incoterm", and there is no mechanism that makes it so.
  • It is not complete. Some blocks come back as redacted_thinking, and even unredacted blocks are a summary-grade artifact, not a transcript of every internal step. An audit trail with unexplained gaps is not an audit trail.
  • It is not stable. Run the same request twice and you get different thinking with the same answer, or — worse for a compliance story — the same answer with reasoning that contradicts itself across runs. A record that changes when you re-derive it is not a record.

The practical consequence is a rule you can hand to a product team: the thinking block may be used to diagnose, never to justify. Read it when an answer is wrong and you want a hypothesis about why. Do not put it in front of a user, do not store it as the reason a claim was denied, and do not write a test that asserts on its contents.

✗ Uses of the trace that will bite you
  • Rendering it to end users as "how we decided".
  • Storing it as the audit record for a regulated decision.
  • Asserting on substrings of it in CI — it changes run to run.
  • Grading answer quality by judging the reasoning.
  • Showing it to a customer in a demo, which sets an expectation you cannot keep.
✓ Uses that are genuinely good
  • Reading five failing traces to find the missing constraint — Part III.
  • Length per turn as an agent health signal — Part IV.
  • Spotting that the model never mentions a rule you thought you gave it.
  • Confirming which of two ambiguous readings it took.
  • Deciding whether a route is thinking at all, or has flatlined at 40 tokens.

Which leaves the question of what to log. The answer is: enough to debug, not enough to be mistaken for a record.

Verify the answer against a checkable artifact

The strongest verification is always the cheapest: find something about the answer a computer can check without a model. Most tasks that justify reasoning in the first place have this property, because tasks with dependent steps usually have arithmetic or structural invariants at the end. Reconciliation totals must equal the sum of the lines. A roster must not double-book anybody. Generated SQL must parse and return a shaped result. Extracted dates must be dates.

checks.py — deterministic invariants, zero model calls, zero dollars 
from decimal import Decimal as D

def check_reconciliation(answer, invoice):
    """Return a list of violated invariants. Empty list = passed."""
    bad = []
    lines = sum((D(str(l["amount"])) for l in invoice["lines"]), D("0"))
    billed = D(str(answer["billed_total"]))
    if abs(billed - lines) > D("0.01"):
        bad.append(f"billed_total {billed} != sum(lines) {lines}")

    booked = D(str(answer["booked_total"]))
    if answer["variance"] is not None:
        if abs(D(str(answer["variance"])) - (billed - booked)) > D("0.01"):
            bad.append("variance is not billed - booked")
    elif not answer["missing"]:
        bad.append("variance is null but nothing listed as missing")

    if answer["disputes"] and answer["variance"] is not None:
        if abs(D(str(answer["variance"]))) < booked * D("0.01"):
            bad.append("disputes raised on a sub-1% variance (contract rule 4)")
    return bad

That duplication is correct: the prompt tells the model what you want, the check tells you whether you got it. A rule that exists only in a prompt is a rule nobody is measuring.

Self-consistency: the technique to measure before you adopt

Run the request three times, take the majority answer. It is the classic reasoning-verification trick, it genuinely works, and on a modern reasoning model it is usually the wrong purchase — because the money it costs buys more accuracy when spent on effort instead. That is not a general law; it is a measurement you must make per route, and the point of this section is the shape of the comparison, not the winner.

The cheap verifier, and what it actually catches

After the deterministic checks there is a residue: answers that are internally consistent and still wrong — the surcharge applied under the wrong incoterm, a dispute raised on a clause that does not apply. These need judgement, but not much judgement, and specifically not the same model that produced the answer. A small model given the answer, the source, and the rules, asked only "is this defensible", is a good buy at roughly one and a half percent of the request it is checking.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Forecasting from the answer you can see

The visible response is 600 tokens; the thinking block is 3,100 and bills at the same $75/M. A proposal built on the part you rendered comes in 3.2× under at effort=high and 12× under at max. Price from usage.output_tokens, never from len(text).

✗ Raising effort to fix a prompt problem

Effort is rung four of five, and rungs one to three are free or nearly so. The reconciliation route's prompt rewrite bought +0.17 accuracy while cutting cost 38%. Bumping the effort instead would have bought less, cost 2.8×, and made the underlying ambiguity permanent.

✗ Shipping your AI1-era prompt unchanged

The numbered procedure and eight worked examples that made a non-reasoning model good make a reasoning model worse: 0.88 → 0.81 on triage, and eleven identical failures out of forty on reconciliation because the model dutifully followed step 3. Lint for it; do not rely on remembering.

✗ Treating the thinking trace as a record

It is not causal, not complete (redactions happen), and not stable across runs. Rendering it to users or archiving it as the reason a claim was denied creates an obligation you cannot meet. Generate a rationale field instead and archive that.

✗ A task budget with no wrap-up guard

Capping turns without telling the model to land the plane cut the answer rate from 83% to 71% — you bought a cheaper product that fails more often. Warn at 70% of the tightest budget dimension and ask for an answer with explicit caveats.

✓ Sweep before you argue

Twenty tasks, five effort levels, two hours, and the model-selection debate is over — with a curve showing the knee rather than two people's intuitions. Re-run it the week a new model ships; the knee moves and nobody sends you a notification.

✓ Deterministic checks before LLM judges

Thirty lines of Decimal arithmetic catch 94% of the wrong answers on an arithmetic route, cost nothing, and never have a bad day. Write those first; the model verifier is for the residue, and it is worth 1.5% of the request it checks.

✓ Effort per step, not per agent

Pay medium for turn 1 and for the stall, low for everything else. Eleven high-effort turns out of 268 matched flat high quality (0.80 vs 0.81) at less than a quarter of the cost.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • 400 thinking.budget_tokens: Extra inputs are not permitted → you copied an older snippet. The field is removed; use output_config={"effort": …}.
  • 400 naming temperature, top_p or top_k → those are rejected on Opus 5 and Sonnet 5. Delete them; there is no replacement.
  • AttributeError: 'ThinkingBlock' object has no attribute 'text' → you are reading msg.content[0]. With thinking on, index 0 is always the thinking block. Filter by b.type == "text".
  • Everything returns stop_reason == "max_tokens" at xhigh and max → your max_tokens is too low. It is a ceiling over thinking plus answer; 32000 is the right default here.
  • Scores identical across all five efforts on every class → your grader is too loose (comparing strings that always match) or your tasks are all in the "pure cost" family. Add a rostering task with a real constraint and check it fails at low.
  • 429s partway through the sweep → serial requests at max are long but token-heavy; add time.sleep(2) between tasks, or drop to a 12-task suite for the first pass.

        wins += da > db
    return wins / n

🧯 Troubleshooting

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • 400 … thinking block signature is invalid outside step 9 → something is editing history. Common culprits: a history-trimming helper, a Pydantic round-trip that drops unknown fields, or a summariser. Append msg.content unchanged.

  • The agent loops forever calling the same tool → you have no budget wired in yet (step 7), and possibly no no_new_information stop rule. Add the hard cap before you debug anything else.
  • Interleaved and turn-1-only produce identical traces → check that the thinking key is genuinely absent on later turns in condition A. Printing think_words per turn tells you immediately; it must be 0 after turn 1.
  • The wrap-up notice causes a 400 → you appended it as a new user message between a tool_use and its result. It must go inside the existing tool-result turn's content list.
  • A redacted thinking block appears and your filter drops it → handle redacted_thinking by passing it through untouched, exactly like a normal thinking block.

  • The saving looks too good (>90%) → check you are not comparing max against low on a route that was never running at max. The baseline must come from the logs, not from the worst case you can imagine.
  • Stakeholders fixate on the model name rather than the effort → re-cut the table with one model and five effort rows. The 14× spread within a single model is usually the more persuasive artifact and it removes the brand argument entirely.
  • Someone asks for a per-request SLA on quality → you cannot give one, and saying so is correct. Offer a measured rate over a defined suite plus the verification layer's catch rate, and put both in section 6.
  • The memo is seven pages → cut sections 2 and 5 to appendices. Nobody has ever complained that a recommendation memo was too short.
