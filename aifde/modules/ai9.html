AI Evaluation & Regression

Golden datasets, quality metrics and regression tests for prompts, models and agent behavior — the machinery that turns "it feels better" into "recall went from 71% to 88%, zero regressions, ship it."

Why it matters for an FDE: Without evals you cannot prove value to the customer and cannot safely change prompts — the skill that separates professionals from amateurs.
Part I · The core argument

Evals are the moat

Every AI system you ship is a probabilistic machine wearing a deterministic UI. Traditional software either passes its tests or it doesn't; an LLM feature is 82% right on normal inputs, 60% on hard ones, and confidently wrong in ways nobody predicted (FN2). You cannot improve what you cannot measure — and with LLMs you can't even hold steady what you don't measure, because a one-word prompt edit, a model version bump, or a temperature change silently reshuffles behavior across your entire input space.

"It feels better" does not survive contact with reality. It doesn't survive the teammate who feels the opposite, it doesn't survive the customer QBR where a VP asks "what exactly did we get for this quarter?", and it doesn't survive week three, when the prompt change that felt better broke the two cases the champion demos to their boss. An eval turns each of those moments from an argument into a number: a golden dataset says what "good" means, a harness measures it, and a regression gate keeps it from silently getting worse.

Evals are the FDE's trust engine. A before/after chart — "extraction accuracy 71% → 88% over six weeks, measured on your own historical tickets" — is the single most persuasive artifact in a renewal conversation (FD4). Demos earn pilots; measured improvement earns contracts. Build the eval in week one and every later week produces evidence for free.

The cost of not having one, as a timeline

Abstract arguments for evals lose to concrete deadlines. Here is the shape of the incident they prevent, taken from an email-to-order extraction pipeline that had been live for two months with no harness. One prompt line changed on a Tuesday afternoon; nobody noticed until Friday.

The harness that would have caught it is roughly a day of work, and the same day of work catches the next forty changes as well. That asymmetry — one-time cost against unbounded recurring protection — is the entire argument, and it is the version of the argument to make to a customer who is asking why week one has no visible feature in it.

The silent-degradation trap. The incident above is the lucky version, because the failure was loud enough to show up in a queue depth. The common version is a change that shifts quality by six points on inputs nobody watches — a category that is 4% of traffic, a language that only one region uses — and nobody ever notices. You do not get an incident report. You get a renewal conversation where the champion is quietly less enthusiastic than last quarter and cannot say why.

What an eval is not

Four things get called "our evals" in customer meetings, and none of them is one. Knowing the difference is the first thing that makes you useful in the room, because the customer is usually holding one of these and believes the problem is solved.

Not thisWhy it feels like an evalWhy it isn't
A public benchmark scoreIt is a number, published, comparable across modelsIt measures a distribution that is not yours. A model six points better on a reasoning benchmark can be worse on your five-field extraction because your inputs are lowercase, truncated and full of order ids. Benchmarks pick a shortlist; they never make the ship decision.
A vibe checkReading outputs is real evidence — you genuinely learn thingsIt has no denominator, no memory and no defence against your own priors. You read the outputs of the variant you built, which you already want to win. Reading outputs is how you generate criteria, not how you decide.
A demoEveryone watched it work end to endA demo is a sample of size one drawn by the person with the strongest incentive to draw a good one. It proves the system can succeed. Your eval must answer how often it does.
A latency/cost dashboardIt is live, it has charts, ops already trusts itThose are operational metrics (PR1, PR2). p95 latency stays flat while the answers become wrong. Every guardrail metric in Part VI is green during a quality collapse.

The positive definition, then: an eval is a fixed set of inputs with declared expected outcomes, a grader that turns each output into a verdict without a human in the loop, and a report that is comparable across runs. Fixed, declared, automatic, comparable. Drop any one of the four and you have a habit rather than an instrument — the "fixed" one is the one teams drop first, by quietly editing the dataset whenever it embarrasses them.

The maturity ladder: spreadsheet to CI gate

Nobody goes from nothing to a gated harness in one step, and pretending otherwise is how eval projects stall at the planning stage. There are five rungs, each of which is genuinely useful on its own, and each of which takes hours rather than weeks. The FDE's job on a new engagement is to identify which rung the customer is on and build exactly the next one — proposing rung 4 to a team on rung 0 gets you a slide deck and no harness.

Part II · Golden datasets

The dataset is the spec

A golden dataset is a versioned collection of cases — input → expected outcome — that operationally defines what "good" means for your feature. It is the closest thing an LLM system has to a requirements document, and unlike a requirements document, it executes. Building it is not grunt work to delegate: writing the golden set is where you learn the problem.

Sourcing: real beats synthetic, every time

SourceQualityNotes
Historical data★★★★Real tickets, emails, documents with known outcomes. The customer already has years of labeled examples — ask for them in the kickoff, not month two.
Production traces★★★★Once live, mine real requests from your tracing (PR1) — especially the failed ones. The distribution is exactly right because it is the distribution.
Customer correction logs★★★★Anywhere a human edits or overrides AI output, you have input + wrong answer + right answer. This is gold: pre-labeled hard cases. Design the product to capture them (Part VI).
Expert-written★★★Domain experts writing cases from memory. Good for coverage of rare-but-important scenarios; skewed toward what experts think users do.
Synthetic (LLM-generated)★★Fine for bootstrapping volume and for adversarial variants — but LLMs generate suspiciously clean inputs. Always hand-edit toward realism: typos, mixed languages, rambling, missing fields.

The case schema — every field earns its keep

A golden set is a data structure before it is a document, and the fields you choose on day one decide what questions you can ask in month six. The temptation is a two-column sheet — input, expected — and it is always regretted, because the first time a score moves you want to know which kind of case moved and where it came from, and neither column exists. Seven fields, one JSONL line per case:

Validate the file, not your memory of the file. A twenty-line check run in CI catches the three failure modes that actually happen — a duplicate id after a merge, a tier typo that makes a case invisible to every threshold, and an expected object whose keys drifted from the schema:

validate_cases.py — runs before the harness, in the same CI job 
import json, sys, collections

TIERS   = {"normal", "hard", "adversarial", "regression"}
FIELDS  = {"name", "order_id", "category", "refund_requested", "amount"}
CATS    = {"damaged", "late", "wrong_item", "billing", "other"}

cases = [json.loads(l) for l in open("cases.jsonl") if l.strip()]
errs, ids = [], collections.Counter(c["id"] for c in cases)

errs += [f"duplicate id {i}" for i, n in ids.items() if n > 1]
for c in cases:
    if c["tier"] not in TIERS:        errs.append(f"{c['id']}: bad tier {c['tier']!r}")
    if set(c["expected"]) != FIELDS:  errs.append(f"{c['id']}: expected keys drifted")
    if c["expected"]["category"] not in CATS:
        errs.append(f"{c['id']}: category {c['expected']['category']!r} not in rubric")
    for f in ("stratum", "provenance"):
        if not c.get(f):           errs.append(f"{c['id']}: missing {f}")

print(f"{len(cases)} cases · {len(errs)} problems")
[print("  " + e) for e in errs]
sys.exit(1 if errs else 0)

Sizing honestly: what n can and cannot tell you

You don't need heavy statistics — you need one honest intuition: with n cases, each case is worth 100/n points. On a 20-case set, one flipped case moves your score by 5 points, so a "3-point improvement" is indistinguishable from noise. Use the flip rule: if the delta between two configurations is smaller than two cases flipping, treat it as a tie and look at which cases changed instead of the aggregate.

SizeGood forNot good for
20–30 casesCatching regressions ("the gate"), smoke-testing a change, week-one baselineDistinguishing improvements smaller than ~10 points
50–100 casesComparing prompts/models with real confidence; meaningful per-tier breakdownsSingle-digit deltas; rare-input coverage
100–300 casesMeasuring incremental improvements; QBR-grade trend charts; slicing by segmentAnything your labeling quality can't support — more bad labels is worse than fewer good ones
1000+Rarely needed offline — sample live traffic online instead (Part VI)Hand-labeling budgets; per-case inspection habits

Start at 20–30 on day one — it takes an afternoon and catches most regressions. Grow toward 100+ as the engagement matures. Never delay shipping the first eval to make it bigger.

Stratification: a dataset is a portfolio

A flat pile of average cases hides exactly the failures that hurt. Tag every case with a tier and report scores per tier — an aggregate that mixes them is how "97% accuracy" ships a system that fails every hard case:

  • Normal (~50%) — representative everyday inputs. Defines your headline number.
  • Hard (~25%) — long inputs, ambiguity, multiple entities, edge formats, mixed languages. Where models actually differ.
  • Adversarial (~15%) — injection attempts, out-of-scope requests, inputs designed to bait a confident wrong answer. Expected behavior is often refusal (PR4).
  • Regression (~10%, growing) — one case per production incident, forever. This tier only grows, and its threshold in CI is zero tolerance: these bugs already embarrassed you once.

Do not let the adversarial tier be the smallest thing you check. Thirteen adversarial cases with zero tolerance is a real gate; three is a formality that passes by luck. If the customer's risk review cares about injection, the injection stratum needs to be big enough that one flipped case is visibly a failure and not a rounding error — and building it out properly is AI16's job, not a side quest here.

Labeling discipline

  • Rubric before labels. Write down what counts as correct — including tie-breaks ("if the email names two people, the customer is the sender") — before labeling. Otherwise your labels encode whatever mood you were in, and your eval measures agreement with that mood.
  • Second-labeler spot checks. Have a teammate independently label 10 random cases. Below ~90% agreement, your rubric is ambiguous — fix the rubric, not the labels. If two humans can't agree, no grader can be right.
  • Label the outcome, not the phrasing. Expected values should be checkable facts (fields, decisions, citations) wherever possible — free-text "ideal answers" force you into fuzzy grading prematurely.

Versioning: a score without a dataset version is an anecdote

The failure this prevents is subtle and extremely common. You report 89% in March. In April someone adds eleven hard cases, quite correctly. In May you report 84% and spend a morning hunting a regression that does not exist — the system got better and the exam got harder. Without a version stamped into every report, the dataset is a moving ruler and nothing you measured with it is comparable.

Two mechanisms, both cheap. A human-readable manifest that travels with the file, and a content hash the runner computes so nobody can claim a version they did not run:

dataset_version.py — imported by the runner, printed in every report header 
import hashlib, json, pathlib

def dataset_fingerprint(path="cases.jsonl"):
    # hash the CONTENT, sorted by id, so line order and whitespace don't matter
    cases = [json.loads(l) for l in pathlib.Path(path).read_text().splitlines() if l.strip()]
    canon = json.dumps(sorted(cases, key=lambda c: c["id"]), sort_keys=True)
    return {"n": len(cases),
            "sha": hashlib.sha256(canon.encode()).hexdigest()[:12],
            "tiers": {t: sum(c["tier"] == t for c in cases)
                      for t in ("normal", "hard", "adversarial", "regression")}}

print(dataset_fingerprint())

That single guard rail eliminates the entire category of "did the score move or did the test move?" arguments.

Maintenance: a dataset is a living artifact

  • Version it with the code. cases.jsonl lives in the repo; every report names the dataset version it ran against (v7, n=84). A score without a dataset version is not reproducible, and irreproducible numbers die in the first skeptical meeting.
  • Leakage hygiene. The moment you iterate a prompt against specific cases, those cases stop measuring generalization — you've memorized the test. Keep a small holdout slice you never look at while tuning, and refresh it periodically from fresh production traces. Never tune on the holdout, ever.
  • Refresh as the product drifts. New document types, new user segments, new languages — each product change should trigger the question "does the golden set contain this yet?" A dataset frozen in January quietly measures a product that no longer exists by June.
The most common eval failure isn't a missing eval — it's a stale one. A green dashboard on last quarter's cases feels like safety while this quarter's traffic fails unmeasured. Schedule a monthly dataset review: mine 10 recent traces, add what's missing, retire what's obsolete, bump the version.
Part III · Grader taxonomy

Choosing the cheapest trustworthy judge

A grader maps (output, expected) → pass/fail + reason. The craft is picking the cheapest grader you can trust for each criterion — and the order of preference is strict:

Grader classExamplesCost / determinismUse when
1 · ProgrammaticExact match, contains, regex, JSON-schema validity, numeric toleranceFree · fully deterministicAlways prefer. Anything with a checkable structure: extracted fields, classifications, formats, citations present
2 · Execution-basedDoes the generated SQL run and return the expected rows; does the code pass its tests; does the API call validateCheap compute · deterministicOutput is executable — run it, don't read it
3 · LLM-as-judgeFaithfulness, tone, completeness, helpfulnessPer-call cost · noisy, biasedGenuinely subjective qualities — and only after calibration (below)
4 · Human reviewExpert reads a sampleExpensive · slow · gold standardThe periodic anchor everything else is calibrated against — never fully retired

Most tasks decompose further than teams assume. "Grade this summary" feels like one fuzzy judgment, but splits into: length under limit (programmatic), all key numbers present (programmatic, from labels), no meta-phrases (regex), faithful to source (judge). Three of four criteria just became free and deterministic — and the judge now answers one narrow question instead of four vague ones. 

# graders.py — pure functions, no API calls, trivially testable
import json, re

def g_json_schema(raw, required=("name","order_id","category","refund_requested","amount")):
    try: d = json.loads(raw)
    except Exception: return False, "not valid JSON"
    missing = set(required) - d.keys()
    return (not missing, f"missing keys: {missing}" if missing else "ok")

def g_exact(d, exp, field):
    ok = d.get(field) == exp[field]
    return ok, "ok" if ok else f"{field}: got {d.get(field)!r}, want {exp[field]!r}"

def g_numeric(d, exp, field="amount", tol=0.01):
    if exp[field] is None: return d.get(field) is None, "expected null"
    try: return abs(float(d[field]) - float(exp[field])) <= tol, "ok"
    except (TypeError, ValueError, KeyError): return False, f"non-numeric: {d.get(field)!r}"

def g_regex(d, field="order_id", pattern=r"^[A-Z]-\d{5}$"):
    ok = bool(re.match(pattern, str(d.get(field, ""))))
    return ok, "ok" if ok else f"malformed {field}: {d.get(field)!r}"

Rung 3 deserves a specific note because it is the rung most teams forget they have. If the output is executable, execute it. Generated SQL has an exact answer key — run the candidate query and the reference query against a pinned fixture database and compare result sets. That is execution accuracy, it costs a few hundred milliseconds, it is completely immune to the twelve different ways of writing the same correct join, and it is strictly better than any judge reading SQL text. AI14 owns the details — result-set canonicalization, ordering, floating point, and the read-only sandbox — but the grader shape belongs in your harness:

graders_exec.py — execution accuracy as an ordinary grader function 
import duckdb

def g_execution(candidate_sql, reference_sql, fixture_db="fixture.duckdb", ordered=False):
    # read-only connection: a generated DELETE can never touch the fixture
    con = duckdb.connect(fixture_db, read_only=True)
    try:
        got  = con.execute(candidate_sql).fetchall()
    except Exception as e:
        return False, f"query failed: {type(e).__name__}: {e}"
    want = con.execute(reference_sql).fetchall()
    if not ordered:                      # ORDER BY only matters if the question asked for it
        got, want = sorted(map(str, got)), sorted(map(str, want))
    ok = got == want
    return ok, "ok" if ok else f"{len(got)} rows, expected {len(want)}"

print(g_execution("SELECT region, SUM(amount) FROM orders GROUP BY 1",
                   "SELECT region, SUM(amount) FROM orders GROUP BY region"))

Rubric design: one bad rubric, one good one

Before any judge code, the rubric. Almost every "the judge is unreliable" complaint is really "the rubric is unanswerable", and you can tell the two apart in thirty seconds: hand the rubric to a colleague with no context and five outputs, and see whether they produce the same verdicts you did. Here are two rubrics for the same task, written by the same person, twenty minutes apart.

✗ The rubric that produces noise
Rate the summary from 1 to 10 on
overall quality. Consider accuracy,
completeness, clarity and tone.
Return the number only.
  • Four dimensions collapsed into one number — a 6 could be perfect-but-terse or fluent-and-wrong, and the score cannot tell you which.
  • No definition of any dimension. "Accuracy" against what, judged how?
  • No tie-breaks, so the judge invents one per call. This is where the 2–5% verdict flip rate comes from.
  • Nothing to fix when the score drops. You learn that quality fell, not what to change.
✓ The rubric a junior colleague can apply
faithful: every claim appears in the
  source, stated or directly implied.
  Background knowledge does NOT count
  as support. Tie-break: a paraphrase
  that changes a number is unfaithful.
complete: every figure and date in the
  source's "Decisions" section appears.
concise: 120 words or fewer.
no_meta: no sentence about the document
  itself ("This report discusses...").
Answer each strictly true or false.
  • Four independent binary questions; the score is the fraction passed and the failures name themselves.
  • Each criterion says what evidence decides it, so two people reach the same verdict.
  • concise is now visibly programmatic — replace it with a word counter and stop paying for it.
  • Tie-breaks are written down. Every one of them came from an actual disagreement while labelling.

The good rubric took twenty minutes longer to write and made the judge's agreement with human labels move from 68% to 91% on the same twenty cases, with no change to the model or the prompt scaffolding. That is the highest-leverage twenty minutes in this module.

LLM-as-judge, done properly

A judge is a model grading a model — powerful, and dangerous precisely because it always returns something. An uncalibrated judge is a random number generator with authority. Four rules:

1 · Binary criteria beat 1–10 scales. Nobody — human or model — can define the difference between a 6 and a 7, so scalar scores drift with phrasing, ordering and moon phase. Decompose quality into yes/no questions a junior colleague could answer consistently. Score = fraction of criteria passed, which also tells you what to fix, not just how much.

2 · Reasoning before verdict. Force the judge to quote evidence first, then emit a machine-parseable verdict on the last line. Verdict-first judges rationalize; evidence-first judges check. 

# judge prompt skeleton — binary criteria, evidence first, JSON last
You are grading a summary against its source document.

Criteria — answer each strictly true or false:
1. faithful  — every claim in the summary is supported by the source
2. complete  — every number and date from the source's key points appears
3. concise   — the summary is 120 words or fewer
4. no_meta   — no phrases like "This document discusses..."

First, for each criterion, quote the exact evidence from source and
summary that decides it. Then output exactly one final line of JSON:
{"faithful": true|false, "complete": true|false,
 "concise": true|false, "no_meta": true|false}

A judge is a detector for a rare, expensive event. Grading it on overall agreement is like grading a smoke alarm on how often it is silent. Report failure recall alongside agreement, always, and treat any judge below about 80% failure recall as advisory-only regardless of how good its headline number looks:

calibrate.py — the three numbers that decide whether you can trust it 
def calibration_report(human, judge_v, criterion):
    ids  = sorted(human)
    h    = [human[i][criterion] for i in ids]
    j    = [judge_v[i][criterion] for i in ids]
    n    = len(ids)

    agree    = sum(a == b for a, b in zip(h, j)) / n
    majority = max(h.count(True), h.count(False)) / n     # the trivial baseline
    p_yes_h, p_yes_j = h.count(True) / n, j.count(True) / n
    chance   = p_yes_h * p_yes_j + (1 - p_yes_h) * (1 - p_yes_j)
    kappa    = (agree - chance) / (1 - chance) if chance < 1 else float("nan")

    real_fails   = [k for k, a in zip(ids, h) if a is False]
    caught       = [k for k in real_fails if judge_v[k][criterion] is False]
    recall       = len(caught) / len(real_fails) if real_fails else float("nan")

    return {"agreement": agree, "baseline": majority,
            "kappa": round(kappa, 2), "failure_recall": recall,
            "missed": [k for k in real_fails if k not in caught]}

The base-rate trap ends careers quietly. A team ships a judge at "92% agreement", the CI gate goes green for four months, and the whole time it is catching one failure in three. Nothing alerts, because the instrument that would have alerted is the broken one. Two defences: build the calibration sample to be failure-enriched — deliberately include roughly as many bad outputs as good ones, by degrading known-good ones if you must — and re-anchor on fresh human labels every dataset version bump.

Re-run the same twenty cases after enriching the sample to 10 good and 10 deliberately broken summaries and the numbers become interpretable: agreement 90%, trivial baseline 50%, kappa 0.80, failure recall 9/10. Same judge, same rubric — the measurement got honest, not the model. Now the judge code itself, with the two API details that bite in practice: verdicts come back as structured output rather than a parsed last line, and a refusal is handled before anything reads content.

judge.py — structured verdicts, refusal-safe, no sampling params 
import anthropic, json
llm = anthropic.Anthropic()

VERDICT_SCHEMA = {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {
        "evidence": {"type": "string", "description": "quotes that decide each criterion"},
        "faithful": {"type": "boolean"}, "complete": {"type": "boolean"},

Evidence first is a schema decision, not a prompt decision. Putting evidence first in the schema's required list means the model emits its quotes before it emits any boolean — the same "reason then verdict" effect, but enforced by the format instead of hoped for in the instructions. On a reasoning model the thinking trace is not that evidence and must never be graded as if it were — see AI13.

Practical split: absolute + binary criteria in the CI gate (stable bar, one call per case), pairwise for decisions ("do we switch to the new model?"). And human review never fully retires — an expert reading 10 sampled outputs monthly is the anchor that keeps every automated layer honest.

One arithmetic note on pairwise, because it is where teams over-claim. Every pair runs twice with the order swapped, so 20 comparisons cost 40 calls, and the ones where the winner changes with the order are ties — they carry no information. In a typical run, 20 pairs produce 13 consistent verdicts and 7 order-flips. Your evidence is 13 comparisons, not 20, and if 8 of those 13 favour the new prompt you are looking at 8–5, which is a coin flip dressed as a decision. Run 40 pairs or accept that you do not yet know.

When a cheap deterministic grader beats a judge

The judge is the most fun part of this module and the part you should use least. Almost every criterion that feels subjective has a deterministic core hiding inside it, and pulling that core out is strictly better on all four axes that matter: it is free, instant, identical on rerun, and it produces a failure message that names the fix. Go through the criteria one at a time and be ruthless:

graders_text.py — the four "subjective" checks that turned out not to be 
import re

BANNED = ("as an ai", "i'm sorry to hear", "this document discusses",
          "in conclusion", "it is important to note")

def g_concise(text, limit=120):
    n = len(text.split())
    return n <= limit, "ok" if n <= limit else f"{n} words > {limit}"

def g_no_meta(text):
    hits = [p for p in BANNED if p in text.lower()]
    return not hits, "ok" if not hits else f"banned phrase: {hits}"

def g_numbers_grounded(text, source):
    # every number in the output must occur in the input, modulo thousands separators
    norm = lambda s: s.replace(",", "").replace(" ", "")
    src  = norm(source)
    bad  = [t for t in re.findall(r"\d[\d,.]*", text) if norm(t) not in src]
    return not bad, "ok" if not bad else f"invented numbers: {bad}"

def g_citations_resolve(text, retrieved_ids):
    cited = set(re.findall(r"\[(\w+#\d+)\]", text))
    ghost = cited - set(retrieved_ids)
    return not ghost, "ok" if not ghost else f"citations to chunks never retrieved: {ghost}"

print(g_numbers_grounded("Revenue rose 12% to $4.1M in Q3.",
                        "Revenue rose 1.2% to $4.1M in the third quarter."))
(False, "invented numbers: ['12']")

On the 84-case set, this moves four of six criteria off the judge. The run goes from 84 judge calls at $0.29 and 6 minutes to 28 judge calls at $0.10 and 2 minutes, and — the part that matters more than the money — the four deterministic criteria stop contributing any run-to-run variance at all, so a two-point move in the score now means something changed in the system rather than in the weather. g_numbers_grounded alone catches the single most damaging faithfulness failure, the silently altered figure, in nine characters of regex.

Score-4 move: when a customer asks for "an AI that grades our AI", decline the framing and build the decomposition instead. Deliver the six-criterion breakdown with four criteria in graders_text.py and two on a calibrated judge, and show them the failure-recall number for the two that remain. It is less impressive in a slide and it is the version that still works in month nine — and it is a far easier conversation than explaining, later, why the grading AI needed its own grading AI.

A minimal pytest harness

You don't need a platform to start — pytest gives you parametrization, IDs, filtering and CI integration for free. The one non-obvious feature worth building on day one is output caching: keyed on prompt + input, so re-running an unchanged configuration costs zero API calls and grader tweaks re-run instantly.

eval_extraction.py — run: pytest eval_extraction.py -v 
import anthropic, hashlib, json, pathlib, pytest
from graders import g_json_schema, g_exact, g_numeric, g_regex

llm = anthropic.Anthropic()
CACHE = pathlib.Path(".eval_cache"); CACHE.mkdir(exist_ok=True)
CASES = [json.loads(l) for l in open("cases.jsonl")]
PROMPT = pathlib.Path("prompts/extract_v3.txt").read_text()

def model_output(case):
    # cache key = prompt + input + model. Change any one and it re-runs.
    key = hashlib.sha256((PROMPT + case["input"] + MODEL).encode()).hexdigest()[:16]
    f = CACHE / f"{key}.json"
    if f.exists(): return f.read_text()            # cached → re-runs are free
    msg = llm.messages.create(model=MODEL, max_tokens=300,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT.format(email=case["input"])}])
    if msg.stop_reason == "refusal":
        return json.dumps({"_refusal": True})    # graded as fail, not as crash
    out = msg.content[0].text
    f.write_text(out)
    return out

MODEL = "claude-sonnet-5"

@pytest.mark.parametrize("case", CASES, ids=[c["id"] for c in CASES])
def test_case(case):
    raw = model_output(case)
    ok, why = g_json_schema(raw)
    assert ok, why
    d, exp = json.loads(raw), case["expected"]
    for field in ("order_id", "category", "refund_requested"):
        ok, why = g_exact(d, exp, field); assert ok, why
    ok, why = g_numeric(d, exp);  assert ok, why
    ok, why = g_regex(d);         assert ok, why

pytest -v now prints a per-case pass/fail table with case IDs, and pytest -k hard runs one tier. That's a working harness in ~40 lines. Note what is not in that call.

"Set temperature to 0 for reproducible evals" is now wrong advice — delete it from your notes. On Opus 5 and Sonnet 5 the temperature, top_p and top_k parameters are rejected: sending them returns a 400, so an old harness copied from a 2024 blog post fails on the first call. It was never doing what people believed anyway — greedy decoding does not make a distributed inference stack deterministic, because batching, kernel selection and hardware mix all move the logits slightly. The old advice bought you an illusion of determinism and charged you a real debugging bill when the illusion broke. Reproducibility in an eval comes from caching outputs and from a written flake policy, not from a sampling parameter. Two more removals from the same era: budget_tokens is gone (use thinking={"type": "adaptive"} with output_config={"effort": "low"}), and assistant-turn prefill returns 400 — the JSON-forcing trick is now output_config={"format": {...}}.

The result store: one row per (run, case, grader)

A harness that prints to stdout answers "is it good now?" and nothing else. The question you will actually be asked — by a teammate on Tuesday and by the customer at the QBR — is "what changed, and when?", and that needs history. The mistake is storing summaries: a JSON file of per-tier percentages per run seems sufficient and is useless the first time you want to know which case broke. Flakiness: how many runs you actually need

Before you can call a two-point move a regression you have to know how big a two-point move is when nothing changed. Measure it once, on day one, and never guess again: run the identical configuration five times against the same 84 cases with the cache disabled, and look at the spread.

Now the decision rule. Because both configurations run the same cases, this is a paired comparison, and the unpaired confidence interval on 84 samples (±6.7 points at 95%) is the wrong tool — it would tell you nothing is ever significant. What carries the information is the discordant pairs: cases whose verdict differs between the two configs. A serviceable rule that needs no statistics library: 

flips_worse = cases that passed in A and fail in B
flips_better = cases that failed in A and pass in B
net = flips_better - flips_worse
total = flips_better + flips_worse

signal if  |net| > 2 * sqrt(total)      # else: noise, look at cases not scores

#         3 < 5.3  ->  NOT a demonstrated improvement, despite "+3 points"
# worked: 9 better, 1 worse -> net 8, total 10, 2*sqrt(10) = 6.3
#         8 > 6.3  ->  real. And you still read all 10 cases.

  • Thresholds per tier, not one aggregate. Typical policy: overall may not drop more than 2 points (noise allowance), regression tier must stay at 100%, hard tier drop blocks, adversarial drop blocks. An aggregate-only gate lets a big win on easy cases smuggle in a safety regression.
  • Blocking vs advisory. Deterministic graders + regression tier: blocking. Judge-scored criteria: advisory at first (comment on the PR), promoted to blocking once the judge has weeks of calibration history. A gate that cries wolf gets deleted.
  • Flake policy: retry once, then believe it. LLM outputs vary even at temperature 0 (serving nondeterminism). Re-run only the failed cases once; a pass on retry is a flake (log it — flake rate is itself a health metric), a second failure is real. Never retry more than once: infinite retries turn the gate into a slot machine.
  • Cost budgeting. Cache generator outputs keyed on prompt+input so unchanged cases are free; use a haiku-class model for judging in CI (calibrate that judge, it's usually enough); run the full set on prompt changes but only a stratified sample (all regression + random 30%) on unrelated commits. A typical gated run costs cents, not dollars (PR2).

Parallelism, rate limits and the 40-minute run nobody waits for

Serially, 84 cases at 2.8 s each is four minutes; add a judge pass and it is ten. Grow to 300 cases and a multi-criterion judge and you are at forty minutes, which is past the threshold where a human stops running it before pushing. Wall-clock is a correctness feature — a harness nobody runs has a quality of exactly zero — so concurrency is not an optimization you get to defer.

The naive version melts against rate limits and produces a wall of 429s that looks like a broken harness. The version that works is bounded concurrency plus honouring retry-after, and it is about twenty lines:

runner_concurrent.py — bounded fan-out, retry-after, deterministic ordering 
import concurrent.futures as cf, time, random, anthropic

llm = anthropic.Anthropic()
CONCURRENCY = 8          # start here; raise until you see 429s, then halve

def call_with_backoff(case, attempts=5):
    for i in range(attempts):
        try:
            return model_output(case)
        except anthropic.RateLimitError as e:
            # the server tells you how long to wait — obey it, don't guess
            wait = float(e.response.headers.get("retry-after", 2 ** i))
            time.sleep(wait + random.uniform(0, 0.4))     # jitter: avoid lockstep retries
        except anthropic.APIStatusError as e:
            if e.status_code < 500: raise              # 400s are your bug, not transient
            time.sleep(2 ** i)
    raise RuntimeError(f"{case['id']}: exhausted retries")

with cf.ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=CONCURRENCY) as ex:
    futures = {ex.submit(call_with_backoff, c): c for c in CASES}
    outputs = {futures[f]["id"]: f.result() for f in cf.as_completed(futures)}

rows = [grade(c, outputs[c["id"]]) for c in sorted(CASES, key=lambda c: c["id"])]

Three details in there are load-bearing. Grading happens after generation, in sorted order, so two runs of the same dataset produce byte-comparable reports and your diff shows real changes rather than thread scheduling. 4xx errors are re-raised, not retried — a 400 from a stale temperature parameter retried five times is thirty seconds of silence followed by a confusing error. And retry-after is obeyed rather than guessed; exponential backoff without the header is how a harness takes eleven minutes to do four minutes of work.

Part V · Harder targets

Evaluating RAG, agents, conversations, safety

Single-turn prompts are the easy case. The same principles — decompose, prefer deterministic graders, calibrate judges — extend to messier systems by splitting the pipeline and evaluating each stage where it can fail:

RAG: split retrieval from generation

Measure recall@k/MRR on question→known-source pairs separately from faithfulness and citation accuracy given correct chunks. The split turns "the bot is wrong sometimes" into a five-minute stage diagnosis — the full treatment is in AI4 Part V.

Agents: unit-test your tools, eval your loop

Tools are ordinary code — cover them with ordinary tests. The loop needs trajectory evals: did it pick the right tool (tool-choice correctness), in a reasonable number of steps (efficiency budget: flag >2× the reference step count), and does the end state assert true — the ticket exists, the row was updated? End-state assertions beat transcript-reading judges (AI6).

Multi-turn: scripted user simulators

A second LLM plays the user from a persona script ("you want a refund but only reveal the order number when asked; get frustrated after turn 3"). Grade the conversation's end state plus per-turn criteria. Simulators drift, so keep scripts tight and spot-check transcripts by hand.

Safety: red-team suites as regression tests

Every injection, jailbreak and data-exfiltration attempt discovered in testing or production becomes a permanent adversarial-tier case with zero-tolerance thresholds. Safety findings that don't become regression tests get rediscovered by customers (PR4).

RAG: you own the harness, AI4 owns the metrics

The boundary matters because both modules will be open on your screen. What recall@k, MRR and nDCG mean, how to pick k, how hybrid retrieval and reranking change them — that is AI4's territory and it is treated properly there. What AI9 owns is the part that makes those metrics run in your gate: a RAG case needs a different expected shape, a different set of graders, and a per-stage report, and if you bolt retrieval scoring onto a harness designed for single-turn extraction you get one number that tells you the pipeline is worse without telling you which half.

The structural move is to store two expected artifacts per case — the chunk ids that should have been retrieved, and the facts the answer must contain — and to grade them separately so every failure lands in exactly one bucket:

def g_recall_at_k(retrieved_ids, must_retrieve, k=5):
    hit = set(retrieved_ids[:k]) & set(must_retrieve)
    ok  = len(hit) == len(must_retrieve)
    return ok, "ok" if ok else f"missed {set(must_retrieve) - hit} in top-{k}"

def g_answer_given_gold(answer, must_state, must_not_state):
    # run this against the GOLD chunks, not the retrieved ones — it isolates
    # generation quality from retrieval quality
    a = answer.lower()
    miss  = [s for s in must_state     if s.lower() not in a]
    wrong = [s for s in must_not_state if s.lower() in a]
    return (not miss and not wrong,
            "ok" if not (miss or wrong) else f"missing {miss} · contradicts {wrong}")

Agent trajectories: same input, different valid path

Agents break the core assumption of everything so far, which is that a case has an expected output. Ask an agent to "refund order B-20977 and tell the customer" and there are at least four correct trajectories: look up the order then refund then email; refund then look up then email; search by customer name first; ask a clarifying question first because the order has two line items. Grading the transcript against a reference transcript marks three of the four wrong. The loop design itself belongs to AI6; what you own is the answer to "what do I assert, if not the path?"

Three assertions, in strict priority order. End state is the one that must always exist: after the run, does the world look right? That is an ordinary integration-test assertion against a fixture, it is completely path-agnostic, and it is the reason agent evals are less exotic than they sound. Forbidden actions come second — an agent that got the right answer by refunding the wrong order failed. Efficiency comes last and is a budget, not a target.

graders_agent.py — assert the world, not the transcript 
def g_end_state(db, expected):
    # the only assertion that survives a legitimately different path
    order = db.get_order(expected["order_id"])
    checks = {
        "refunded":   order.status == expected["status"],
        "amount":     abs(order.refund_amount - expected["amount"]) < 0.01,
        "email_sent": db.emails_to(order.customer_id, since=RUN_START) == 1}
    bad = [k for k, v in checks.items() if not v]
    return not bad, "ok" if not bad else f"end state wrong: {bad}"

def g_no_forbidden(trace, forbidden=("delete_order", "issue_credit", "email_all")):
    # safety is about what it did, never about what it said it would do
    called = [s["tool"] for s in trace if s["type"] == "tool_use"]
    hits = [t for t in called if t in forbidden]
    return not hits, "ok" if not hits else f"forbidden tool: {hits}"

def g_efficiency(trace, reference_steps, factor=2.0):
    # a budget, not a target: 2x the reference path is fine, 5x is a loop
    n = sum(1 for s in trace if s["type"] == "tool_use")
    ok = n <= reference_steps * factor
    return ok, "ok" if ok else f"{n} tool calls vs reference {reference_steps}"

Do not grade the reasoning trace. It is the single most tempting mistake in agent and reasoning-model evaluation, because the trace is right there and it reads like an explanation. It is not a commitment: a model can produce an immaculate plan and then do something else, or reach a correct end state by a route its own narration misdescribes. Score the tool calls and the end state — the observable artifacts. AI13 makes the same point for extended thinking, and makes it more strongly: the thinking block is not the answer, and an eval that grades it is measuring the model's prose about its work rather than its work.

The fixture is the real engineering cost here, and it is worth stating plainly before a customer asks why agent evals took two days instead of two hours. Each case needs a database reset to a known state, a set of tool stubs that behave deterministically (including the failure cases — a payment API that times out on case a07, every time), and a way to snapshot the end state. Build it once as a pytest fixture with per-case teardown, and every future agent case costs ten minutes instead of a day.

Adversarial: the tier you should not build alone

Everything above assumed a user who wants the system to work. The adversarial tier assumes the opposite, and it is different in kind rather than degree: the input distribution is chosen by someone trying to find the gap, so it does not converge, and last month's coverage means nothing about this month's technique. This is where AI9 stops and hands off.

AI9 owns (this module)
  • The tier and its threshold. Adversarial cases live in the same cases.jsonl, run through the same runner, and get zero-tolerance gating.
  • The grader shape. Refusal is an observable state: structured fields null, no tool called, no system-prompt text echoed. Never a judge asking "was this safe?"
  • Regression permanence. Every finding becomes a case that never retires, so the same jailbreak cannot land twice.
  • The report line. "adversarial 13/13" next to the quality numbers, in the same artifact, every run.
AI16 owns (go there for depth)
  • The attack taxonomy — what the classes of attack actually are, and which apply to your architecture.
  • Generating an attack corpus at a scale that means something, rather than the five injections you thought of on a Tuesday.
  • Severity rubrics — because adversarial findings are not equally bad, and a flat pass/fail hides that.
  • Cadence and disclosure — re-test schedules, agentic attack surfaces, and what you tell the customer when something lands.

Read that as a genuine instruction rather than a cross-reference. Thirteen adversarial cases you invented yourself are a smoke test, not a red-team, and presenting them as safety coverage is the kind of overclaim that ends badly in a security review. AI16 reuses this exact harness — same case file, same runner, same gate — so the work is additive rather than a second system; what it adds is the discipline of generating attacks systematically instead of anecdotally. And when a finding needs fixing, the fix is a control, which is PR4's territory: AI9 measures, AI16 attacks, PR4 defends, and none of the three re-teaches the others.

The universal trick is decomposition. Whenever a system feels "too complex to eval", you're looking at a pipeline, not a monolith. Find the stage boundaries, define an expected artifact at each boundary, and grade the artifacts. Complexity is a reason to split the eval, never to skip it.
Part VI · Online

Production is the eval that never stops

Everything so far measures a frozen set of inputs you chose. Production sends inputs nobody chose, from users who change what they ask as they learn what the system can do, through upstream systems that alter their formats without telling you. Your offline suite can be at 89% and rising while the live experience degrades, and there is no contradiction in that: the offline number is a statement about your dataset, and the dataset is a hypothesis about reality that expires. Part VI is the machinery that keeps the hypothesis honest.

Sampling production traffic for offline scoring

The cheapest online eval is not a new system. It is your existing graders, pointed at a weekly sample of real traffic. The only thing you have to get right is the sampling, and uniform random sampling is the wrong default — at 20,000 requests a week, a 50-request uniform sample contains roughly zero examples of the 0.4% of traffic that is non-English and roughly zero refusals, which are precisely the two things you wanted to look at. Stratify the sample the same way you stratified the dataset, and over-sample the tails deliberately with a weight you record so you can un-weight when reporting.

A/B testing after the gate, and closing the loop

  • Offline gate first, online test second. A/B is for changes that pass the offline gate but whose real-world impact is uncertain (tone changes, model swaps with mixed offline results). Randomize per user, not per request; pick the metric before starting (acceptance rate, escalation rate); mind that AI metrics are noisy — small effects need more traffic than intuition suggests.
  • Size the test before you start it, or don't start it. Acceptance rate at 62%, and you want to detect a 3-point lift: that needs roughly 4,200 users per arm at conventional power. At 300 daily active users the test runs for four weeks, during which you cannot ship anything else to that surface. Very often the honest conclusion is this change is too small to A/B — ship it on the offline evidence and spend the traffic budget on a change worth measuring.
  • Close the loop. Every 👎, every correction, every drift-sample failure is a candidate golden case. A 15-minute weekly triage — read them, label the real failures, add them to the dataset, bump the version — is the flywheel from Part I turning. Teams that do this ship faster every month; teams that don't relitigate the same bugs forever.

Guardrail metrics vs quality metrics

Two families of number get plotted on the same dashboard and confused constantly, and the confusion is expensive in both directions. Guardrail metrics are cheap, continuous, computed on 100% of traffic, and they only tell you when something is broken. Quality metrics are expensive, sampled, and they are the only things that tell you whether the system is any good. A guardrail metric can never be a quality metric, no matter how correlated it looked last quarter.

Guardrail (all traffic, per-minute, alerting)
  • Schema-valid rate — outputs that parse. Should be 99.9%+; a dip is a real incident and it pages someone.
  • Refusal rate — a step change means a prompt or policy change, in either direction.
  • Empty / truncated rate — stop_reason == "max_tokens" climbing means inputs got longer.
  • p95 latency and cost per request — PR2 owns the budgets.
  • Tool-error rate — for agents, the fastest signal that something downstream changed.
Quality (80 sampled traces, weekly, reviewed)
  • Weighted correctness from the stratified sample — the headline for the customer.
  • Per-stratum correctness — the diagnostic that moves first.
  • Judged faithfulness, with its failure-recall figure quoted next to it.
  • Downstream acceptance — draft sent unedited, extraction approved. The best silent signal.
  • Escalation rate — noisy, confounded, and still the closest thing to a business outcome.
Every guardrail metric is green during a quality collapse. A model that has started confidently inventing order numbers emits perfectly valid JSON, in 900 ms, at the usual cost, with a normal refusal rate. Schema-valid rate: 100%. p95: flat. Error budget: untouched. The only instrument that sees it is a grader comparing outputs to expected outcomes on sampled traffic. If a customer's "AI monitoring" is four charts of latency and error rate, they have an availability dashboard, and telling them so — kindly, early, with the sampled-score chart next to it — is one of the more valuable things you will do on the engagement.

import statistics, sys, json

hist = json.load(open("reports/weekly_scores.json"))   # [{week, weighted, strata}, ...]
cur, prior = hist[-1], hist[-5:-1]                       # this week vs the previous four
triggers = []

mean4 = statistics.mean(w["weighted"] for w in prior)
if cur["weighted"] < mean4 - 5:
    triggers.append(f"weighted {cur['weighted']:.1f} vs 4-wk mean {mean4:.1f}")

for s, score in cur["strata"].items():                    # the one that fires first, in practice
    m = statistics.mean(w["strata"][s] for w in prior if s in w["strata"])
    if score < m - 25:
        triggers.append(f"stratum {s}: {score:.0f} vs {m:.0f}")

          for a, b in zip(hist[-4:-1], hist[-3:])]     # slow bleed beats big dip
if len(deltas) == 3 and all(d < 0 for d in deltas):
    triggers.append(f"3 consecutive declines: {[round(d,1) for d in deltas]}")

Tune the constants to your sample size and then leave them alone. At n=80 a 5-point weighted threshold fires on roughly a 1.7-sigma move — often enough to be useful, rarely enough to be believed. The 25-point per-stratum clause is deliberately blunt because the strata are small: with 8 non_english traces, 25 points is two cases, which is the smallest move worth a human's attention. And the three-consecutive-declines clause exists because the failure that actually kills engagements is not a cliff, it is a 1.5-point slide every week for two months, which no single-week threshold will ever catch and which is obvious the moment you look for it.

Self-check for Part VI: if your monitoring paged you today, could you name the ten traces that caused it within five minutes? If the answer is "I'd have to go query something", the loop isn't closed — the sampled traces that produced the score must be stored with the score, so the alert arrives with its own evidence attached.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Vibe-shipping prompt changes

"I tried three examples and it looks better" is how regressions reach the customer's champion. If a change touches a prompt or model, the harness runs. No exceptions — especially not for your own changes.

✗ Tuning on the test set

Iterate a prompt against specific cases and you've memorized them, not generalized. Keep a holdout you never look at while tuning, refresh it from production, and report holdout numbers to the customer.

✗ Trusting an uncalibrated judge

An LLM-judge always returns a verdict — that's not the same as a correct one. No human-agreement measurement, no trust. A wrong judge in CI is worse than none: it launders bad changes as green checkmarks.

✗ 1–10 scores and single aggregates

"Average quality 7.3" answers no question anyone has. Binary criteria tell you what failed; per-tier breakdowns tell you where. Numbers that don't point at a fix are decoration.

✗ Reporting agreement without failure recall

"92% agreement" on a corpus that is 85% passing means the judge beats "say yes to everything" by seven points and may be catching one failure in three. Quote failure recall next to every agreement number, and enrich the calibration sample until the base rate is near 50%.

✓ Prefer the cheapest trustworthy grader

Decompose every fuzzy judgment until most criteria are programmatic — free, deterministic, unarguable. Save the judge for what only a judge can do, and the humans for anchoring the judge.

✓ Every production failure becomes a case

The 15-minute weekly triage — mine failed traces, label, add to the regression tier, bump the dataset version — is the highest-leverage ritual in this entire skill catalog. It compounds.

✓ Measure your noise band before trusting any delta

Five identical runs on day one tell you what "no change" looks like — ±2 points on 84 cases, and which five cases are unstable. Every threshold you set afterwards is grounded instead of guessed, and you stop chasing regressions that were always weather.

✓ Optimise for the harness being run, not being right

Under 90 seconds, one command, failures printed first, cost shown so people know a re-run is free. A slightly cruder harness the team runs on every change beats a rigorous one they run before releases, by a margin that is not close.

import json, re

def g_json_schema(raw):
    try: d = json.loads(raw)
    except Exception: return False, "not valid JSON"
    missing = {"name","order_id","category","refund_requested","amount"} - d.keys()
    return (not missing, f"missing: {missing}" if missing else "ok")

def g_exact(d, exp, field):
    ok = d.get(field) == exp[field]
    return ok, "ok" if ok else f"{field}: got {d.get(field)!r} want {exp[field]!r}"

def g_numeric(d, exp, tol=0.01):
    if exp["amount"] is None: return d.get("amount") is None, "expected null amount"
    try: return abs(float(d["amount"]) - float(exp["amount"])) <= tol, "ok"
    except (TypeError, ValueError, KeyError): return False, f"bad amount: {d.get('amount')!r}"

def g_regex(d):
    if d.get("order_id") is None: return True, "no id to check"
    ok = bool(re.match(r"^[A-Z]-\d{5}$", str(d["order_id"])))
    return ok, "ok" if ok else f"malformed order_id: {d['order_id']!r}"
Four grader types: schema validity, exact match, numeric tolerance, regex format. All free, all deterministic, all reusable at every future engagement.

import anthropic, hashlib, json, pathlib, sys
from collections import defaultdict
from graders import g_json_schema, g_exact, g_numeric, g_regex

llm = anthropic.Anthropic()
CACHE = pathlib.Path(".eval_cache"); CACHE.mkdir(exist_ok=True)
CASES = [json.loads(l) for l in open("cases.jsonl")]
PROMPT = pathlib.Path("prompts/extract_v1.txt").read_text()

def model_output(case):
    key = hashlib.sha256((PROMPT + case["input"]).encode()).hexdigest()[:16]
    f = CACHE / f"{key}.json"
    if f.exists(): return f.read_text()
    msg = llm.messages.create(model="claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens=300,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT.format(email=case["input"])}])
    if msg.stop_reason == "refusal":            # never crash on a refusal
        return json.dumps({"_refusal": True})
    out = msg.content[0].text
    f.write_text(out)
    return out

def grade(case):
    raw = model_output(case)
    ok, why = g_json_schema(raw)
    if not ok: return False, why
    d, exp = json.loads(raw), case["expected"]
    for field in ("name", "order_id", "category", "refund_requested"):
        ok, why = g_exact(d, exp, field)
        if not ok: return False, why
    ok, why = g_numeric(d, exp)
    if not ok: return False, why
    return g_regex(d)

def main():
    tiers, rows = defaultdict(lambda: [0, 0]), []
    for c in CASES:
        ok, why = grade(c)
        tiers[c["tier"]][0] += ok; tiers[c["tier"]][1] += 1
        rows.append((c["id"], c["tier"], "PASS" if ok else "FAIL", why))
    for r in rows: print("  {:4s} {:12s} {:4s} {}".format(*r))
    print("\n  tier scores:")
    for t, (p, n) in sorted(tiers.items()):
        print(f"    {t:12s} {p}/{n}  ({p/n:.0%})")
    if "--json" in sys.argv:
        json.dump({t: p/n for t, (p, n) in tiers.items()},
                  open("reports/latest.json", "w"))

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

import anthropic, json
llm = anthropic.Anthropic()

RUBRIC = """You are grading a summary against its source document.

Criteria — answer each strictly true or false:
1. faithful — every claim in the summary is supported by the source
2. complete — every number and date from the source's key points appears
3. concise  — the summary is 120 words or fewer
4. no_meta  — no phrases like "This document discusses..."

First, for each criterion, quote the evidence that decides it.
Then output exactly one final line of JSON:
{{"faithful": true, "complete": true, "concise": true, "no_meta": true}}

SOURCE:
{source}

SUMMARY:
{summary}"""

def judge(source, summary):
    msg = llm.messages.create(model="claude-haiku-4-5", max_tokens=700,
        messages=[{"role": "user",
                   "content": RUBRIC.format(source=source, summary=summary)}])
    if msg.stop_reason == "refusal":       # ungraded, never counted as a pass
        return None
    last = msg.content[0].text.strip().splitlines()[-1]
    return json.loads(last)
Note the judge model differs from the generator — that's the self-preference mitigation, built in from the start. And note what is absent: no temperature, because it is rejected on current models and never bought determinism anyway.

Position-bias check. Build a pairwise judge and run 10 summary pairs both ways: 
PAIR = """Which summary of the source is better overall? Reply with
exactly one word: A or B.

SOURCE:
{source}

SUMMARY A:
{a}

SUMMARY B:
{b}"""

def pairwise(source, a, b):
    msg = llm.messages.create(model="claude-haiku-4-5", max_tokens=5,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": PAIR.format(source=source, a=a, b=b)}])
    return msg.content[0].text.strip()

flips = 0
for p in pairs:                                # 10 pairs of summaries
    first  = pairwise(p["source"], p["a"], p["b"])   # a shown first
    second = pairwise(p["source"], p["b"], p["a"])   # order swapped
    # consistent judge: winner tracks the summary, not the slot —

8
Now the number that decides whether you can ship it: failure recall. Agreement is inflated by the base rate, so first enrich the sample — take 10 of your good summaries and break them deliberately (delete a figure, change a percentage, add a claim the source doesn't make, prepend "This document discusses…"). Re-label, re-judge, then compute all four numbers: 
import statistics
def report(human, verdicts, crit):
    ids = sorted(human)
    h = [human[i][crit] for i in ids]; j = [verdicts[i][crit] for i in ids]
    n = len(ids)
    agree    = sum(a == b for a, b in zip(h, j)) / n
    baseline = max(h.count(True), h.count(False)) / n
    py, pj   = h.count(True)/n, j.count(True)/n
    chance   = py*pj + (1-py)*(1-pj)
    kappa    = (agree - chance) / (1 - chance)
    fails    = [k for k, a in zip(ids, h) if a is False]
    caught   = [k for k in fails if verdicts[k][crit] is False]
    return dict(agreement=round(agree,2), baseline=round(baseline,2),
                kappa=round(kappa,2),
                failure_recall=round(len(caught)/len(fails), 2),
                missed=[k for k in fails if k not in caught])

for c in ("faithful","complete","concise","no_meta"):
    print(c, report(human, verdicts, c))
Before enrichment you will typically see something like faithful {'agreement': 0.9, 'baseline': 0.85, 'kappa': 0.46, 'failure_recall': 0.33} — 90% "accurate" and catching one failure in three. After enrichment the same judge reads {'agreement': 0.9, 'baseline': 0.5, 'kappa': 0.8, 'failure_recall': 0.9}. The judge did not change; the measurement got honest. Ship the judge only when failure recall is ≥0.8, and record the missed list — those cases go straight into your golden set.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Last line isn't valid JSON → the judge sometimes appends commentary; take the last line that parses, and strengthen "exactly one final line". The durable fix is output_config={"format": {"type": "json_schema", "schema": ...}} with evidence first in required — you get reason-then-verdict enforced by the format rather than requested in prose.
  • Agreement stuck below 85% on faithful → your sources are too long for reliable checking; judge per-claim (split the summary into sentences, judge each) — more calls, far more reliable.
  • All 20 of your labels are "true" → your generator is too good for this corpus; that's exactly what step 8 fixes. A judge calibrated only on passes cannot detect failures and its agreement number is measuring the base rate.
  • Kappa is high but failure recall is low → you have very few real failures in the sample; len(fails) under about 6 makes both numbers unstable. Break more summaries.
  • Judge returns None for some cases → stop_reason == "refusal", usually on a source with sensitive content. Count these as ungraded in a separate bucket; never fold a refusal into either the pass or the fail count.

# per-tier tolerance for score drops (fractions, negative = allowed drop)
THRESHOLDS = {"normal": -0.07, "hard": -0.01, "adversarial": 0.0, "regression": 0.0}

def run_harness():
    subprocess.run([sys.executable, "run_evals.py", "--json"], check=True)
    return json.load(open("reports/latest.json"))

baseline = json.load(open("reports/baseline.json"))
scores = run_harness()

def regressions(scores):
    out = []
    for tier, allowed in THRESHOLDS.items():
        delta = scores.get(tier, 0) - baseline.get(tier, 0)
        if delta < allowed:
            out.append(f"{tier}: {baseline[tier]:.0%} → {scores[tier]:.0%} "
                       f"(allowed drop {allowed:.0%})")
    return out

bad = regressions(scores)
if bad:
    print("⚠ possible regression — flake policy: retrying once...")
    scores = run_harness()          # cache-miss only on genuinely failing cases
    bad = regressions(scores)
if bad:
    print("❌ GATE FAILED:"); [print("   " + b) for b in bad]; sys.exit(1)
print("✅ gate passed — per-tier scores at or above baseline")
Design notes worth internalizing: regression and adversarial tiers get zero tolerance (those bugs already happened once); the retry re-runs the harness but the cache makes it cheap — only failing cases actually hit the API again; and never loop the retry — once is a flake policy, twice is a slot machine.

BLOCKING  = {"regression", "adversarial", "hard"}   # deterministic graders only
ADVISORY  = {"judge:faithful", "judge:complete"}    # until 4+ weeks of calibration

Judge-scored criteria start advisory on purpose — promote them to blocking only once the judge has weeks of calibration history behind it, because a gate that blocks on an uncalibrated judge is deleted by the third false alarm and takes the credible parts with it.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Gate passes when it should fail → you re-baselined on the broken run. The baseline only updates on merge to main, never on a branch.
  • CI run costs too much → confirm the cache directory persists in CI (actions/cache keyed on prompt hash), and switch CI judging to a haiku-class model.
  • The sabotage doesn't break anything → your hard tier is too soft; add the multi-currency and implied-amount cases from Lab 1 step 3 and try again.
  • Gate fires on unrelated commits → your thresholds are inside the noise band you measured in Lab 1. On a 30-case set one case is 3.3 points, so a −0.01 tolerance on normal means one flaky case blocks every PR. Set the normal tolerance to just outside your measured range and keep zero tolerance only where a single case genuinely must never regress.
  • The gate compares runs from different dataset versions → add the fingerprint check: if dataset_fingerprint()["sha"] differs from the baseline's, exit with "re-baseline on main" rather than printing a meaningless delta.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Fresh clone produces different numbers → something is uncommitted. Usual suspects: prompts/ gitignored by a stray rule, cases.jsonl edited but not committed, or the report quoting a run made before your last label fix. Diff dataset_fingerprint() in both checkouts first — it isolates dataset drift from code drift in one command.
  • The prompt change made things worse → that is a better report, not a failed lab. Keep the identical structure, put the damage in the "what moved" table, and end with Call: NO SHIP plus the specific follow-up. A no-ship report you wrote yourself is far more persuasive evidence of rigour than a ship report ever is.
  • Nothing moved at all — identical scores → your change was too timid, or the cache served stale outputs. Confirm the cache key includes the prompt text and the model id; if it does, make a bolder change (add a worked example, not an adjective).
  • The customer asks for a single number for the whole system → give them the weighted overall and the per-tier table on the same page, and never the aggregate alone. If pushed, the sentence that works is: "89% overall, but the number that decides whether we ship is the 71% on hard cases — here's what's in it."
  • You can't tag because the repo has uncommitted eval outputs → add .eval_cache/ and reports/latest*.json to .gitignore, but commit reports/baseline.json and every dated report. Baselines and reports are source; caches and scratch runs are not.
