Cloud AI Platforms

Deploy models through AWS Bedrock, GCP Vertex AI and Azure OpenAI: IAM roles instead of API keys, private endpoints instead of public internet, quotas and provisioned throughput instead of hope — the plumbing that turns a working prototype into something a customer's cloud team will actually run.

Why it matters for an FDE: Enterprise customers usually mandate the cloud they have already signed with — direct provider API calls are often not allowed.
Part I · Why this is a gatekeeper skill

"You can't call api.anthropic.com from here"

Week three of an engagement. The prototype works, the demo landed, the business sponsor is happy. Then a platform engineer joins the call and says the sentence every FDE eventually hears: "Just so you know — nothing in that subnet can reach the public internet, and Procurement won't onboard a new AI vendor this quarter. We're an AWS shop. Can you run it through Bedrock?"

That is not a technical objection you argue with. It is the shape of the deal. If you can answer "yes, here's the architecture, here's the IAM policy, here's what I need from your cloud team" within the same call, the project keeps moving. If you can't, the project stalls in a security review queue for six weeks while somebody else writes that document — and the momentum you built is gone.

Five reasons enterprises route AI through their own cloud

Understanding why lets you argue the exceptions credibly, and lets you stop arguing the ones you can't win:

DriverWhat they actually sayWhat's underneath
Procurement & billing"We can't onboard a new vendor this quarter."They have a multi-year committed spend with the cloud; AI usage draws down that commitment. A new vendor means legal, security questionnaire, a PO, a new invoice trail. Months.
Compliance posture"Our SOC 2 / ISO scope covers the cloud account."Their auditors already accept the cloud provider's controls. Adding a fourth-party processor re-opens the scope (PR5).

Score-4 move: ask the cloud/network question in discovery, before you write a line of prototype code (FD1). "Which cloud will this run in, and does that account have internet egress?" costs you thirty seconds and can save a rewrite.

The three majors, and where Claude lives

AWS Bedrock

Multi-model marketplace: Claude, Llama, Mistral, Amazon Nova, Cohere. Claude is a first-class citizen. IAM-native, PrivateLink, CloudWatch, Guardrails. The most common enterprise landing spot in APAC and the US.

GCP Vertex AI

Google's unified ML platform. Gemini plus a Model Garden that includes Claude. Service-account auth, Private Service Connect, VPC-SC perimeters. Common where the customer is already deep in BigQuery.

Azure OpenAI

GPT family only — no Claude. Entra ID auth, private endpoints, content filters. Dominant in large enterprises with Microsoft licensing agreements; you will meet it.

Write that asymmetry on your own whiteboard: Claude runs on Bedrock and Vertex; Azure gives you GPT. If the customer is an Azure shop and the solution depends on a Claude-specific capability, that's a conversation to have in week one, not week nine. (Microsoft Foundry has begun carrying Claude too — check current availability before you promise either way; see Resources.)

The portability principle: write once, deploy anywhere

Here is the discipline that makes this module cheap instead of expensive. In AI2 you built an LLM client wrapper: one module that owns retries, timeouts, token accounting and error mapping. Keep the cloud choice inside that wrapper. Everything above it — your prompts, your RAG pipeline, your agent loop, your evals — should never know which cloud it is talking to.

Get this right and "port the PoC to Bedrock" is a config change plus a day of infrastructure work. Get it wrong — provider SDK calls scattered across twenty files — and it is a two-week refactor performed under deadline pressure, in front of a customer who is now watching closely.

Part II · The mental model that transfers

Six concepts, three clouds, one shape

The single most useful thing about cloud AI platforms is that they are structurally identical. Learn the six concepts once and you can read any of the three clouds' documentation with the right questions in mind. You will never be an expert in all three — nobody is — but you can be fluent enough to run a design session in all three, and genuinely deep in the one your customer uses.

1 · Managed model endpoints

You do not host a model. The cloud exposes a regional HTTPS API that speaks a model-family-specific request format. You pick a model ID, you pick a region, you call. What differs is the naming and the wrapper: Bedrock has InvokeModel and the unified Converse API; Vertex has publisher-model endpoints under your project; Azure has deployments — a named instance of a model that you create before you can call anything.

2 · Identity: roles, not keys

This is the mental shift that trips up developers coming from provider APIs. There is no ANTHROPIC_API_KEY. Your compute has an identity — an IAM role, a GCP service account, an Entra managed identity — and that identity is granted permission to invoke specific models. Credentials are short-lived, rotated automatically, and every call is attributable to a principal in the customer's audit log.

What this means in practice: your container gets credentials from the environment, not from a secret you manage. On AWS: a task role (ECS), an instance profile (EC2), or IRSA (EKS). On GCP: Workload Identity. On Azure: a managed identity. Your code calls the SDK with no key at all — and if it works locally but 403s in the cluster, the problem is almost always the role, not the code (PR13).

3 · Private networking

By default, calling a cloud AI endpoint goes out over the public internet — TLS-encrypted, but leaving the VPC. Every major cloud offers a way to keep it inside their backbone: an interface VPC endpoint (AWS PrivateLink), Private Service Connect (GCP), Private Endpoint (Azure). You create the endpoint in the customer's subnets; DNS for the service resolves to a private IP; traffic never touches the internet, and the security team gets to say so in writing.

This is usually the single item that unblocks the security review. It is also frequently the item the FDE forgets, because everything works fine from a laptop.

4 · Regions & data residency

Model availability is regional and uneven. The model you demoed in us-east-1 may not exist in ap-southeast-1 yet. Cross-region inference profiles (AWS) and multi-region endpoints (Vertex's us / eu / global) improve availability and throughput — but they also mean requests may be served from a different region within the geography, which is exactly the detail a compliance officer will ask about. Know the answer before you're asked.

5 · Quotas & provisioned throughput

Every cloud meters you in requests per minute and tokens per minute, per model, per region, per account. Default quotas are sized for experimentation, not production. A launch that goes from 5 to 500 concurrent users will hit a throttling wall, and the fix — a quota increase request — is a ticket in the customer's cloud support queue that takes days, sometimes weeks. Provisioned throughput (Bedrock) and reservations (Vertex) buy guaranteed capacity for a committed price.

Plan quota like you plan a database migration. Estimate peak tokens/minute from your load model, compare to the account's current limit, and raise the request weeks before go-live. "We'll request more when we need it" is how launches slip (PR12).

6 · Logging, billing & observability

Because it is their cloud, it is their logs and their invoice. Model invocation logging goes to CloudWatch / Cloud Logging / Azure Monitor. Costs show up as line items in the customer's existing billing dashboard, tagged by whatever cost-allocation tags you remembered to set. Your own tracing (PR1) still matters — it is the only place with prompt-level detail — but you must also make the numbers visible where their finance team already looks.

Part III · AWS Bedrock, hands-on

The one you should go deep on first

Bedrock is where most FDE engagements land, and it has the cleanest story for Claude. Go deep here; carry the mental model to the others.

Step 0 · Enable model access (the step everyone forgets)

A fresh AWS account cannot call any Bedrock model. An account administrator must explicitly request access per model family, per region, in the Bedrock console under Model access. Until that happens every call returns AccessDeniedException — and the message says nothing about model access, so engineers burn an afternoon debugging their IAM policy. 

# confirm what this account/region can actually see
aws bedrock list-foundation-models --region us-east-1 \
  --query "modelSummaries[?providerName=='Anthropic'].modelId" --output table

# and which inference profiles exist (cross-region routing)
aws bedrock list-inference-profiles --region us-east-1 \
  --query "inferenceProfileSummaries[].inferenceProfileId" --output table

Always run this before writing code. Model IDs on Bedrock carry a provider prefix (anthropic.…) and cross-region profiles carry a geography prefix (us.anthropic.…, apac.anthropic.…). Copy the exact string this command prints rather than trusting a blog post — IDs change and vary by region.

Path A · boto3 Converse — the AWS-native way

The Converse API is Bedrock's unified message interface: same request shape for every model family, so a customer's platform team can swap Claude for Llama without touching call sites. Credentials come from the standard AWS chain — env vars locally, task role in production. No API key exists. 

import boto3, json

# No API key. boto3 resolves credentials from the environment:
# env vars → shared config → ECS task role → EC2 instance profile → IRSA.
brt = boto3.client("bedrock-runtime", region_name="us-east-1")

MODEL_ID = "us.anthropic.claude-sonnet-5"   # from list-inference-profiles

resp = brt.converse(
    modelId=MODEL_ID,
    system=[{"text": "You answer in one short paragraph."}],
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": [{"text": "What is a VPC endpoint?"}]}],
    inferenceConfig={"maxTokens": 512, "temperature": 0},
)

print(resp["output"]["message"]["content"][0]["text"])
print("tokens:", resp["usage"])          # {'inputTokens': 21, 'outputTokens': 88, ...}
print("latency ms:", resp["metrics"]["latencyMs"])

Streaming is converse_stream with the same arguments; you iterate resp["stream"] and pull contentBlockDelta events. Tool use is toolConfig — a normalized schema across model families (AI8).

Path B · the Anthropic SDK, pointed at Bedrock

If your code already uses the Anthropic SDK, you do not have to rewrite it. The SDK ships Bedrock client classes that keep the exact same messages.create(...) surface — you swap the constructor and the model ID, and everything downstream is unchanged. This is the single most valuable fact in this module, because it is what makes a direct-API PoC portable to a customer's cloud in an afternoon. 

pip install "anthropic[bedrock]" boto3
from anthropic import AnthropicBedrockMantle

# AWS credentials come from the environment, exactly like boto3.
# AnthropicBedrock (no "Mantle") is the older InvokeModel path — prefer this one.
client = AnthropicBedrockMantle(aws_region="us-east-1")

msg = client.messages.create(
    model="anthropic.claude-sonnet-5",     # note the anthropic. prefix
    max_tokens=512,
    system="You answer in one short paragraph.",
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "What is a VPC endpoint?"}],
)
print(msg.content[0].text)
print(msg.usage.input_tokens, msg.usage.output_tokens)
The three things that change, and nothing else: the client class (Anthropic → AnthropicBedrockMantle), the credential source (API key → AWS role), and the model ID (claude-sonnet-5 → anthropic.claude-sonnet-5). Your prompts, tool definitions, streaming code and response handling are identical. Lab 2 turns this into a config switch.

Caveat worth knowing before you promise it: not every first-party API feature reaches Bedrock at the same time. Batch processing, the Files API and some server-side tools are first-party-only. Check the platform availability table (Resources) before you design around a feature — and if a customer's requirement depends on one that isn't there, surface it in week one, not at UAT.

Least-privilege IAM — the artifact their security team reads

Do not hand a customer bedrock:* on Resource: "*". Hand them a policy scoped to the exact models you invoke, in the exact regions you deploy. It is five extra minutes and it changes how the review goes.

When you use an inference profile, note that you need permission on both the profile ARN and the underlying foundation models it can route to.

Six elements: private compute, a role, a private endpoint, a locked-down security group, provider-side logging, and your own tracing. Every security review of an AI workload is, in the end, a conversation about this picture. Learn to draw it from memory.

Creating the endpoint (what you hand their cloud team)

# 1 · the interface endpoint itself — one per region, in the app's subnets
aws ec2 create-vpc-endpoint \
  --vpc-id vpc-0abc123 \
  --vpc-endpoint-type Interface \
  --service-name com.amazonaws.us-east-1.bedrock-runtime \
  --subnet-ids subnet-0aaa subnet-0bbb \
  --security-group-ids sg-0app443 \
  --private-dns-enabled

# 2 · private DNS makes bedrock-runtime.us-east-1.amazonaws.com resolve
#     to a private IP inside the VPC — no SDK/client change needed.

# 3 · verify from inside the VPC (this is the acceptance test)
dig +short bedrock-runtime.us-east-1.amazonaws.com   # expect 10.x.x.x, not a public IP
The verification step is the deliverable. Anyone can create an endpoint. The engineer who pastes a dig output showing a private IP — and a failed call from a non-approved subnet — is the one whose architecture gets signed off without a second meeting.

Guardrails, logging and cost tags

Bedrock Guardrails is a managed policy layer applied on the request and the response: denied topics, word filters, PII detection and redaction, and contextual grounding checks. It is not a replacement for your own defence-in-depth (PR4) — an attacker who gets past one filter is not stopped by the other — but it is the customer's own cloud-native control, which makes it disproportionately persuasive in a review. Attach it per call: 

resp = brt.converse(
    modelId=MODEL_ID,
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": [{"text": user_input}]}],
    guardrailConfig={
        "guardrailIdentifier": "abc123",
        "guardrailVersion": "1",
        "trace": "enabled",           # returns why it intervened
    },
    inferenceConfig={"maxTokens": 512},
)

if resp.get("stopReason") == "guardrail_intervened":
    # Design this path deliberately: log it, show the user something honest,
    # and never silently return an empty answer.
    log.warning("guardrail blocked", extra={"trace": resp.get("trace")})

Model invocation logging is off by default. Turning it on writes prompts and completions to CloudWatch Logs and/or S3 — which is exactly what an auditor wants and exactly what a privacy officer will want scoped and retention-limited. Decide this with the customer, not for them (PR5): 

aws bedrock put-model-invocation-logging-configuration --region us-east-1 \
  --logging-config '{
    "cloudWatchConfig": {
      "logGroupName": "/aws/bedrock/fde-pilot",
      "roleArn": "arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/BedrockLoggingRole"
    },
    "textDataDeliveryEnabled": true,
    "imageDataDeliveryEnabled": false
  }'

Cost tags close the loop with finance. Tag the endpoint, the role, and any provisioned throughput with a consistent project / cost-center pair, then activate those as cost allocation tags in the billing console. Three weeks later, when the CFO asks "what is this AI thing costing us?", the answer is a filter in a dashboard they already use — not a spreadsheet you maintain (FD4).

Part IV · Vertex AI & Azure OpenAI

Enough to run the design session

You will not be deep in all three. That is fine and expected. What is not fine is being unable to hold a coherent conversation when a customer's architect says "we're on GCP" — because that conversation happens in the first week, long before you can go and learn.

GCP Vertex AI

Vertex is Google Cloud's unified ML platform. Claude is available through the Model Garden; Gemini is the native family. The scope container is a project plus a location, and authentication is Application Default Credentials — the same mechanism every other GCP client library uses. 

pip install "anthropic[vertex]"

# Local dev: one command, no key file to manage or leak.
gcloud auth application-default login

# In production you attach a service account to the workload instead
# (GKE Workload Identity / Cloud Run service identity) — same code, no change.
from anthropic import AnthropicVertex

# Two required args. No API key: ADC resolves the credentials.
client = AnthropicVertex(project_id="customer-prod-1234", region="us-east5")

msg = client.messages.create(
    model="claude-sonnet-5",          # NOTE: no provider prefix on Vertex
    max_tokens=512,
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "What is Private Service Connect?"}],
)
print(msg.content[0].text)

Four things to have in your head about Vertex:

  • Model IDs carry no prefix — claude-sonnet-5, not anthropic.claude-sonnet-5. Dated snapshots use an @ separator (claude-opus-4-5@20251101), which is a genuinely easy detail to get wrong.
  • Region selection matters. global gives the best availability; us / eu multi-regions and specific regions like europe-west1 give residency guarantees. The customer's compliance answer decides this, not your latency preference.
  • Private Service Connect is the PrivateLink equivalent, and it is usually paired with VPC Service Controls — a perimeter that blocks data movement out of a set of projects. VPC-SC is powerful and unforgiving; if the customer runs it, expect an extra week and involve their platform team early.
  • IAM is role-based, not resource-policy-based. Grant roles/aiplatform.user to the service account on the project. Finer-grained per-model restriction is weaker than Bedrock's ARN-level control — know that, so you don't promise it.
# The Vertex equivalent of the IAM work in Part III
gcloud iam service-accounts create fde-ai-runtime --project customer-prod-1234

gcloud projects add-iam-policy-binding customer-prod-1234 \
  --member "serviceAccount:fde-ai-runtime@customer-prod-1234.iam.gserviceaccount.com" \
  --role "roles/aiplatform.user"

# Bind it to a GKE workload — no key file ever created
gcloud iam service-accounts add-iam-policy-binding \
  fde-ai-runtime@customer-prod-1234.iam.gserviceaccount.com \
  --role roles/iam.workloadIdentityUser \
  --member "serviceAccount:customer-prod-1234.svc.id.goog[ai-ns/ai-service]"

Azure OpenAI

Azure OpenAI is structurally different in one way that matters: you do not call a model, you call a deployment — a named instance of a model that an admin creates on a resource, with its own TPM allocation. The deployment name is chosen by the customer, so it is a configuration value in your app, never a constant in your code.

Authentication is Entra ID (formerly Azure AD). Key-based auth exists and most enterprises disable it. Content filters are on by default at a configurable severity, and a filtered response comes back as an error rather than a completion — a real behavioural difference your error handling must cover. 

pip install openai azure-identity
from azure.identity import DefaultAzureCredential, get_bearer_token_provider
from openai import AzureOpenAI

# Managed identity in production; az login locally. No API key.
token_provider = get_bearer_token_provider(
    DefaultAzureCredential(), "https://cognitiveservices.azure.com/.default"
)

client = AzureOpenAI(
    azure_endpoint="https://customer-aoai.openai.azure.com/",
    azure_ad_token_provider=token_provider,
    api_version="2024-10-21",
)

resp = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="gpt-4o-prod",              # DEPLOYMENT name, not a model name
    max_tokens=512,
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "What is an Azure private endpoint?"}],
)
print(resp.choices[0].message.content)
The Azure conversation to have early. Azure OpenAI serves the GPT family — there is no Claude on it. If your solution leans on a Claude-specific behaviour, you have three honest options: propose a second cloud path for the AI workload only (common, and easier than it sounds — a private-endpointed Bedrock or Vertex call from an Azure-hosted app is a normal enterprise pattern), re-validate the solution on GPT with your eval suite (AI9), or check whether Claude is available through Microsoft Foundry for that customer. Choose in week one and say so plainly (FD6).

The honest allocation of your learning time

✓ Deep on the incumbent

Whichever cloud this customer uses: know the IAM verbs, the endpoint service name, the quota page, the logging switch, the console screens. You should be able to pair with their platform engineer without reading docs mid-call.

✓ Fluent on the other two

Know the translation table, the auth model, the private-networking name, and whether Claude is available. That is enough to scope, estimate, and avoid promising something that doesn't exist.

Also worth knowing that the landscape has more than three doors: Anthropic-operated access through AWS infrastructure (SigV4 auth, AWS Marketplace billing, full first-party feature parity) sits alongside Bedrock and solves the same procurement problem with fewer feature caveats. When a customer's blocker is billing rather than network, that option is worth raising.

And there is a fourth door for the hardest cases: a genuinely air-gapped environment — defence, some healthcare, some government — where no managed endpoint is acceptable at all. That is a self-hosted open-weights conversation (AI12), with a very different capability and operations profile. Recognize it early: if the customer says "nothing leaves our data centre", cloud AI platforms are not the answer and you should say so on the call rather than three weeks later.

2 · The architecture that passes review

Security reviews are not adversarial — the reviewer wants a defensible yes. Give them five statements they can verify, and the meeting is short:

  • "Traffic never leaves your network." Private endpoint + private DNS, plus a Deny condition that makes any other path fail. Show the dig.
  • "Access is a role, scoped to these models, in this region." Paste the policy. No wildcards, no long-lived keys anywhere in the design.
  • "Every call is attributable and logged." Provider invocation logs plus your own tracing (PR1) — and a stated retention period.
  • "Data is not retained or trained on, and here's the contractual basis." The cloud's own terms, cited, not paraphrased (PR5).
  • "Tenants cannot see each other's data." How identity flows from the caller to the model call, and where you enforce it (PR13).

Write these as five headings with evidence underneath. That document is the deliverable in Lab 4.

3 · Quota & throughput planning

Do the arithmetic once, in the open, and put it in the doc: 

peak concurrent users            120
requests per user per minute     0.8
→ peak RPM                       96
avg input tokens                 3,200   (RAG context — measure, don't guess)
avg output tokens                450
→ peak TPM                       96 × 3,650 ≈ 350,000 tokens/minute

account limit today (Sonnet, us-east-1)   200,000 TPM   ← NOT ENOUGH
action: quota increase request, filed 6 weeks before launch
mitigation while pending:
  · route classification + extraction to Haiku       (PR2 — cuts ~40% of TPM)
  · prompt caching on the static system prefix       (AI3 — cuts input tokens)
  · queue + backoff on ThrottlingException           (PR3)
  · consider Provisioned Throughput for the peak window

Model routing (PR2) is a capacity lever as well as a cost lever — this is the moment that becomes obvious, and prompt caching (AI3) buys you input-token headroom for free on any workload with a stable system prefix. Multi-tenant fairness under a shared quota belongs to PR12: one enthusiastic tenant can consume the whole account's TPM and throttle everyone else unless you meter per tenant.

7Load test at projected peak; file the quota requestDiscovering the ceiling in production is the classic failure of this module.

Steps 1, 2 and 7 are the ones that take calendar time you don't control. Start them the day the customer says the word "Bedrock". Steps 3–6 are yours and take days. Sequencing that correctly is most of what senior looks like here.

5 · Keeping a fast lane for development

Argue for one thing, gently: a non-production path — direct API in your account with synthetic data, or an unrestricted dev project — so your eval loop stays fast. Iterating against a locked-down production VPC turns a 30-second experiment into a 20-minute deployment cycle, and slow loops produce worse systems. Frame it as risk reduction, offer the guarantee that no customer data touches it, and most security teams agree. If they don't, accept it gracefully and budget for the slower loop — the relationship is worth more than the convenience (FD7).

In the field

Regional insurer, 4,000 staff. Six weeks in, the claims-triage assistant is a hit in UAT. Then the go-live security review lands: "This calls a third-party API from a subnet that has no egress. We can't approve it."

The score-2 response is to escalate — ask the sponsor to push for an exception, and lose two weeks in a fight the security team will win. The score-3 FDE had already asked the egress question in discovery, so the wrapper was cloud-agnostic from the start. She flips LLM_BACKEND=bedrock, re-runs the eval suite against Bedrock overnight (44/46 pass, two prompt tweaks), and comes back the next morning with a four-page document: request-path diagram, the IAM policy with the aws:SourceVpce deny, the endpoint CLI commands, invocation-logging config with a 30-day retention, cost tags, and a TPM projection showing the account needs a quota bump from 200k to 400k.

Nothing in that story required deep AWS expertise. It required asking the right question in week one and having the document ready in week six. That is the whole skill.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Discovering the cloud requirement in week six

The most expensive mistake in this module, and the easiest to avoid. Two questions in discovery — which cloud, and is there egress — cost nothing and change everything.

✗ Provider SDK calls scattered everywhere

Twenty files importing the vendor client means porting is a refactor under deadline. One wrapper (AI2) means porting is a config change.

✗ Handing over bedrock:* on *

It works, and it tells the reviewer you didn't think about their problem. Model-ARN-scoped actions plus a source-VPCE deny take five minutes and change the tone of the whole review.

✗ Ignoring quotas until launch week

Default limits are experiment-sized. Load test at projected peak, file the increase weeks ahead, and have routing and caching as the mitigation while it's pending.

✗ Assuming same model = same behaviour

Version pinning, default parameters, feature availability and safety layers all differ across platforms. Re-run the eval suite after every backend switch (AI9) — never assume.

✗ Testing the private path from your laptop

Your laptop has internet. The pod doesn't. Verify DNS resolution and a successful call from inside the actual subnet, and verify a call from outside the approved path fails.

✓ Draw the request path in every kickoff

Compute → identity → private endpoint → model → logs → bill. Six boxes on a whiteboard surfaces every constraint the customer has, and marks you as someone who has done this before.

✓ Tag costs on day one

Retro-tagging is impossible — you cannot attribute last month's spend. Consistent tags from the first resource mean the ROI conversation (FD4) is a dashboard filter, not an argument.

The cloud-agnostic wrapper

🎯 Objective

Make "port this to the customer's cloud" a one-line config change. You will extend your AI2 client so identical application code targets direct-Anthropic, Bedrock and Vertex, then prove it by running the same test suite against every backend you have access to.

📦 Setup

1
Start from your AI2 wrapper if you have one; otherwise the file below is self-contained. Install whatever backends you can reach: 
pip install anthropic                 # direct — always available
pip install "anthropic[bedrock]"      # if you have AWS
pip install "anthropic[vertex]"       # if you have GCP
pip install pytest
You only need one working backend to complete this lab meaningfully. The design value is in the abstraction, and the test suite is written to skip backends you can't reach.

🔬 Steps

2
The factory. Create llm_client.py. The whole trick is that all three constructors return an object with the same messages.create() surface — so only the model ID and the constructor differ: 
import os
from dataclasses import dataclass

# One place, and only one place, that knows about clouds.

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Backend:
    name: str
    client: object
    model: str
    haiku: str

def _direct() -> Backend:
    from anthropic import Anthropic
    return Backend(
        name="direct",
        client=Anthropic(),                       # ANTHROPIC_API_KEY
        model="claude-sonnet-5",
        haiku="claude-haiku-4-5-20251001",
    )

def _bedrock() -> Backend:
    from anthropic import AnthropicBedrockMantle
    region = os.environ.get("AWS_REGION", "us-east-1")
    return Backend(
        name="bedrock",
        client=AnthropicBedrockMantle(aws_region=region),   # IAM role
        model="anthropic.claude-sonnet-5",                  # prefixed!
        haiku="anthropic.claude-haiku-4-5-20251001",
    )

def _vertex() -> Backend:
    from anthropic import AnthropicVertex
    return Backend(
        name="vertex",
        client=AnthropicVertex(                             # ADC / service account
            project_id=os.environ["GCP_PROJECT_ID"],
            region=os.environ.get("GCP_REGION", "us-east5"),
        ),
        model="claude-sonnet-5",                            # NO prefix on Vertex
        haiku="claude-haiku-4-5-20251001",
    )

_FACTORY = {"direct": _direct, "bedrock": _bedrock, "vertex": _vertex}

def get_backend(name: str | None = None) -> Backend:
    name = name or os.environ.get("LLM_BACKEND", "direct")
    if name not in _FACTORY:
        raise ValueError(f"unknown LLM_BACKEND={name!r}; "
                         f"expected one of {sorted(_FACTORY)}")
    return _FACTORY[name]()
Note the three deliberate details: lazy imports (so a missing optional dependency for an unused backend doesn't crash startup), per-backend model IDs (the prefix trap lives here and nowhere else), and a loud error on an unknown value rather than a silent default.
3
The call surface your application uses. Append to the same file — this is the only function the rest of your codebase imports: 
import time, logging

log = logging.getLogger("llm")

def complete(prompt: str, *, system: str | None = None,
             max_tokens: int = 512, fast: bool = False,
             backend: Backend | None = None) -> dict:
    """Cloud-agnostic completion. Callers never name a cloud."""
    b = backend or get_backend()
    model = b.haiku if fast else b.model      # routing lever — PR2

    kwargs = {"model": model, "max_tokens": max_tokens,
              "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": prompt}]}
    if system:
        kwargs["system"] = system

    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    msg = b.client.messages.create(**kwargs)
    dt = (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000

    # Same telemetry shape regardless of backend — PR1 depends on this.
    result = {
        "text": msg.content[0].text,
        "backend": b.name,
        "model": model,
        "input_tokens": msg.usage.input_tokens,
        "output_tokens": msg.usage.output_tokens,
        "latency_ms": round(dt),
        "stop_reason": msg.stop_reason,
    }
    log.info("llm_call", extra=result)
    return result
The contract is the point. Application code calls complete(...) and gets the same dict shape every time. Your tracing, your evals and your dashboards all work unchanged when the backend changes — which is exactly what makes the migration in Part V a config change.
4
The portability test suite. Create test_backends.py. It skips backends you can't reach, so it is honest about your environment rather than red: 
import os, pytest
from llm_client import get_backend, complete

BACKENDS = ["direct", "bedrock", "vertex"]

def _available(name):
    try:
        get_backend(name)
        return True
    except Exception:
        return False

@pytest.mark.parametrize("name", BACKENDS)
def test_backend_contract(name):
    if not _available(name):
        pytest.skip(f"{name} not configured in this environment")
    b = get_backend(name)

    r = complete("Reply with exactly the word: OK", max_tokens=16, backend=b)

    assert r["backend"] == name
    assert "OK" in r["text"].upper()
    assert r["input_tokens"] > 0 and r["output_tokens"] > 0
    assert r["latency_ms"] > 0
    assert set(r) == {"text", "backend", "model", "input_tokens",
                      "output_tokens", "latency_ms", "stop_reason"}

@pytest.mark.parametrize("name", BACKENDS)
def test_fast_route_uses_haiku(name):
    if not _available(name):
        pytest.skip(f"{name} not configured")
    b = get_backend(name)
    r = complete("Say OK", max_tokens=16, fast=True, backend=b)
    assert "haiku" in r["model"]

def test_unknown_backend_fails_loudly():
    with pytest.raises(ValueError, match="unknown LLM_BACKEND"):
        get_backend("azure-openai")
pytest -v test_backends.py
Expected: 2 passed, 4 skipped with one backend, or 4 passed, 2 skipped with two. Skips are information, not failure — they document exactly which clouds this workstation can reach.

6
Now find where the abstraction leaks — and write it down, because pretending it's perfect is worse than knowing the edges. Probe at least three of these and record what you find:
  • Errors. Trigger a failure on each backend (bad model ID). The exception types differ (botocore vs anthropic). Where should you normalize them — and what do callers actually need to distinguish?
  • Streaming. Does client.messages.stream(...) behave identically across your backends? Test it.
  • Feature gaps. Pick one feature you rely on (batch, prompt caching, a server-side tool) and check whether it exists on the partner platform. Note the answer — this is the check that saves an engagement.
  • Rate limits. Direct API limits and cloud quotas are different systems with different error shapes. Which retry policy covers both (PR3)?
Add a short # KNOWN DIFFERENCES block at the top of llm_client.py. An honest list of leaks is a stronger artifact than a claim of perfect portability — and it is the first thing a reviewer looks for.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • ImportError on AnthropicBedrockMantle → older SDK. Upgrade (pip install -U "anthropic[bedrock]"); the older AnthropicBedrock class also works but uses the legacy InvokeModel path.
  • Vertex 403 PERMISSION_DENIED → the service account lacks roles/aiplatform.user, or ADC is pointing at the wrong project. gcloud config get-value project.
  • Bedrock works but Vertex 404s on the same model name → you left the anthropic. prefix on. Vertex uses bare IDs; that's what the per-backend model field exists to prevent.
  • Tests pass but production 403s → local credentials differ from the workload's role. Always test from the deployment target too.

✅ Done when

One config value switches backends, the test suite passes on every backend you can reach and skips the rest cleanly, and you have a written # KNOWN DIFFERENCES list with at least three real entries.

The abstraction, the tests, and the loud-failure behaviour are all real and all reviewable; only the live confirmation is deferred.

print(resp["stopReason"])          # 'guardrail_intervened' on a blocked probe
print(resp.get("trace"))           # why it intervened — log this, always
Write three probes: one benign (must pass), one hitting a denied topic (must block), one containing a fake email address (must redact). Record all three results. No AWS access? Write the guardrail specification instead — denied topics with rationale, PII entity list with action per entity, and the app's behaviour when stopReason == "guardrail_intervened". That specification is the deliverable either way.
5
The data-handling section. Three short paragraphs, because these are the three questions every reviewer asks:
  • Retention & training. What the cloud's terms say about retaining or training on inference data — cited, with a link, not paraphrased from memory.
  • Logging. What invocation logging captures (prompts and completions), where it lands, who can read it, and the retention period you're proposing. Note that this is a customer decision, and give them a recommended default.
  • Residency. Which region serves inference, and — if you use cross-region inference profiles — which other regions could serve it, and whether that is acceptable under their policy.
Cross-reference PR5 for the compliance framing and PR13 for how caller identity reaches the model call in a multi-tenant system.
6
Pressure-test it. Give the package to a colleague and ask them to play a hostile-but-fair security architect. They ask five questions; you answer from the document alone, without improvising. Every question you cannot answer from the page is a gap to fix now rather than in front of the customer. Log the questions and your fixes — that log is itself good evidence.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Endpoint created but calls still go public → --private-dns-enabled was omitted, or a VPC-level DNS setting (enableDnsHostnames/enableDnsSupport) is off. dig is the fastest diagnostic.
  • Everything denied after adding the deny statement → the VPCE ID is wrong, or you're testing from outside the VPC (your laptop). Expected behaviour, in fact — confirm from inside.
  • Guardrail blocks everything → thresholds too aggressive, or a denied topic phrased too broadly. Tune with your three probes, and keep the benign probe passing as a regression test.
  • Reviewer asks about egress from the endpoint onward → PrivateLink terminates at the AWS service; traffic never enters the public internet. Have that sentence ready.

You have a one-page diagram, a complete IAM policy with a plain-English explanation per statement, endpoint commands with three verification steps, a guardrail (built or specified with three probes), a data-handling section, and a log of the five hostile questions with your answers.

The deployment package

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • The document is getting long → you are explaining the cloud instead of your design. Reviewers know their cloud; they need your choices and your evidence. Aim for four to six pages.
  • You can't answer Q6 (residency) → you haven't decided between a single region and a cross-region profile. Decide, and state the trade-off explicitly.
  • Section 7 has no numbers → you skipped the load model. Estimate from your prototype's measured tokens; a stated assumption beats a blank.
  • Route B feels like less work than Route A → it isn't, if you do it properly. Predicting every output correctly is harder than watching them happen.
