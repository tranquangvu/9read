Production Debugging & Incident Response

Diagnose production failures, communicate impact and create durable corrective actions — the discipline that turns "your AI is wrong" from an account-ending phone call into a postmortem the customer forwards to their board as evidence you are worth keeping.

Why it matters for an FDE: When the customer's AI system "answers wrong", the FDE must find out why within hours — the skill that keeps trust after go-live.
Part I · Why AI incidents are different

The system is up. The answer is wrong.

Every fullstack engineer arrives with a debugging reflex: something is broken, so look at the error. Stack trace, 500s on the dashboard, a red line on the latency graph, a failing test. The bug announces itself, and the loop from symptom to cause is a well-worn groove.

AI systems break that reflex on day one of production. The request returned HTTP 200 in 2.4 seconds. No exception was raised, no alert fired, no metric moved. The response was well-formed JSON, the UI rendered beautifully, the p95 is healthy — and the answer told a customer that their refund window is 90 days when the policy says 14. Nothing in your monitoring knows this happened. The only detector in the whole system is a human who noticed, and by the time they tell you, the wrong answer has been given four hundred times.

Four properties that change everything

Failures are silent

The failure mode with the highest business cost — a confident, fluent, wrong answer — is indistinguishable from success at the infrastructure layer. Uptime dashboards are green during your worst incident.

Failures are semantic

"Wrong" is a judgement about meaning, not a status code. Two engineers can disagree about whether a given answer is an incident. Severity has to be defined before the incident, or you will argue about it during one.

Reproduction is unreliable

You paste the question into your dev console, get a correct answer, and conclude "can't reproduce". Sampling, retrieval drift, a different prompt version, a different user's permissions, a stale cache — the customer's failure lives in a state you did not recreate.

The blast radius is trust

A five-minute outage is forgiven. Ten wrong answers to a VP's own question can end the rollout. Users who stop believing the system quietly stop using it — and adoption never recovers on its own.

The report you will actually receive

Classical incidents arrive as telemetry. AI incidents arrive as sentences, usually in a chat message, usually from a non-engineer, usually with no reproduction steps:

Classic incidentAI incidentWhat it costs you
Alert fires at 03:12"A few people said it's been weird since Tuesday"Detection lag measured in days, not seconds
Error rate 4.1% → 38%"It's wrong sometimes"No impact number until you go find one
Stack trace names the lineFluent paragraph, no evidence of its own originDiagnosis requires a trace you hopefully built (PR1)
Deterministic reproSame input, different output"Works on my machine" is now the default state
Rollback = redeploy the binaryRollback = which prompt? which model? which index?Mitigation is slow unless you versioned everything

And you are, most likely, alone

In a product company, an incident summons a rota: an on-call SRE, a comms lead, a service owner, a scribe. Embedded at a customer (FD7), the FDE is frequently all of those roles at once — the only person who understands the retrieval layer, the only one who can read the traces, and simultaneously the person the customer's operations director is messaging every twenty minutes. That is why this module is as much about process and language as it is about debugging: under pressure, alone, you do not rise to your intentions — you fall to the checklist you prepared in advance.

The go-live cliff. The most dangerous week of any engagement is the first week after launch. Real users ask questions your eval set never imagined, real documents are messier than your sample, and real stakeholders are watching. Teams that spent week one building the incident muscle — traces, severity definitions, a rehearsed rollback, a comms template — keep their accounts. Teams that spent it celebrating find out what a silent failure costs.
Part II · The AI-failure decision tree

From "it gave a wrong answer" to a named stage, in minutes

This is the core skill of the module and the single thing that separates a score-3 FDE from everyone else in the room during an incident. An AI pipeline has a small number of stages, and a wrong answer was produced by exactly one of them (occasionally two). Your job is not to fix it yet — your job is to name the stage, fast, with evidence. Everything after that is normal engineering.

The trace-first habit. Before you form a hypothesis, before you open a notebook, before you re-ask the question yourself: open the trace of the actual failing request. Read what really happened — the resolved query, the retrieved chunks, the exact prompt, the tool responses, the model id, the prompt version. Ninety percent of AI debugging is reading, not experimenting. Engineers who guess first waste hours confirming stories the trace would have demolished in ninety seconds.

The seven stages where wrong answers are born

StageSymptom that points hereThe ONE check that confirms itOwner module
1 · Input & parsing Answer is about the wrong thing entirely; follow-ups behave randomly Read the resolved query in the trace (post-condensation, post-template). Does it match what the user asked? AI3
2 · Retrieval Answer is plausible but unsupported; "it can't find things that exist" Read the retrieved chunks. Does the correct answer appear in them at all? If no → retrieval. If yes → not retrieval, move on. AI4
3 · Data & sync Answer was correct last month; quotes a superseded policy verbatim Compare updated_at on the retrieved chunk against the source system. Is the index behind? FN5
4 · Prompt & model Right context in the window, wrong conclusion drawn from it; format regressions Diff prompt_version and model against the last known-good trace. Something changed, or nothing did. AI1
5 · Tool & integration Numbers that don't match the source system; "it invented an order id" Read the tool spans: status, latency, and the raw response body. Empty or error payloads get narrated as facts. PR8
6 · Guardrail "It refuses normal questions"; suspiciously generic or truncated replies Check the guardrail decision span: was the output blocked/rewritten, and by which rule? PR4
7 · Provider Sudden burst of failures, timeouts, truncation across all query types at once Error/latency rate by model over time + the provider status page. Correlate with a timestamp, not a feeling. PR2

Memorize the shape, not the words: did we understand the question → did we fetch the right material → was the material current → did the model reason correctly over it → did the tools tell the truth → did a guardrail interfere → is the provider healthy. Walked in order, most incidents are classified before the customer's second message arrives.

The two cheap global checks come first on purpose. "Is it everyone or just this user?" and "did a guardrail eat it?" are answered in seconds from dashboards and disqualify entire branches. Only then do you descend into the per-request pipeline.

What a diagnosis actually sounds like

Practise saying it in this exact shape — stage, evidence, confidence, next step. It is how you sound calm even when you are not: 

✗ "Yeah, LLMs are non-deterministic, sometimes they just hallucinate."

✓ "Stage 3, data staleness — high confidence. Trace tr_8812 retrieved
   refund_policy.md chunk 4, updated_at 2026-05-11. The source document in
   SharePoint was updated 2026-07-29. Our connector last completed a full
   sync on 2026-07-14, so the last two weeks of policy edits are not in the
   index. Mitigation: force a re-sync of that library now (~20 min) and pin a
   'policy answers must cite a document updated within 90 days' check.
   Root cause on the connector to follow."

Same twenty seconds of speaking. One version ends the meeting with the customer reassured; the other ends it with them asking whether AI was a mistake (FD6).

Debugging tactics that pay for themselves

  • Bisect the pipeline, not the code. Feed the failing request's exact retrieved context to the model by hand. Correct answer → retrieval is innocent, look upstream at generation inputs. Still wrong → the model or prompt is the problem. One experiment eliminates half the tree.
  • Compare against a known-good trace. The fastest root cause in existence is a diff. Same question, last week, correct: diff prompt version, model, retrieved ids, tool payloads, user groups. The field that changed is your suspect.
  • Ask "why only this user?" Permissions (PR13), locale, tenant-specific config and per-tenant indexes are the classic explanations for a failure nobody else can reproduce.
  • Count before you dig. "How many requests are affected?" is both the severity input and a diagnostic: 0.2% of traffic is a data-specific bug; 60% is a deploy or a provider.
  • Trust the trace over the human report. Users compress, misremember and paraphrase. The trace is what happened. Reconcile the two gently — "I found the exact session, let me tell you what it did" — but debug the trace.
Part III · Incident response process

Stop the bleeding first. Understand it second.

Engineers instinctively want to understand. Incident response demands the opposite ordering: make the harm stop, tell people it stopped, and only then find out why it started. Every minute spent on root cause while users are still receiving wrong answers is a minute of avoidable damage — and diagnosis is easier, not harder, once the pressure is off.

Severity levels, defined for AI systems

Write these into the runbook before go-live, agreed with the customer. Severity drives who is woken, how fast you communicate, and whether you are allowed to break things to fix things.

SevCriteria (any one)ResponseComms
SEV1
harmful output		 Data/PII leak across users or tenants · legally or medically dangerous advice · discriminatory or abusive output · financial commitment made on the customer's behalf · systemic wrong answers on a regulated topic · the feature is fully down for everyone Drop everything. Mitigate within minutes — disabling the feature is an acceptable mitigation. Human gate before anything ships again. Customer notified < 30 min; updates every 30–60 min; written postmortem within 3 business days
SEV2
degraded quality		 Accuracy visibly below the agreed bar for a class of questions · a major integration failing · latency or error rate past SLO · citations broken · one important workflow unusable Mitigate same day; rollback or fallback preferred over a forward fix; feature stays up Customer notified same day; daily update until closed; postmortem in the weekly report
SEV3
cosmetic / narrow		 Formatting or tone issues · a single edge-case question wrong · slow but within SLO · a minor UI or citation-label glitch Ticket it, batch it into the next release, add an eval case Mentioned in the weekly report; no interrupt
Two rules that prevent the classic arguments. (1) Anyone can declare a SEV1; only the incident lead can downgrade one. Over-declaring costs an hour; under-declaring costs the account. (2) Severity is about impact, not effort. A one-character prompt typo that causes wrong dosage guidance is a SEV1, and a week-long re-architecture that annoys nobody is a SEV3.

The response loop — two clocks running at once

Most teams collapse the two clocks into one and run them at the speed of the slow one. The whole art is separating them: mitigation is not a fix and does not need to be principled — it needs to be fast, reversible and verifiable.

The mitigation menu — pick in this order

1 · Roll back the version

Prompt, model, config or index version back to the last known-good. Seconds if you built it, hours if you didn't. Always the first candidate when the incident started right after a change.

2 · Flip to the fallback

Alternate model or provider when the primary is degraded; cached/simplified path when a dependency is down (PR2, PR3).

3 · Disable the offender

Turn off the one tool, source, or feature flag causing harm and let the rest keep working. Narrow beats total: degrade the feature, not the product.

4 · Tighten a guardrail

Block the harmful class of output or force a refusal on the affected topic while you investigate (PR4). Conservative and reversible.

5 · Insert a human gate

Route affected requests to review-before-send. Slower for users, but the product keeps working and the harm stops. Often the most palatable SEV1 mitigation for a customer.

6 · Turn it off

Last resort, and a legitimate one. "We disabled the assistant at 14:20 while we investigate" is a sentence customers respect far more than a day of quietly-wrong answers.

Whatever you choose: verify the mitigation actually worked before you announce it. Re-run the failing request, watch the metric that detected the incident, confirm on a real user session. Announcing a fix that didn't take is the fastest way to burn the credibility you are trying to protect.

Rollback is a design decision you make months earlier

You cannot roll back what you never versioned. The mitigation menu above is only available to systems where every behaviour-defining artifact is a versioned, pinned, swappable object — the discipline built in AI1 (prompts as versioned artifacts), PR2 (model routing and fallbacks) and PR7 (deploy/rollback pipelines): 

# config/active.yaml — the entire behaviour of the system, in one revertible file
prompt_version:   qa_answer@v14        # git-tracked file, immutable once released
model:            claude-sonnet-5      # pinned id, never an alias that moves under you
fallback_model:   claude-haiku-4-5-20251001
index_version:    kb-2026-07-28        # the previous index is retained for 30 days
guardrail_set:    strict-v3
tools_enabled:    [lookup_order, kb_search]   # refund_api removed → tool disabled in 1 line
human_review:     off                  # flip to `on` = instant human gate

# every trace records these six fields. every deploy is a diff of this file.
# rollback = git revert + reload. Mean time to mitigate: under two minutes.
The rehearsal rule. A rollback path that has never been executed is a hypothesis, not a capability. Practise it in a calm week — deliberately deploy a bad prompt to staging, roll it back, and time yourself (Lab 2). Teams discover their rollback is broken during the incident approximately every single time they have not rehearsed it.

Part IV · Root cause & prevention

Making a nondeterministic bug hold still

With the harm stopped, the second clock starts. Its output is not "we fixed it" — it is "this class of failure cannot silently recur". That requires three things in sequence: a reliable reproduction, an honest causal analysis, and a durable prevention artifact.

Capture and replay: the antidote to "works on my machine"

Re-typing the user's question into your dev environment reproduces almost nothing. You have a different prompt version, a different index, different retrieved chunks, different permissions, different sampling parameters and a different day. Reproduction means replaying the request from the trace, not re-enacting it from the story. Everything you need should already be in the span (PR1): 

REPLAY BUNDLE — everything required to make a failure deterministic
├─ input          user message, conversation history, resolved/condensed query
├─ params         model id, temperature, top_p, max_tokens, stop sequences, seed if any
├─ versions       prompt_version, config_version, index_version, guardrail_set, code sha
├─ context        the exact retrieved chunk ids + their text as sent, in order
├─ tools          each call's arguments and the exact response payload returned
├─ identity       user groups / tenant / locale (permission-shaped bugs live here)
└─ output         the model's response, finish reason, token counts, guardrail verdict

With that bundle you can replay offline: same prompt, same context, same params, tools stubbed with their recorded responses. Set temperature=0 to remove sampling noise, then run the bundle N times. Two useful outcomes, and both are progress:

  • Fails every time at t=0 → the failure is structural: the context, prompt or tool payload was genuinely insufficient or misleading. This is the good case; it is now an ordinary deterministic bug.
  • Fails 3 times in 10 → the failure is probabilistic: the prompt is under-constrained and the model was always going to get this wrong sometimes. The fix is never "retry" — it is a tighter prompt, a smaller decision per call, a structured output schema, or a validation step. Report it honestly as a rate, e.g. "reproduced in 30% of replays".
Temperature is a debugging instrument. Run the replay at t=0 to ask "is this deterministic?" and at production temperature to measure "how often?". Never quietly change production temperature as your fix — you have moved the failure rate, not removed the cause, and you have changed behaviour on every other query too.

Five whys, adapted for AI

The classic technique works, with one adaptation: stop looking for a single cause. AI incidents are almost always the intersection of a technical trigger and a missing safety net. If your chain terminates at "the model hallucinated", you have not finished — that is a description of the symptom, not an explanation of why your system let it reach a user. 

INCIDENT · assistant quoted a retired refund policy to 42 users

Why did users see wrong refund terms?
  → The answers cited refund_policy v2, which was retired in June.
Why was a retired document retrieved?
  → It was still in the index; the connector never propagates deletions.
Why does the connector not propagate deletions?
  → It was written as an append-only nightly sync in week 2 of the pilot;
    deletion handling was a known TODO with no ticket.
Why did nobody notice for six weeks?
  → No eval case covers "answers must come from current policy", and no
    monitor compares index doc counts against the source system.
Why did our release process allow it?
  → Prompt/model changes go through CI evals; data-pipeline changes do not.
    The ingestion path has no quality gate at all.

CONTRIBUTING FACTORS (not one root cause):
  1. Ingestion: no deletion propagation            → owner: data · fix by Aug 12
  2. Eval gap: no freshness/currency assertions    → owner: FDE  · fix by Aug 08
  3. Monitoring gap: no index-vs-source drift check → owner: FDE  · fix by Aug 15
  4. Process gap: data pipeline outside CI gating  → owner: eng lead · Aug 22

Notice how the chain walks from the answer, through the pipeline, into the process — and how the last two "whys" produce the prevention items that actually matter. The trigger (a retired document) will never repeat identically. The gaps (no freshness eval, no drift monitor) would have caught a hundred variants.

The incident → eval pipeline: the highest-leverage habit in this module

If you take one behaviour from PR11 into your career, take this one: every real production failure becomes a permanent regression case in the eval suite (AI9), the same day, before the postmortem is written. Not a ticket. Not a note. An executable case that fails today and passes after the fix, wired into CI so no future release can reintroduce it. 

# evals/regressions/INC-2026-07-31-stale-policy.jsonl
{"id": "INC-2026-07-31-a", "q": "How long do EU customers have to request a refund?",

            "cited_doc_updated_after": "2026-06-01"},

# CI gate: every prompt/model/index change runs this suite.
# A suite grown from real incidents beats any synthetic benchmark you can buy,
# because every case in it is a failure that genuinely happened to this customer.

The compounding effect is the point. After six months, your regression suite is the institutional memory of the engagement — and it is the artifact you hand over at the end (FD10) that lets the customer's own team keep the system trustworthy without you.

Closing the detection gap

The second half of prevention is making sure that if this does recur, you find out before the customer does. For every incident, ask: "what signal would have caught this on day one?" — and then go build that signal (PR1, PR4).

Failure classDetection signal to addWhere it lives
Stale or deleted source dataIndex-vs-source doc count & checksum drift; oldest-updated_at alarm; sync job success/lagIngestion monitor

Retrieval collapseAlert on empty-retrieval rate and on mean top-1 relevance score droppingRuntime metric

Tool/integration failuresTool error and timeout rate per tool; assert non-empty payloads before they enter a promptRuntime metric + code

A good rule for the postmortem: at least one action item must reduce time-to-detect, not just prevent the specific bug. Detection improvements pay out on incidents you have not imagined yet.

Part V · Communication

What actually keeps the account

Here is the uncomfortable truth of forward-deployed work: customers do not remember your mean time to recovery. They remember how it felt to be on the other side of the incident — whether they were informed or left guessing, whether you told them the impact honestly or made them extract it, whether the same thing happened again a month later. Incidents are, perversely, the highest-bandwidth trust-building opportunity in the entire engagement. Nothing else lets a customer see how you behave under pressure.

Communicating during the incident

Three rules, in priority order (they extend FD5 and FD6):

  • Be first. Tell them before they tell you, and if that ship has sailed, acknowledge within minutes. Silence is interpreted as either incompetence or concealment, and both are worse than the incident.
  • Lead with impact, in their units. Not "the retriever returned stale chunks" — "42 customer-support answers since 08:05 may have quoted an outdated refund policy; 31 end users affected; here is the list of sessions". Business impact, quantified, is what a stakeholder can act on.
  • Commit to the next update, not to the fix. "Next update by 10:00" is a promise you can always keep. "Fixed within the hour" is a promise you are guessing at, and a missed one costs more than the original failure.
--- INCIDENT UPDATE TEMPLATE (Slack/email, 5 lines, no jargon) -----------
WHAT:     Support assistant may have given outdated refund terms.
WHEN:     Started 08:05 today. Detected 09:14. Mitigated 09:31.
IMPACT:   42 answers, 31 end users. Affected session list attached.
          No data was exposed; no refunds were auto-approved.
NOW:      Rolled back this morning's prompt change; answers verified correct.
NEXT:     Root cause today. Written postmortem + prevention plan Thursday.
          Next update from me by 14:00 either way.

Two things that template does quietly but deliberately: it states what did not happen (the fear a stakeholder is actually sitting with is usually bigger than the reality), and it ends with a time. Every update ends with a time.

Never do these three during an incident: speculate about cause in writing before you have trace evidence ("we think it might be the model" becomes the customer's headline, then it turns out to be their data); minimize impact you have not measured ("only a couple of users" — you don't know that yet); or blame the provider, the customer's data team, or a colleague. Blame reads as deflection even when it is accurate, and the FDE owns the outcome regardless (FD8).

The blameless postmortem

Blameless does not mean nobody is accountable. It means the analysis assumes competent people acting sensibly with the information and systems they had — because the alternative (naming a culprit) reliably destroys the honest reporting you depend on. If the person who deployed the bad prompt fears the postmortem, the next incident gets hidden, and hidden incidents are the ones that end engagements. Every finding is phrased as a system property: not "Minh forgot to run the eval" but "the release path allowed a prompt change to ship without evals; CI now blocks it".

The seven sections
  1. Summary — 3 sentences a non-engineer understands
  2. Impact — numbers, users, duration, money if known
  3. Timeline — timestamped, from first bad answer to resolution
  4. Root cause & contributing factors — the 5-whys chain
  5. What went well — genuinely; this section is not filler
  6. What made it harder — the missing tool, the ambiguity, the gap
  7. Action items — each with an owner, a date, and a type
Action-item quality bar
  • A named human owner — never a team, never "we"
  • A date, and a ticket that exists before the meeting ends
  • At least one detection item and one eval item
  • "Be more careful" and "add documentation" are not action items
  • Reviewed at the next weekly — unclosed items are the real postmortem failure mode

Two audiences, one truth

Write the internal postmortem first — full technical depth, trace ids, code, the honest ugly parts. Then derive the customer-facing version from it. The customer version is shorter and less technical, never less true: same impact numbers, same timeline, same commitments, with implementation detail compressed and internal process laundry removed. If you find yourself writing a customer version that contradicts the internal one, stop — that is the moment engagements are quietly lost.

You have converted an apology into a demonstration that the customer's system got permanently stronger because this happened. That is the moment renewals are decided; more than one engagement has expanded off the back of a well-run incident.

Self-check before claiming 3: a customer messages "since yesterday it's been giving wrong prices to some customers". Without opening anything, say out loud: your first three actions in order, the severity you'd declare and why, the exact sentence you'd send within ten minutes, and the two artifacts that must exist before you call it closed. If that comes out fluently, you are at 3. If you started by asking "can you send me an example so I can try it?", re-read Part II.

Repro, RCA & the regression case

Take one nondeterministic failure, make it deterministic with capture-and-replay, run a real 5-whys, and convert it into a CI-gated eval case.

LAB 4 · EVIDENCE ~1.5 h

The full incident postmortem

Timeline, impact, root cause, mitigations and prevention items with owners — plus the customer-facing version. The catalog evidence artifact.

In the field

Insurance customer, week three after go-live. 14:40 on a Thursday, a message from the operations director: "Two of my agents say the assistant is quoting the wrong excess amounts. Should we turn it off?"

The score-2 response is to ask for examples, try them locally, get correct answers, and reply "I can't reproduce it — can you send more details?" Four hours later the customer has escalated to their CTO and the answer to "should we turn it off" has been decided by someone else.

The score-3 response takes eleven minutes. Pull all traces for those two agents' sessions; find six answers quoting an excess of £250 where the current schedule says £500. Query the traces by retrieved chunk id: eighty-one answers since 06:00 cite the same retired document — so this is not two agents, it is everyone, and the impact number now exists. The document was retired in the source system on Tuesday; the index still has it: stage 3, data staleness, and the connector has no deletion path. Mitigation at minute fourteen: add a hard filter excluding that document id from retrieval — one config line, reversible, verified by replaying all six failing questions.

At minute nineteen the customer gets a message: what happened, that it is mitigated, that eighty-one answers across the whole team are affected (not two), the attached session list so they can proactively correct any customer who was misquoted, and a promise of an update by 17:00. On Monday the postmortem shows a new eval case — "every policy answer must cite a document current as of today" — passing in CI, plus a nightly index-versus-source drift check that would have caught this on Tuesday afternoon.

Six weeks later that operations director sponsors the expansion into claims triage. Her stated reason, in the kickoff: "when something went wrong, they found it, told us the real number before we asked, and made sure it couldn't happen again." No successful demo ever bought that much credibility.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Debugging by re-prompting

Pasting the question into your own console reproduces your environment, not the user's. Different prompt version, index, permissions and day. Open the trace first — always (PR1).

✗ Root-causing before mitigating

Every minute of investigation while wrong answers keep shipping is avoidable harm. Roll back, flip the flag, gate it behind a human — then be curious.

✗ "The model hallucinated" as a root cause

That is the symptom. The cause is the missing context, the under-constrained prompt, the unvalidated tool output, or the absent guardrail that let it reach a human. Keep asking why.

✗ Going quiet while you fix it

Heads-down silence feels productive and reads as concealment. A two-line update every 30 minutes costs nothing and is most of what the customer will remember (FD5).

✗ Postmortems with no closed actions

An action list nobody reviews is theatre, and the second occurrence of the same incident costs ten times the first. Owners, dates, weekly review, done.

✓ Every incident becomes an eval case

Same day, executable, in CI (AI9). Your regression suite becomes the memory of the engagement — and the best thing you hand over at the end.

✓ Rehearse the rollback in a calm week

Break staging on purpose and time your recovery. An untested rollback path is a hypothesis; the incident is a terrible place to test it (Lab 2).

✓ Agree severities before go-live

Write the SEV1/2/3 criteria and the comms cadence into the runbook with the customer while everyone is calm. It converts a panicked judgement call into a lookup (FD6).

Lab 1 · ~2 h
Diagnosis drill: six traces, six stages

🎯 Objective

Build a deck of six realistic failing-request traces, then drill the decision tree against a stopwatch until "read trace → name stage → name the confirming evidence" is reflex. This lab is deliberately a drill, not a build: the skill is speed and discipline under an artificial clock, so that under a real one you have something to fall back on.

📦 Setup

1
Workspace: 
mkdir -p pr11-labs/fixtures && cd pr11-labs
python3 -m venv .venv && source .venv/bin/activate
pip install anthropic
export ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=sk-ant-...
If you already have a traced service from PR1, use real trace shapes from it instead of the ones below — the closer these look to your own telemetry, the more the drill transfers.

🔬 Steps

2
Write the six fixtures. Create fixtures/traces.jsonl — one JSON object per line, each a complete trace of a request that produced a wrong answer. Each contains exactly enough evidence to identify one stage. Here are two, fully written; write the other four yourself following the pattern. 
{"id":"tr_001","q":"How long do EU customers have to request a refund?",
 "resolved_query":"EU refund window",
 "prompt_version":"qa@v14","model":"claude-sonnet-5","temperature":0,
 "index_version":"kb-2026-07-28",
 "retrieved":[{"id":"shipping.md#2","score":0.71,"updated_at":"2026-06-02",
               "text":"EU deliveries arrive within 5 working days..."},
              {"id":"faq.md#9","score":0.66,"updated_at":"2026-05-20",
               "text":"Contact support for any order question."}],
 "tools":[],"guardrail":{"verdict":"pass"},
 "answer":"EU customers can request a refund within 30 days.",
 "expected":"14 days","user_groups":["staff"],"latency_ms":2410}

{"id":"tr_002","q":"What is the excess on policy P-88213?",
 "resolved_query":"excess policy P-88213",
 "prompt_version":"qa@v14","model":"claude-sonnet-5","temperature":0,
 "index_version":"kb-2026-07-28",
 "retrieved":[{"id":"policy_schedule.md#3","score":0.93,"updated_at":"2026-07-30",
               "text":"Standard excess: GBP 500 (effective 2026-07-01)."}],
 "tools":[{"name":"policy_lookup","args":{"id":"P-88213"},
           "status":"error","http":504,"latency_ms":4002,"response":null}],
 "guardrail":{"verdict":"pass"},
 "answer":"The excess on policy P-88213 is GBP 250.",
 "expected":"GBP 500","user_groups":["agent"],"latency_ms":7100}
Now write four more, one per remaining stage, each with a single decisive clue:
  • tr_003 · stale data — the retrieved chunk is perfectly relevant and highly ranked, but its updated_at predates a known policy change, and index_version is weeks old.
  • tr_004 · prompt regression — retrieval is perfect, the answer ignores the context, and prompt_version is qa@v15 while every good trace says qa@v14. Include a prompt_diff field showing a removed "cite your sources" line.
  • tr_005 · guardrail false-block — an ordinary question about a "termination clause"; guardrail verdict is "blocked" with rule:"self_harm_keywords", and the answer is a canned refusal.
  • tr_006 · provider degradation — finish_reason:"error", http:529, retries exhausted, plus a context field noting 38% of all requests in that 10-minute window failed the same way.
Writing these is half the learning: to invent a convincing failure you must understand the mechanism that produces it.
3
Build the drill runner. drill.py shows one trace at a time, hides the answer key, and times you: 
import json, random, time

STAGES = ["1 input/parsing", "2 retrieval", "3 data/sync", "4 prompt/model",
          "5 tool/integration", "6 guardrail", "7 provider"]
KEY = {"tr_001":"2 retrieval", "tr_002":"5 tool/integration",
       "tr_003":"3 data/sync", "tr_004":"4 prompt/model",
       "tr_005":"6 guardrail",  "tr_006":"7 provider"}

traces = [json.loads(l) for l in open("fixtures/traces.jsonl")]
random.shuffle(traces)
results = []

for t in traces:
    print("\n" + "=" * 70)
    print(json.dumps(t, indent=2)[:2200])
    print("=" * 70)
    for i, s in enumerate(STAGES): print(f"  {i+1}. {s}")
    start = time.perf_counter()
    guess = STAGES[int(input("\nstage › ")) - 1]
    why = input("the ONE field that proves it › ")
    secs = time.perf_counter() - start
    ok = guess == KEY[t["id"]]
    print(("✅ correct" if ok else f"❌ actual: {KEY[t['id']]}") + f"  · {secs:.0f}s")
    results.append({"id": t["id"], "ok": ok, "secs": round(secs), "why": why})

n_ok = sum(r["ok"] for r in results)
print(f"\nSCORE {n_ok}/{len(results)} · median "
      f"{sorted(r['secs'] for r in results)[len(results)//2]}s")
json.dump(results, open("drill_run1.json", "w"), indent=2)
The why prompt is not decoration — a correct guess with the wrong justification is luck, and luck does not survive a real incident.
4
Run it three times, at least an hour apart. Record score and median seconds each round in a small table. Target for run 3: 6/6 in under 60 seconds median. If a stage keeps fooling you, that is the stage to re-read in Part II — and probably the one you will be slowest on in production.
5
Write your personal one-page triage card. Distil the tree into something you could actually use at 09:14 on a Thursday: the two global checks first, then the per-request order, then for each stage the one field you read and the mitigation you would reach for. Keep it to one screen. This card is what you will actually open during an incident — the full page you just read is what makes the card make sense.
6
Stretch: the ambiguous seventh. Write tr_007 where two stages are plausible — e.g. retrieval returned a mediocre chunk and the prompt version changed the same morning. Practise saying the honest version out loud: "Most likely stage 4 — the prompt changed at 08:02 and the failures start at 08:05. Stage 2 is a secondary suspect; the rollback tests both hypotheses at once, so I'm rolling back first." Real incidents are usually this shape, and choosing a mitigation that discriminates between hypotheses is a score-4 move.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Fixtures feel unrealistically easy → you probably wrote the answer into the answer field's wording. Strip the tell; leave only the mechanism.
  • Can't tell retrieval from data-staleness → the discriminator is "is the correct text in the index at all?". Add an index_probe field to the fixture recording a direct lookup of the expected document.
  • Every fixture takes you three minutes → you are reading the whole JSON. Train yourself to jump straight to guardrail → tools → retrieved → prompt_version, in that order.

✅ Done when

Three recorded drill runs with improving times, 6/6 on the final run with correct justifications, and a one-page triage card you would genuinely open during an incident.

🎯 Objective

Turn "we should be able to roll back" into a measured capability. You will make prompt, model and config versioned artifacts of a running service, ship a deliberately bad prompt, and rehearse the mitigation with a stopwatch until it lands under five minutes.

📦 Setup

Answer the user's question using ONLY the sources provided.
Cite every claim like [1]. If the sources do not contain the answer,

🔬 Steps

2
A service that reloads config without a redeploy. service.py: 
import yaml, pathlib, time, anthropic
from fastapi import FastAPI

app, llm = FastAPI(), anthropic.Anthropic()
CFG = pathlib.Path("config/active.yaml")
_cache = {"mtime": 0, "cfg": None}

def cfg():
    m = CFG.stat().st_mtime            # hot-reload on file change
    if m != _cache["mtime"]:
        _cache.update(mtime=m, cfg=yaml.safe_load(CFG.read_text()))
        print(f"[config] reloaded: {_cache['cfg']}")
    return _cache["cfg"]

SOURCES = "[1] refund_policy.md (updated 2026-07-30): EU refund window is 14 days."

@app.get("/ask")
def ask(q: str):
    c = cfg()
    model = c["fallback_model"] if c["use_fallback"] else c["model"]
    system = pathlib.Path(f"prompts/{c['prompt_version']}.txt").read_text()
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    msg = llm.messages.create(
        model=model, max_tokens=400, temperature=0, system=system,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": f"Sources:\n{SOURCES}\n\nQuestion: {q}"}])
    return {"answer": msg.content[0].text,
            "prompt_version": c["prompt_version"], "model": model,
            "latency_ms": int((time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000)}
uvicorn service:app --reload --port 8000
curl "localhost:8000/ask?q=How+long+is+the+EU+refund+window%3F"
Every response echoes prompt_version and model. That is not a nicety — it is what lets you diff a bad request against a good one during an incident.
3
Build the canary. canary.py — three questions with known answers, run continuously. This is your detection layer: 
import requests, time
CASES = [("How long is the EU refund window?", "14"),
         ("What is the refund window for enterprise plans?", "14"),
         ("What is the price of a Model X?", "I don't have information")]

while True:
    bad = []
    for q, must in CASES:
        r = requests.get("http://localhost:8000/ask", params={"q": q}).json()
        if must.lower() not in r["answer"].lower():
            bad.append((q, r["answer"][:70], r["prompt_version"]))
    print(f"{time.strftime('%H:%M:%S')} · {len(CASES)-len(bad)}/{len(CASES)} ok"
          + ("  🚨 " + str(bad) if bad else ""))
    time.sleep(10)

4
Ship the bad deploy — on purpose, without looking at the clock yet. Create prompts/qa_v14.txt with a subtle, realistic regression (this is exactly how real prompt incidents happen — someone "improves" the prompt and deletes a constraint): 
# prompts/qa_v14.txt — "improved: friendlier and more helpful"
You are a friendly, helpful assistant. Answer the user's question.
Use the sources where helpful, and use your general knowledge of standard
industry practice to fill any gaps so the customer always gets an answer.
sed -i '' 's/qa_v13/qa_v14/' config/active.yaml   # macOS; drop '' on Linux
Watch the canary. Within ten seconds it should go red — the model now answers the unanswerable question from "industry practice", and may drift on the refund number too. You have just reproduced the single most common AI production incident there is.
5
Now the drill. Start the stopwatch when the canary goes red. Do all of it, in order, out loud:
  1. Note the time and the failing cases (impact).
  2. Diff config against the last known-good: git diff HEAD~1 config/active.yaml.
  3. Roll back: git revert or flip the version back to qa_v13.
  4. Verify: canary green for two consecutive cycles, and re-run the specific failing question by hand.
  5. Write the five-line customer update from Part V — actually type it out.
Stop the clock after step 5. Record the number. Repeat the whole drill twice more; most people go from ~9 minutes to under 3 by the third run, purely from knowing where things live.
6
Rehearse the other three mitigations, timing each: 
# fallback flip — primary provider degraded (stage 7)
sed -i '' 's/use_fallback: false/use_fallback: true/' config/active.yaml

# disable a misbehaving tool (stage 5) — one line, feature stays up
sed -i '' 's/tools_enabled: \[kb_search\]/tools_enabled: []/' config/active.yaml

# human gate for a SEV1 you cannot yet explain
sed -i '' 's/human_review: false/human_review: true/' config/active.yaml
Extend /ask so human_review: true returns a queued-for-review response instead of an answer. Confirm the canary reflects each change. Four rehearsed mitigations, each verifiable in seconds — that is what "mitigate before root cause" actually requires underneath.
7
Write the runbook page. Half a page, in the repo, that a colleague could execute at 3am without you: where config lives, the exact rollback command, how to verify, who to notify, and the SEV table from Part III. Then have someone else follow it while you stay silent. Every ambiguity they hit is a defect you just found for free.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Config change not picked up → the mtime cache; make sure you write the file rather than editing in an editor that writes to a temp path, or drop the cache in dev.
  • Canary stays green after the bad deploy → your bad prompt isn't bad enough, or the case is too easy. Strengthen "use your general knowledge" and add a question the sources genuinely cannot answer.
  • Rollback took > 5 minutes → find out which step ate the time. It is almost always "where is the config" or "how do I verify" — both are runbook defects, not skill defects.

✅ Done when

You have three timed rollback drills with a final time under five minutes, all four mitigations rehearsed and verified, and a runbook page someone else executed successfully without your help.
Lab 3 · ~2 h
Repro, RCA & the regression case

🎯 Objective

Take one failure that "only happens sometimes" and drive it all the way through: capture it, replay it deterministically, measure how often it really occurs, run a 5-whys that ends in system properties rather than blame, and leave behind an executable regression case that gates CI.

📦 Setup

1
Get a genuinely flaky failure. Best case: one from your own AI4/AI6 system. If you don't have one, manufacture a realistic one — an under-constrained prompt at production temperature is the most common cause of intermittent wrongness in the wild: 
# prompts/qa_v16.txt — plausible, under-constrained, intermittently wrong
Answer the user's question about policies. Be concise and helpful.
Use the sources below if they are relevant.
# flaky.py — run the same question 20 times at production temperature
import anthropic, collections, pathlib
llm = anthropic.Anthropic()
SRC = "[1] refund_policy.md (2026-07-30): EU refund window is 14 days. " \
      "Enterprise contracts are negotiated individually."
Q = "What's the refund window for our enterprise customers?"

out = collections.Counter()
for _ in range(20):
    m = llm.messages.create(model="claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens=200,
            temperature=1.0, system=pathlib.Path("prompts/qa_v16.txt").read_text(),
            messages=[{"role":"user","content":f"Sources:\n{SRC}\n\nQuestion: {Q}"}])
    out[m.content[0].text.strip()[:60]] += 1
for text, n in out.most_common(): print(f"{n:2d}×  {text}")
You should see the model sometimes correctly say enterprise terms are negotiated, and sometimes wrongly generalise "14 days" to enterprise. That distribution is the bug. Note the rate — you will report it.

🔬 Steps

2
Capture the replay bundle. Everything needed to reproduce, from the trace — never from memory: 
import json, datetime
bundle = {
  "captured_at": datetime.datetime.now().isoformat(timespec="seconds"),
  "trace_id": "tr_local_001",
  "input": {"question": Q, "history": [], "resolved_query": Q},
  "params": {"model": "claude-sonnet-5", "temperature": 1.0,
             "max_tokens": 200, "top_p": None},
  "versions": {"prompt_version": "qa_v16", "index_version": "kb-2026-07-28",
               "guardrail_set": "strict-v3", "code_sha": "local"},
  "context": [{"id": "refund_policy.md#1", "text": SRC}],
  "tools": [], "identity": {"groups": ["agent"], "tenant": "acme"},
  "output": {"answer": "Enterprise customers also have 14 days.",
             "finish_reason": "end_turn"}
}
json.dump(bundle, open("bundle_001.json", "w"), indent=2)
Rule to internalise: if a field is missing from your traces, you cannot debug that class of failure. Note every field you had to fabricate here — each one is a gap to close in PR1.
3
Replay it — deterministically, then statistically: 
import json, anthropic, pathlib
llm = anthropic.Anthropic()
b = json.load(open("bundle_001.json"))

def replay(n=1, temperature=None):
    sys_prompt = pathlib.Path(f"prompts/{b['versions']['prompt_version']}.txt").read_text()
    src = "\n".join(c["text"] for c in b["context"])
    outs = []
    for _ in range(n):
        m = llm.messages.create(
            model=b["params"]["model"], max_tokens=b["params"]["max_tokens"],
            temperature=b["params"]["temperature"] if temperature is None else temperature,
            system=sys_prompt,
            messages=[{"role": "user",
                       "content": f"Sources:\n{src}\n\nQuestion: {b['input']['question']}"}])
        outs.append(m.content[0].text.strip())
    return outs

det  = replay(1, temperature=0)                       # structural?
dist = replay(20)                                     # how often?
bad  = sum(1 for o in dist if "14 day" in o.lower())
print("t=0 reproduces:", "14 day" in det[0].lower())
print(f"production temp reproduction rate: {bad}/20")
Write down both numbers. "Reproduces at t=0" means a structural defect you can fix with certainty; "7/20 at production temperature" means an under-constrained prompt — and it is the honest thing to put in a postmortem instead of "intermittent".
4
Run the 5-whys properly. Write it in rca.md, one why per line, and enforce two rules: (a) it may not terminate at "the model hallucinated"; (b) the last two whys must be about your process, not the model. Then extract contributing factors with owners: 
# RCA — enterprise refund window answered as 14 days

Why wrong? → Generalised the consumer 14-day window to enterprise.
Why? → The prompt says "be concise and helpful", with no rule against
       extrapolating beyond the sources.
Why is there no such rule? → v16 dropped the "never state a number not in a
       source" line while shortening the prompt for latency.
Why did that ship? → Prompt changes are reviewed by eye; no eval covers
       "declines to extrapolate to an unlisted segment".
Why was it not caught in production for 11 days? → No alert on answers that
       state numbers absent from the retrieved context.

Contributing factors:
  1. Prompt: missing anti-extrapolation constraint  → owner: __ · date: __
  2. Eval gap: no negative/segment cases            → owner: __ · date: __
  3. Detection gap: no unsupported-number check     → owner: __ · date: __
Reproduction: deterministic at t=0: no · rate at t=1.0: 7/20 (35%)
5
Fix, and prove the fix with numbers. Restore the constraint (qa_v17), then re-run the same 20-sample replay. Report as a rate, never as "seems better": 
before  qa_v16 · t=1.0 · 7/20 wrong  (35%)
after   qa_v17 · t=1.0 · 0/20 wrong  ( 0%)   # same bundle, same params
If you cannot get to 0/20 with a prompt change alone, that is a finding, not a failure: escalate the fix to a structural one — a validation step that rejects answers containing numbers absent from the context, or a required refusal path.
6
Convert it into a permanent regression case (AI9) — the step most teams skip and the one that compounds: 
# evals/regressions/INC-local-001.jsonl
{"id":"INC-local-001","q":"What's the refund window for our enterprise customers?",
 "context_fixture":"bundle_001.json",
 "assert":{"not_contains":["14 day"],
           "contains_any":["negotiated","individually","don't have information"]},
 "runs":10,"max_fail_rate":0.0,
 "origin":"incident INC-local-001","sev":2,"added":"2026-08-04"}
# runner: n runs per case at production temperature, fails the build on any miss
import json, sys
cases = [json.loads(l) for l in open("evals/regressions/INC-local-001.jsonl")]
fails = 0
for c in cases:
    outs = replay(c["runs"])
    bad = [o for o in outs
           if any(s.lower() in o.lower() for s in c["assert"].get("not_contains", []))]
    rate = len(bad) / c["runs"]
    print(f"{c['id']}: fail rate {rate:.0%} (max {c['max_fail_rate']:.0%})")
    fails += rate > c["max_fail_rate"]
sys.exit(1 if fails else 0)
Note runs and max_fail_rate: a probabilistic system needs a probabilistic gate. A single-run assertion on a 35%-failure bug passes two times in three and teaches you nothing.
7
Wire it into CI so the gate is real: 
# .github/workflows/evals.yml
on: { pull_request: { paths: ["prompts/**", "config/**", "evals/**"] } }
jobs:
  regressions:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: pip install anthropic
      - run: python evals/run_regressions.py
        env: { ANTHROPIC_API_KEY: "${{ secrets.ANTHROPIC_API_KEY }}" }
Verify the gate honestly: open a PR that reverts to qa_v16 and confirm CI goes red. An untested gate is the same hypothesis problem as an untested rollback.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Can't make it fail at all → your sources answer the question too directly. Good failures need a gap the model is tempted to fill; remove the enterprise line entirely.
  • Fails 20/20 even at t=0 → excellent, that is a structural bug: easier to fix and easier to gate. Note it as such rather than forcing flakiness.
  • CI costs feel high → use the cheaper model for regression runs where the case doesn't depend on model strength, and gate the full suite nightly (PR2).

✅ Done when

You have a saved replay bundle, before/after reproduction rates as numbers, an rca.md whose last two whys are about process, and a regression case that goes red on the old prompt and green on the new one — in CI, demonstrably.

## Root cause & contributing factors
Root cause: qa_v14 removed the constraint "never state a number that does not
appear verbatim in a source", allowing extrapolation from consumer terms to
enterprise. Reproduction: not deterministic at t=0; 7/20 (35%) at t=1.0.
Contributing factors:
  1. Prompt changes shipped without an eval gate (process)
  2. No eval case covering segment-specific refusals (coverage)
  3. No detection for answers containing numbers absent from context (monitoring)
  4. Detection depended entirely on a user noticing (69 min to detect)

## What went well
- Traces contained prompt_version, so the config diff took 3 minutes
- Rollback was rehearsed; mitigation took 4 minutes from decision
- Customer was informed before they escalated internally

## What made it harder
- No impact query saved; the count was assembled by hand under time pressure
- Canary covered consumer questions only, so it stayed green throughout

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Timeline has gaps → you did not take notes during the incident. Next drill, keep a running log in a scratch file from minute one; reconstruction is always worse.
  • Impact is "some users" → go and count. If you cannot count it, your first action item is the query that would have let you.
  • Action items are all "add documentation" → none of those prevent recurrence. Every postmortem should yield at least one eval case and one detection change.
  • The customer version feels too blunt → check whether you are protecting them or yourself. Plain facts plus dated commitments read as competence, not weakness.
