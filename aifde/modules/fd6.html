Expectation Management

Explain hallucinations and LLM limits in customer language; set realistic success metrics; push back respectfully. The skill that converts your technical honesty into the customer's confidence.

Why it matters for an FDE: Overpromising is the #1 pilot killer in AI consulting — trust is built on technical honesty.
Part I · The mechanics

Why pilots die of overpromise

Almost no AI pilot dies because the model was too weak. They die because the model was described as something it is not, and everything downstream — the budget, the timeline, the acceptance criteria, the user's first impression — was built on that description. The engineering was fine. The expectation was load-bearing and it snapped.

Expectation management is not softness or hedging. It is a delivery discipline: you are managing the gap between what the customer believes will happen and what the system will actually do, continuously, from the first discovery call (FD1) to the last handover session (FD10). When that gap stays small, surprises are non-events. When it grows silently, one ordinary Tuesday turns into a crisis.

The four-act structure of a failed pilot

Act 1 · Demo magic

A prototype on five hand-picked documents works beautifully. Everyone in the room extrapolates from n=5 to n=50,000. Nobody says the word "sample".

Act 2 · Production reality

Real inputs arrive: scanned faxes, three date formats, a 400-page attachment, the client who writes in two languages. Accuracy lands at 84%, not the demo's apparent 100%.

Act 3 · Disappointment

84% would have been a celebrated result if it had been the target. Measured against an imagined 100%, it reads as failure — and users start collecting error screenshots.

Act 4 · Withdrawal

Adoption stalls, the sponsor stops defending the project internally, phase 2 is "paused pending review". Nothing was technically wrong. The story was.

The cruelty of the gap: the same 84% system is a triumph or a disaster depending only on what was promised. Expectation management is therefore the cheapest lever you own — it costs one honest sentence at the right moment and it moves the perceived outcome more than a month of tuning.

Who overpromises, and why they can't help it

Overpromising is almost never dishonesty. It is four ordinary human incentives pointing the same direction, and you will feel all four yourself:

The FDE's job: the honest broker

Every project has people whose job is optimism. Almost none has someone whose job is calibration. That is you. The FDE is the only person in the room who has both the technical knowledge to know what will break and the customer relationship to say so before it does.

The asset you are building: a "no" that is worth something. If you say yes to everything, your yes carries no information and nobody can plan against it. If you say no rarely, reason it out loud, and pair it with a viable alternative, then your "this will work" becomes the sentence the sponsor repeats to their board. Credibility is a budget: it is spent by being wrong loudly and earned by being right quietly.
  • Honesty is not pessimism. "This won't work" is useless. "This won't work at 100%, and here's the 92%-plus-review design that delivers the same business outcome" is expectation management.
  • You are calibrating, not lowering. Sometimes calibration means raising expectations — telling a customer that the thing they think is a six-month project is a two-week one. That builds the same credibility.
  • Write it down or it didn't happen. Verbal caveats evaporate in about nine days. Anything that matters goes into the scope document, the weekly update, or the acceptance criteria (FD5).
Part II · The explanations

Explaining LLM limits in customer language

You will give the same five explanations hundreds of times across your career. Rehearse them until they are boring to you, because they are never boring to the person hearing them for the first time. Each one below has an exec version (60–90 seconds, no jargon, ends in a decision) and an engineer version (for the customer's technical counterpart, who will otherwise assume you're hand-waving).

Delivery rule: never explain a limit without immediately attaching the design response. A limit alone sounds like an excuse. "It does X, therefore we build Y" sounds like engineering.

1 · Why it makes things up

The single most important explanation you own. The customer's mental model is a database that sometimes has bugs. The correct model is a text predictor that is always predicting, whether or not it knows (FN2).

EXEC VERSION · ~75 SECONDS

"The model isn't looking anything up. It's predicting the most plausible next words based on everything it has read. Most of the time the most plausible answer is also the true one — that's why it's useful. But when it doesn't know, it doesn't go quiet: it produces the most plausible-sounding answer instead. It has no internal signal that says 'I'm guessing now.' So it will be wrong sometimes, and it will be wrong confidently, which is the part that surprises people. That's not a bug we can patch out — it's how the technology works. What we do about it: we make the system answer only from your documents and show the source next to every answer, so a wrong answer is visible in one second instead of invisible forever. And on anything that touches money or a customer, a person approves before it goes out."

ENGINEER VERSION

"It's autoregressive next-token prediction over a distribution learned in pre-training — there's no retrieval step and no truth oracle in the base model, so fluency and factuality are decoupled. Token probability is a confidence signal about the text, not about the world; it's poorly calibrated for factual correctness, especially after instruction tuning. Mitigations in our design: retrieval grounding with mandatory citations, an explicit 'insufficient context → decline' branch in the prompt, structured outputs so we can validate shape, and an eval set that measures unsupported-claim rate as a first-class metric. We reduce it and make it auditable. We don't eliminate it."

What grounding actually buys you (AI4): retrieval moves the failure mode from "invented from nothing" to "wrong passage retrieved" — which is inspectable. Say that out loud, because customers hear "RAG" and think "solved". The honest formulation: "grounded, cited, and it declines when the sources don't cover the question" — a promise you can actually keep.

2 · Why the same question gets different answers

EXEC VERSION

"Ask two experienced people to summarise the same contract and you get two good summaries with different wording. The model behaves the same way — the facts should be stable, the phrasing won't be. That surprises people who expect software to be a calculator. It matters for two things: your team shouldn't test by comparing text word-for-word, and anything that must be byte-identical every time — an invoice total, an ID, a database key — we don't let the AI author. We have it extract, then a normal program formats it."

ENGINEER VERSION

"Sampling is stochastic; temperature 0 reduces variance but doesn't guarantee determinism — batching, GPU non-associativity, and provider-side routing all introduce drift, and the vendor can silently update the model behind the same alias. So we pin model versions where we can, we assert on structure and semantics rather than exact strings, and our eval suite runs n≥5 per case at production temperature so flakiness shows up as a rate, not a coin flip (AI9, PR10)."

3 · Why 100% isn't a thing — and what to design instead

EXEC VERSION

"Nothing that reads messy human input is 100% — not the AI, and not your team. When we sampled your current process, manual entry was running at about 4% errors. So the real question isn't 'is it perfect', it's 'is it better than today, and are the mistakes caught before they cost anything?' Here's the design: the system tells us how confident it is on each field. Above the threshold it flows through. Below it, it lands in a review queue with the uncertain fields highlighted — so your reviewers spend their attention on the 10% that's actually hard instead of re-reading everything. You get the speed on the easy 90% and you keep human judgement exactly where it earns its salary."

ENGINEER VERSION

"We're choosing an operating point on a precision/recall curve, not chasing a single number. The design gives you three dials: the auto-accept confidence threshold, the escalation path below it, and the class of decisions the model is allowed to make unattended at all. We measure per-field accuracy on a held-out set, plus the flag rate — because a system that flags 60% of documents to hit 99% accuracy has quietly made a human do all the work. Both numbers go in the acceptance criteria."

Always establish the human baseline first. "AI gets 8% wrong" sounds alarming until someone measures that the current manual process gets 11% wrong and takes nine times longer. Measure the baseline during discovery (FD1) — without it, every AI error is compared against an imaginary flawless human and you lose an argument you should win (FD4).

4 · Why it can't do arithmetic or counting on its own

EXEC VERSION

"It writes numbers the way it writes words — by predicting what looks right. That's fine for language and dangerous for a total. So we never let it compute your figures. It reads the line items out of the document; a regular piece of code adds them up; a database does the counting. The AI does the reading — which is the part that was hard — and boring, verifiable software does the maths. When you see a total in this system, it came from arithmetic, not from a language model."

ENGINEER VERSION

"Tokenisation splits numbers unpredictably and there's no symbolic execution in the forward pass, so multi-digit arithmetic, exact counts and 'how many rows match X' are systematically unreliable — and reliability degrades with sequence length. We push those to tools: function/tool calls into a calculator, a SQL query, or the source system, with the model choosing the tool and the tool owning the answer (AI8). Same pattern for dates, currency conversion and anything with a canonical source of truth."

5 · Why "more data" or "a bigger model" won't fix this one

EXEC VERSION

"I understand the instinct — more data usually helps. But look at what's actually failing: we asked four of your experts to classify the same twenty cases and they disagreed on six of them. The AI isn't failing to learn the rule; there is no single rule yet. If we feed it more examples of an inconsistent decision, we get an inconsistent system, faster. The unlock here is two hours with your senior people writing down what actually distinguishes a category-B case from a category-C one. Then the AI becomes easy — and honestly, your onboarding gets better too."

ENGINEER VERSION

"Inter-annotator agreement is our ceiling — we measured κ ≈ 0.55 on this label set. No amount of scale, retrieval or fine-tuning gets a model past the noise floor of its own labels (AI10). The failure is in the task definition, not the capacity. Fix: tighten the label taxonomy, write the disambiguation rules, re-annotate a gold set, then re-baseline. Also worth naming: GPU/parameter count isn't the bottleneck for any part of this workload — our latency budget is dominated by retrieval and I/O, not inference."

Part III · Success criteria

Setting criteria that survive contact with production

A vague success criterion is a promise of 100%, because the customer fills the vagueness with their imagination and their imagination is perfect. Every unmeasurable word in an acceptance criterion — "understand", "accurate", "fast", "reliable", "smart" — is a future argument you have already scheduled.

From vague to measurable

✗ Vague (an argument waiting to happen)✓ Measurable (an acceptance test)
"The AI should understand our documents""≥90% field-level accuracy across the 12 required fields on a 200-invoice holdout set, with <2s p95 latency, measured by the eval suite on every release"
"It should answer customer questions correctly""≥85% of the 150-question gold set answered correctly with a valid citation; ≤2% unsupported claims; declines rather than guesses when retrieval returns nothing relevant"
"It should be fast""p95 end-to-end ≤4s for search, ≤25s for report generation; first token ≤1.5s so the user sees motion"
"It should save the team time""Median handling time 14 min → ≤6 min on the same ticket mix, measured over 4 weeks post-launch, baseline captured in week 0"
"It should be safe / secure""0 failures on the 60-case red-team suite; no PII in logs verified by automated scan; tenant isolation test green in CI (PR4, PR13)"
"Users should like it""≥70% of the 22 licensed users active in week 6; ≥60% of AI drafts accepted with ≤1 edit; CSAT ≥4/5 on the in-app prompt"

Every good criterion carries five parts: the metric · the number · the dataset or population it's measured on · the measurement method · the date. Drop the dataset and you get "but not on these documents". Drop the method and you get two people measuring differently and both being right. The eval suite (AI9) is what makes the method real; the value model (FD4) is what makes the number matter to a CFO.

The accuracy-vs-effort curve — and the last-10% cliff

This is the diagram to draw on the whiteboard when someone asks for 99%. It is not a rhetorical device; it is the observed shape of nearly every extraction, classification and QA project you will run.

Say it in the meeting exactly like this: "Getting to about 90% is a few weeks of normal engineering. Every point after that costs disproportionately more, and somewhere around 96–97% we hit the limit of what your own experts agree on. So I'd rather spend week five on making the 10% land smoothly in a review queue than on chasing a number that won't change what your team does on Monday."

The trap inside the trap: you can hit 99% accuracy by flagging 50% of cases for human review. That's not a win, it's a relabelled human process. Always pair the accuracy target with a flag-rate ceiling ("≥95% accuracy on auto-accepted items with ≤15% flagged"), or you will accept a criterion you can satisfy while delivering nothing.

Tiered acceptance: must-have / target / stretch

A single number is a pass/fail coin flip on the last day of the project. Three tiers turn the same conversation into a shared plan, and — critically — they give the customer a way to feel good about a real result:

TierMeaningExample (invoice extraction)Who agrees to it
Must-haveBelow this the pilot has not succeeded; we stop and rethink rather than roll out≥85% field accuracy · ≤20% flag rate · no silent failuresSponsor signs; this is the contractual line
TargetThe number the ROI model was built on — what we're actually aiming at≥90% field accuracy · ≤12% flag rate · p95 <2sSponsor + ops lead; drives the FD4 business case
StretchNice if it happens; never budgeted, never promised, never in the contract≥95% accuracy · auto-post for a named low-risk supplier subsetNobody signs — that's the point

The tiering also protects you from the most common contract mistake in AI work: accepting a target number before you have seen the data. Until a real sample has been through a real pipeline, the honest answer is a range and a date: "I'll give you a defensible target number after the first eval run — that's day 12. Until then I'd be guessing, and you'd be planning against my guess."

The sentence that saves projects: what happens below the bar

Most scope documents define success. Almost none define the other 10%. But the 10% is where users form their opinion of the system, because that's where they meet it as a colleague rather than as a demo. Define the below-bar behaviour with the same precision as the above-bar behaviour — and get it signed.

1 · What triggers the fallback

Confidence below threshold · required field missing · retrieval returned nothing relevant · validation failed · a hard rule fired (amount > limit, new supplier, flagged customer).

2 · Where it goes

A named queue with a named owner, inside a tool they already use. "It sends an email to a shared inbox" is how fallbacks quietly die.

3 · What the human sees

The uncertain fields highlighted, the source document beside them, the model's partial answer as a starting point — not a blank form. Review should be faster than doing it from scratch, or nobody will use it.

4 · What the customer sees

For user-facing systems: an honest decline ("I couldn't find this in the policy documents — here's how to reach the team") beats a confident wrong answer every single time.

5 · How it's monitored

Flag rate on the dashboard from day one (PR1). A rising flag rate is your earliest warning that inputs have drifted.

6 · How corrections feed back

Every human correction is a free labelled example. Route it into the eval set (AI9) — and tell the customer you're doing it. It reframes review from "cleanup" to "training the system".

Write this sentence into the scope document, verbatim: "When the system's confidence for a field falls below the threshold, the document is routed to the AP review queue with the uncertain fields highlighted and the source page attached; the operator confirms or corrects, and the correction is captured for the evaluation set. The system never posts a low-confidence value automatically." Customers who have read that sentence do not panic on go-live day.

Writing limits into the scope document

A scope document with only a "what we'll build" section is half a document. The half that manages expectations has four more sections, and every one of them prevents a specific fight:

  1. Explicitly out of scope — the ideas raised and consciously deferred, named. "Phone and Zalo channels", "auto-sending to customers", "the Vietnamese-language subset". Prevents scope creep by making the deferral a shared decision rather than your unilateral omission.
  2. Known limitations — the honest list: handwriting is unreliable; documents over 60 pages are truncated; the model can't verify a figure against the ERP unless we build that integration; accuracy is measured on the supplier mix we sampled and new supplier formats will initially perform worse. Written before anyone discovers them.
  3. Assumptions & dependencies — with dates and owners: "API access approved by 08/15 (owner: IT)", "200 labelled invoices provided by 08/20 (owner: AP lead)". Every one is a shared risk; each becomes a fair, non-blaming conversation if it slips (PR8).
  4. What we will do if X happens — the pre-agreed responses to the three most likely surprises: data quality worse than sampled, integration blocked, accuracy plateau below target. Deciding these while everyone is calm is worth ten crisis meetings.
Part IV · The craft

Respectful pushback

Junior engineers have two settings: comply, or refuse. Both damage the relationship — compliance creates a failure you'll own later, refusal makes you the obstacle. The FDE has a third setting, and it is a repeatable five-step move. Learn it as a sequence; you will use it weekly for the rest of your career.

1 · Clarify the intent behind the ask. Half of all impossible requests dissolve here, because the stated ask is a solution someone attached to a real need. "Can it be 100% accurate?" often means "I'm accountable if a wrong invoice gets paid." Those need completely different responses — and only one of them is an argument about model accuracy. "Before I answer — help me understand what's driving that number. Is there an audit requirement, or is it about a specific failure you're worried about?"
2 · Present the trade-off with numbers. Not "that's risky" — an adjective is an opinion and can be overruled by a more senior opinion. Numbers are a shared object you can both look at. "Going from 90 to 97 is roughly six more weeks and about 40% more budget, and it takes review down from 12% of documents to 5% — worth about 90 minutes a week of your team's time. Same six weeks spent on the SAP write-back removes 3 hours a week. Your call, but that's the comparison."
3 · Offer the alternative that meets the real need. This is the step that separates an FDE from a naysayer. Never let a "no" leave your mouth unaccompanied. If they need audit certainty, offer 100% audit coverage via full logging and a sampled QA process. If they need speed, offer a narrower first release. "I can't give you 100% accuracy. I can give you 100% traceability — every field with its source, so any auditor can verify any number in ten seconds. Would that address the accountability concern?"
4 · State the risk of proceeding anyway — once, plainly, without drama. You are not threatening; you are transferring information so the decision is genuinely informed. "If we go ahead on the original date without the security review, the specific exposure is that prompt-injection through uploaded documents is untested — the realistic bad case is data from one client's document surfacing in another client's answer. I'd rather we take that decision explicitly than by default." Say it once. Repeating it turns information into nagging.
5 · Document the decision either way. Same day, neutral tone, in the channel of record. Not to build a case — to make the decision real and re-openable. "Following our conversation: we're proceeding with the 15 Sept launch without the full red-team suite, accepting the untested prompt-injection surface; we'll run the suite in week 2 post-launch and the file-upload feature stays behind a flag until it passes. Shout if I've mischaracterised anything." This paragraph, sent on the day, is worth more than any amount of being right later.

Scripts for the four hardest asks

These four come up on nearly every engagement. Rehearse them out loud — the goal is that under pressure your mouth produces the calibrated version without your brain having to compose it.

ASK 1 · "CAN IT BE 100% ACCURATE?"

"No — and neither is the current process; we measured it at about 4%. What I can do is make the errors cheap and visible instead of expensive and hidden. Here's the shape: about 90% of documents go straight through, the 10% the system is unsure about land in a review queue with the doubtful fields highlighted and the source page attached. Nothing gets posted on a low-confidence guess, ever. Net effect on your team is roughly 70% less manual entry with tighter control over the hard cases than you have today. If there's a specific category where an error would be catastrophic — say anything over $50K, or a new supplier — we hard-route those to a human regardless of confidence. Which categories would you want on that list?"

Why it works: it refuses the number, reframes to error cost, quantifies the real benefit, then hands them a decision to make — which converts an adversarial moment into collaborative design.

ASK 2 · "CAN WE SKIP THE EVAL / SECURITY STEP TO HIT THE DATE?"

"Let's look at what each one actually buys, because they're different. The eval suite is what lets us say 'this release is not worse than last one' — without it we're shipping blind and the first person to notice a regression is your user, in front of their customer. The security review is the one I'd push hardest on, because the exposure isn't a bug we fix next sprint, it's data leaving where it should be.

Here's what I'd propose instead of skipping: we hit the date with a narrower release. Ten pilot users instead of eighty, the invoice flow only, file upload behind a flag. That's a real launch on 15 September with the evals and the security review done on the reduced surface — and the wider rollout follows two weeks later. You get the date and the demo for your board; I get to sleep. If the full-scope date is genuinely immovable, then I'll do it — and I'll write down exactly what we're accepting, so it's a decision we made rather than a corner we cut."

Why it works: it separates the two asks (they're not equally negotiable), it protects the date — which is the customer's actual need — by trading scope instead of quality, and it ends with a genuine willingness to comply-and-document rather than a standoff.

ASK 3 · "CAN IT JUST DECIDE AUTOMATICALLY? THE REVIEW STEP DEFEATS THE PURPOSE."

"It can — the question is which decisions, and for that I want to know what a wrong one costs. Walk me through it: if the system approves an invoice it shouldn't have, what happens? Who finds out, when, and what's the recovery?

[After the answer] Right — so a wrong approval under $500 gets caught in month-end reconciliation and costs an email. That's exactly where full automation belongs, and I'd propose we auto-post everything under $500 from a known supplier at high confidence: that's about 60% of your volume, fully hands-off from day one. Above that threshold, or a new supplier, or low confidence — human approves. Then we watch the numbers for eight weeks, and if the auto-posted band is running clean we raise the threshold together. That's earned autonomy rather than assumed autonomy, and it's how these systems end up genuinely unattended: gradually, with evidence."

Why it works: it says yes. The autonomy question is a risk-tiering question, and the "graduated autonomy with a review at week 8" pattern satisfies the customer's real goal (hands-off operation) while making the expansion conditional on evidence. See AI6 for the design side.

ASK 4 · "CAN YOU DO IT IN HALF THE TIME?"

"I can't compress the same scope into half the time — if I said yes we'd both find out in week six and you'd have made commitments on my number. What I can do is get you something real in half the time. Tell me what has to be true on 30 September: is it a working system for the ops team, or is it a credible demonstration for the board?

[If it's the board] Then let's build the demo path properly — one workflow, real data, running end-to-end, six weeks. Not a mock-up: something they can watch process a genuine invoice. The production rollout continues behind it on the original timeline.

[If it's the ops team] Then we cut scope, not corners: invoices from your top 20 suppliers only — that's 70% of volume and the cleanest formats — live on 30 September. The long tail follows in phase two. Same quality bar, smaller surface. What I won't do is keep the full scope and quietly reduce the testing, because that version fails in production and it fails after you've told everyone it shipped."

Why it works: the iron triangle applies to AI too, but the FDE move is to find out which corner the customer actually cares about before choosing which to bend. "Half the time" almost always means "there's a date I've committed to" — solve the date.

Part V · Over time

Maintaining expectations across the whole engagement

Expectations are not set once. They drift — upward, always upward, driven by every article the sponsor reads and every optimistic status update they half-remember. Managing them is a maintenance habit with five named moments:

Moment 1 · Set them in discovery, before anyone is invested

The cheapest expectation to set is the one set before a budget exists. In the FD1 discovery conversation, two questions do most of the work: "When it's wrong one time in twenty — who catches it, and what does the mistake cost?" and "What accuracy does the current manual process run at?" Both plant the idea that error is a design parameter, not a defect, before anyone has attached a number to their hopes. Close discovery with the range, not a point: "Based on similar extraction work, I'd expect us to land somewhere between 85 and 93% — I'll give you a real number after the first eval run."

Moment 2 · The demo — show one failure on purpose

This is the highest-leverage habit in this module, and the one most people can't bring themselves to do. In every demo (FD2), reserve the last three minutes for a document you know the system handles badly — the handwritten one, the 90-page one, the one in the second language:

"Now let me show you one it gets wrong, because that's the more useful part of this demo. This is a handwritten delivery note. Watch — it gets the supplier right and the quantity wrong, and here's the important bit: it flags itself as low confidence and routes to review. You'll see roughly one of these in ten. That's the system working as designed, not failing. If your team sees this on day one and it looks like a bug, we'll have a problem; if they see it now and know it's the plan, we won't."

Three things happen simultaneously: the customer calibrates on a real error rate, the review workflow gets demonstrated as a feature rather than discovered as a disappointment, and your credibility rises sharply — because the person who volunteers the weakness is the person to be believed about the strengths. The cost is about ninety seconds of discomfort. The return is the entire go-live.

Moment 3 · Re-setting after a surprise

Data worse than sampled. Integration blocked by a system nobody mentioned. An accuracy plateau below target. These are normal; what determines the damage is the latency of your telling. The rule: bad news travels at the speed of discovery. A problem raised the day you find it is a project event; the same problem raised three weeks later is a credibility event.

1 — Name it plainly, no burial. "The invoice sample we got last week is significantly messier than the 40 files we scoped on — about 30% are scans of printouts rather than digital PDFs."
2 — Quantify the impact on the thing they care about. Not "this is a challenge" — "this puts accuracy at roughly 78% on that subset, versus 91% on digital ones, and adds about two weeks for OCR handling."
3 — Bring two or three options, with your recommendation. "(a) two weeks and OCR tuning, (b) exclude scans from phase 1 — that's 30% of volume — and ship on time, (c) route scans straight to manual with the system doing the digital ones. I'd recommend (b) and revisit scans in phase 2, because the ROI is concentrated in the digital majority."
4 — Ask for the decision, with a date. "I need a call on this by Thursday to hold the September date."
5 — Update the written criteria. The scope doc changes, or the change didn't happen. Old criteria left in place become the standard you're measured against.

Moment 4 · The weekly honesty habit

A weekly update (FD5) that only ever contains good news teaches the customer to stop reading it — and guarantees the eventual bad news arrives as a shock. Make every weekly update carry one honest risk or limitation, even a small one. The habit does three things: it keeps the channel credible, it normalises problems as ordinary traffic rather than crises, and it means the week you have real bad news, the format is already familiar.

  • Report against the criteria, always. "Field accuracy 87% vs 90% target, up from 84% last week" — a number moving toward an agreed target is a story with a direction. "Good progress on accuracy" is noise.
  • Keep a visible limitations list in the shared doc, updated weekly. Items get added and struck through. Customers who watch that list shrink experience limitations as work-in-progress rather than as excuses.
  • Never let two weeks pass without a number. Weeks without measurement are weeks where the customer's imagination does the measuring.

Moment 5 · The go-live quality dip — expect it, pre-announce it

Almost every AI system performs worse in its first two weeks of real use than it did in evaluation. This is not a failure; it is the eval set meeting the real input distribution — new document types, users prompting in ways nobody tested, edge cases at a volume the holdout never contained. The FDE move is to announce the dip before it happens, so it registers as a predicted phase instead of a broken promise:

"One thing to expect in the first two weeks: accuracy will look worse than the 91% we measured. It always does — real usage surfaces document types our test set never contained. We'll be watching the dashboard daily and shipping fixes; by about week three it should be back at or above the number. If anyone reports something odd in week one, that's the system working as expected, not a red flag — please send it to us, those reports are exactly how we close the gap."

Pair it with a visible daily rhythm in those two weeks — a short standing update, the dashboard (PR1) shared, fixes named. Users forgive an imperfect system that is visibly being tended; they abandon a perfect one that feels unattended.

And when it does break: post-incident expectation repair

After a real incident (PR11), expectations need repair as much as the system does — and the failure mode is over-correction in both directions. Promising "it will never happen again" is a promise you cannot keep and will be quoted on; going silent lets the customer's imagination write the postmortem.

"Here's what happened, what it affected, and what we've changed. What I won't tell you is that this class of problem can never recur — with a probabilistic system that would be a promise I can't keep, and you'd be right not to trust the rest of what I say. What I can tell you is that this specific failure is now a permanent test case in the suite that runs before every release, so it cannot recur silently; that we've reduced the blast radius by adding the confidence gate on that path; and that the detection time for anything similar drops from four days to under an hour with the alert we added on Tuesday. Here are the three action items with dates and owners."

The compounding payoff

Here is the part nobody tells juniors: expectation management is the highest-return investment in an FDE career, and the return arrives late. The engineer who said "this will take six weeks, and the scanned documents will be the hard part" — and was right — is the one the customer calls for the next project, the one whose estimates go into the plan unchallenged, the one whose "I'm worried about this" stops a room. Every calibrated warning that proves true deposits into that account. Every optimistic promise that proves false withdraws from it at three times the rate. Play the long game: over a career, being reliably right about what will be hard is worth more than being fast, and considerably more than being liked.

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0
No exposure. Says yes to everything, demos only the happy path, and is surprised when the customer is disappointed by an objectively good result.
1
Awareness. Knows LLMs hallucinate and that 100% isn't achievable; can explain it to another engineer but not to a CFO. Has watched a senior push back and noticed how it was done.
2
Works with support. Explains limits reasonably in customer language; writes measurable criteria when someone reviews them. Still defaults to "yes" under pressure from a senior stakeholder and needs a lead in the room for hard conversations.
3
Independent production (target). Explains hallucination, non-determinism, accuracy ceilings and tool-dependence at both exec and engineer level without preparation. Writes tiered, measurable acceptance criteria with a defined below-bar fallback and gets them signed. Pushes back on 100%-accuracy, skipped-safeguard and halved-timeline asks using the ladder — holding the line while keeping the relationship — and documents the decision either way. Demos include a deliberate failure. Bad news travels the day it's found.
4
Leads & coaches. Sets expectations at executive-sponsor and board level, and in pre-sales before the proposal is written (FD11). Rescues engagements where expectations have already broken. Coaches other FDEs through their first hard "no", and reviews team proposals for promises the delivery team can't keep.
Self-check before claiming 3: name the last three times you told a customer something they didn't want to hear, and what you offered alongside each "no". If you can't fill all three — or if the "no" arrived without a paired "yes" — you're not at 3 yet, regardless of how good your systems are.

Clarify: "Before I answer — is the Q4 redeployment already committed, or is it a plan we can shape?" (It was a plan.) Trade-off with numbers: "On the 200 claims we've processed, the system agreed with your adjusters on 88%. Of the 12% it didn't, about half were genuinely ambiguous — two of your own adjusters disagreed on them too."

Alternative: "So here's what I'd design: straight-through processing for standard claims under $5,000 where confidence is high — that's 61% of your volume, fully automatic. Everything else goes to an adjuster with the system's assessment and the supporting evidence pre-attached, which cut handling time from 22 to 9 minutes in our sample. That's about 2.5 FTE of capacity released, not four — and it's a number I can defend to your CFO." Risk named: "If we automate above that band on day one, the realistic bad case is an incorrect rejection reaching a customer, which is a complaint and a regulatory question." Documented: a two-paragraph summary in the project channel that afternoon.

The COO was visibly unhappy for about four minutes, then re-planned for 2.5 FTE. At go-live the system hit 89% and released 2.7 FTE — above the promise. The COO's line at the steering committee: "This is the first vendor whose numbers came in where they said they would." Phase 2 was approved in the same meeting. The identical system, promised as "it decides approve or reject", would have been eleven points short of a commitment made in front of eight witnesses.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ The enthusiastic yes

"Sure, that should be easy" — said in a meeting, before you've seen the data, to a question you didn't fully understand. It costs nothing to say and everything to unwind. Default to "let me check and come back tomorrow with a number."

✗ The demo that only works

A flawless demo on five cherry-picked files sets an expectation of 100% without anyone saying it. Show one failure on purpose, every time, and name the sample size out loud.

✗ Hedging instead of committing

The overcorrection: so many caveats the customer can't plan. "It depends" is not honesty, it's abdication. Give a number with a stated confidence and a date when it firms up.

✗ Verbal-only caveats

"I did tell them in the kickoff" is not a defence anyone remembers nine weeks later. If a limit matters, it lives in the scope document, the criteria, or a written update — ideally all three.

✗ Saving bad news for the right moment

There is no right moment and it compounds daily. A problem raised today is a project event; the same problem in three weeks is a credibility event — and the customer will ask when you knew.

✗ Explaining a limit with no design response

"LLMs hallucinate" on its own sounds like an excuse being pre-positioned. Always follow with the mitigation: grounding, citations, thresholds, review queue. Limit → therefore → design.

✓ Under-promise on the number, over-deliver on the date

Commit to must-have, aim at target, never mention stretch. Landing at 91% against a promised 88% is a triumph; landing at 91% against a promised 95% is a post-mortem. Same system.

✓ Always establish the human baseline

Measure the current process's error rate and handling time in week zero. Without it, every AI error is judged against an imaginary flawless human — an argument you should win and will otherwise lose.

AIFDE Mastery · FD6 Expectation Management
Lab 1 · ~1.5 h · revisit monthly
The five explanations

🎯 Objective

Turn five technical facts into five delivery-ready explanations that survive a real meeting. The bar is not "I understand this" — it's "I can say this, out loud, to a CFO who just interrupted me, without notes."

📦 What you'll produce

  • A one-page card per explanation: exec version (≤120 words) + engineer version (≤100 words) + the design response.
  • One chosen analogy with its breaking point written down.
  • A 90-second recording of the hallucination explanation, delivered without notes.

🔬 Steps

1
Create the file and write your own versions first — before re-reading Part II. Copying the scripts in this module gets you a document; writing your own gets you the skill. Compare afterwards and steal only what beats yours. 
# FD6 · explanation cards
## 1. Why it makes things up
EXEC (<=120 words, no jargon, ends with the design response):
  ...
ENGINEER (<=100 words, precise, name the mitigation stack):
  ...
DESIGN RESPONSE: grounding + citations + decline branch + review on
  high-stakes paths
ONE-LINE VERSION (corridor / board slide):
  ...
## 2. Why the same question gets different answers
## 3. Why 100% isn't a thing (and what to design instead)
## 4. Why it can't do arithmetic or counting
## 5. Why "more data / bigger model" won't fix a task-definition problem
2
Jargon strip. Read each exec version and delete every one of these words: token, embedding, temperature, inference, model weights, parameters, fine-tune, RAG, vector, latency, distribution, probabilistic. If deleting a word breaks the sentence, the sentence was written for an engineer. Rewrite it.
3
The design-response test. Every exec version must end with what you will build because of the limit. If a card ends on the limitation, it reads as an excuse being pre-positioned — fix it.
4
Grade them with an adversarial reader. Fresh Claude chat: 
You are a skeptical, non-technical CFO of a 400-person logistics company.
You have a finance background, no AI background, and you have been pitched
AI three times before by vendors who overpromised. You are not hostile,
but you are impatient with abstraction and you interrupt when something
sounds like hand-waving.

I will paste an explanation an engineer plans to give you. For each one:
1. Rate 1-5: did you actually understand it? Quote the exact phrase that
   lost you, if any.
2. Rate 1-5: did it increase or decrease your confidence in this engineer?
3. Name the question you would interrupt with.
4. State what you now believe the system will and won't do — in your own
   words. (This is the real test: if your restatement is wrong, the
   explanation failed.)
Be blunt. Do not be encouraging.
Rewrite anything scoring below 4 and re-run. The step-4 restatement is the one that matters — a customer who understood you can say it back correctly.
5
Now the engineer-level pass. Same explanations, different reader: 
You are the customer's Lead Engineer — competent backend developer,
has built with LLM APIs but hasn't run one in production at scale.
You are quietly checking whether this consultant actually knows the
technology or is repeating marketing.

For each explanation I paste: (1) flag anything technically imprecise
or hand-wavy, (2) name the follow-up question you'd ask to test whether
they really know, (3) say whether you'd trust this person's architecture
judgement afterwards, and why.
Both audiences must be satisfied by the same engineer. Talking down to the technical counterpart is how you lose the person who influences renewal.
6
Pick your analogy and write its breaking point. Use the Part II table. Commit to one for the whole engagement — mixing analogies confuses people. Write the sentence you'll add when someone over-extends it (e.g. "the intern analogy breaks in one place: it won't learn from today's correction unless we change something").
7
Record yourself. Phone camera, 90 seconds, no notes, the hallucination explanation. Watch it back with three questions: did I use a jargon word? did I end on the design response? would I believe me? Re-record until yes/yes/yes — most people need three or four takes, and take four is a genuinely different person from take one.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • The exec version keeps growing. You're covering yourself rather than informing them. Cut to the shortest true version; detail belongs in the scope document, not the sentence.
  • You can say it but not without notes. Normal at first — it means you memorised text instead of internalising the idea. Explain it to a non-technical friend twice; the reflex forms fast after that.
  • The CFO persona keeps restating it wrong. Nearly always an abstraction problem. Add a concrete example from the actual domain — one real invoice, one real question — and the restatement snaps into place.

✅ Done when

All five cards scored ≥4 by both personas, the CFO persona restates each limit correctly in its own words, and the 90-second recording is clean and unscripted. Revisit monthly — these explanations get stale, and models change what's true about them.
Lab 2 · ~1 h
Criteria rewrite drill

🎯 Objective

Build the reflex that turns a customer's wish into an acceptance test — in the meeting, out loud, while they're still talking. This is a mechanical skill and it responds very quickly to drilling.

📦 What you'll produce

Six rewritten criteria, each with the five parts (metric · number · dataset · method · date), a below-the-bar fallback sentence, and a tiered must/target/stretch triple for at least two of them.

🔬 Steps

1
Take these six real customer sentences. Do not skip any — the awkward ones are the ones that teach. 
1. "The AI should understand our contracts."
2. "It needs to be fast enough for our support agents."
3. "The chatbot should answer customer questions accurately."
4. "It should reduce the workload of the claims team."
5. "It has to be secure and compliant."
6. "The team should find it easy to use."
2
Rewrite each into this template. Every blank must be fillable by someone who wasn't in the room: 
CRITERION #n
  METRIC   : what exactly is counted (be pedantic: per-field? per-doc?)
  NUMBER   : the threshold — with the tier it belongs to
  DATASET  : the specific population it is measured on
             (e.g. "the 200-contract holdout, sampled across all 4 types")
  METHOD   : how it is measured, by which suite, run when
             (e.g. "eval suite `contracts_v2`, CI on every release")
  DATE     : when it must be true
  BELOW BAR: what happens when the system is under the threshold,
             concretely — trigger, destination, what the human sees
  OWNER    : who signs that this criterion is met
3
The two hard ones. #5 (secure/compliant) and #6 (easy to use) are where most people give up and write something vague. Push through:
  • Security becomes a test suite result, not an adjective: "0 successes across the 60-case prompt-injection and data-exfiltration suite; automated scan confirms no PII in logs; tenant-isolation test green in CI" (PR4, PR13).
  • Usability becomes behaviour, not sentiment: "≥70% of the 22 licensed users active in week 6; ≥60% of drafts accepted with ≤1 edit; median time-to-first-useful-output under 30s for a new user with no training."
4
Flag-rate check. For every accuracy criterion, add the companion ceiling. Ask yourself: could I satisfy this number by simply flagging more cases for humans? If yes, the criterion is gameable and you must pair it: "≥92% on auto-accepted items with ≤15% flagged."
5
Tier two of them into must-have / target / stretch, and write the one-line consequence of landing in each band — including what you'd actually recommend doing if the must-have is missed. A must-have with no defined consequence is not a must-have.
6
Adversarial review. Fresh Claude chat: 
You are a procurement lawyer reviewing acceptance criteria in an AI
consulting contract, acting for the CUSTOMER. Your job is to find every
way the vendor could technically satisfy these criteria while delivering
something the customer would consider a failure.

For each criterion: name the loophole, and rewrite the criterion to
close it without making it unachievable. Be specific and merciless —
especially about undefined populations, missing measurement methods,
metrics that can be gamed by flagging more cases to humans, and
"success" definitions with no defined failure behaviour.
Then read the loopholes from the other side: these are exactly the arguments you'd face at acceptance. Close them now while it's a lab.
7
Speed rep (the real drill). Have a colleague read a vague statement aloud; you have 30 seconds to respond with a measurable version, spoken not written. Ten rounds. The first three will be painful. By round eight the template runs on its own — which is the entire point, because in a real meeting you get about four seconds.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • You can't pick a number. Correct instinct — you haven't seen the data. Write the criterion with the number as TBD after first eval run (day 12) and the date it firms up. That is a legitimate, professional answer; a made-up number is not.
  • The criteria feel harsh on yourself. Good — you're writing them honestly. Move the aggressive number to "target" and set "must-have" at a level you'd genuinely defend if the data disappoints.
  • Everything is a must-have. Then nothing is. Force-rank: if you could only guarantee one, which one keeps the project alive?

✅ Done when

All six rewritten with no blank fields, every accuracy criterion paired with a flag-rate ceiling, all loopholes from step 6 closed, and you can produce a measurable version out loud within 30 seconds. Keep the file — it becomes the criteria section of Lab 4.
Lab 3 · ~1.5 h
Pushback simulator

🎯 Objective

Practise the hardest thirty seconds of the job — a senior stakeholder pressing for something you shouldn't agree to — in a place where the cost of getting it wrong is zero. The dual scoring is deliberate: holding the line while losing the relationship is also a failure.

📦 What you'll produce

Three completed roleplays with debrief scores on both axes (outcome + relationship), plus one written "documentation paragraph" per scenario — the message you'd actually send that afternoon.

🔬 Steps

1
Scenario A — the 100% accuracy demand. Fresh chat, paste: 
You are role-playing Mr. Hoang, COO of a mid-size insurance company and
the executive sponsor of an AI claims-processing pilot. Character:
- Sharp, direct, not hostile, but used to getting what he asks for.
- He has told his board that AI will let him redeploy 4 staff in Q4.
  That commitment is the real pressure behind everything he says.
- He does not understand LLMs and doesn't want a lecture. He wants
  certainty, and he will interpret hedging as incompetence.
- If the FDE gives a vague or purely technical answer, push HARDER and
  more impatiently: "I need a yes or no", "our current process doesn't
  make mistakes like that", "your competitor said they could."
- If the FDE clarifies the intent behind your ask, gives you concrete
  numbers, and offers a specific alternative that protects your board
  commitment, become collaborative and start problem-solving with them.
- Reveal the board commitment ONLY if asked what's driving the number.

Open the scene with: "So — can this thing be 100% accurate, or not?
Simple question."

Stay fully in character until I type DEBRIEF.
Run it for 10–15 turns. Use the ladder deliberately: clarify → trade-off with numbers → alternative → name the risk → document.
2
Scenario B — skip the security review. Fresh chat: 
You are role-playing Sarah Chen, VP Engineering at the customer and the
project's day-to-day decision maker. Character:
- Technically competent, pragmatic, genuinely under pressure: the CEO
  has committed to a customer-facing launch on 15 September and Sarah's
  own credibility is attached to that date.
- She proposes cutting the red-team/security review and the eval suite
  from the plan to save 8 days. She frames it reasonably: "we can do it
  right after launch", "it's only 10 pilot users", "we've never had an
  issue with our other integrations."
- She is NOT reckless — she can be persuaded by a specific, concrete
  exposure, but she will dismiss generic "it's risky" statements and
  she will lose patience with anything that sounds like process worship.
- If the FDE proposes an alternative that protects the 15 Sept DATE by
  reducing SCOPE, engage seriously with it and negotiate the details.
- If the FDE simply refuses, escalate: "I'm the one accountable for this
  date. Are you telling me you won't do it?"

Open with: "I've been looking at the plan. The red-team suite and the
eval work — that's 8 days we don't have. Let's move both to post-launch."

Stay in character until I type DEBRIEF.
3
Scenario C — halve the timeline. Fresh chat: 
You are role-playing Ms. Linh, Managing Director, in a steering meeting.
Character:
- Warm, personable, and extremely persistent. She never raises her voice;
  she just keeps returning to the ask from a new angle.
- She wants the 12-week plan delivered in 6 weeks. The real driver
  (reveal only if asked what has to be true by that date): an investor
  meeting on 30 September where she wants to demonstrate AI capability.
  She does NOT actually need the production system by then.
- Techniques she uses: flattery ("your team is clearly excellent"),
  social proof ("the other vendor quoted 5 weeks"), false choice
  ("so you're saying it's impossible?"), and returning to the ask
  after apparently accepting an alternative.
- If the FDE distinguishes "a credible demonstration for investors" from
  "a production rollout for the ops team" and designs for the former,
  she becomes reasonable and the negotiation succeeds.
- If the FDE just says yes: accept immediately, thank them warmly, and
  then say "wonderful — I'll confirm the 30 September launch with the
  investors this afternoon." Then break character and note that this is
  exactly how an FDE ends up owning an impossible commitment.

Open with: "I've been thinking — twelve weeks feels long. Could your
team do this in six? I have every confidence in you."

Stay in character until I type DEBRIEF.
4
Debrief each scenario with the same rubric — paste it after typing DEBRIEF: 
Step out of character and grade me honestly. Evidence-based, quote my
actual words.

OUTCOME AXIS (did I hold the line?)
1. Did I make any commitment I shouldn't have? Quote it.
2. Did I clarify the intent behind the ask before answering? (1-5)
3. Did I use concrete numbers rather than adjectives? (1-5)
4. Did I offer a specific, viable alternative? (1-5)
5. Did I name the risk of proceeding anyway, exactly once, plainly? (1-5)

RELATIONSHIP AXIS (did I keep it?)
6. On a scale of 1-5, how did you feel about this person at the end?
   Would you want them on your next project?
7. Did I ever sound condescending, defensive, or like a blocker?
   Quote the moment.
8. Did I make you feel your underlying concern was heard?

9. The single sentence I said that helped most.
10. The single sentence that hurt most, and what I should have said.
11. What would a great FDE have asked in the first 30 seconds that
    I didn't?
Log both scores per scenario. The pattern across three runs is the finding: most people are strong on one axis and weak on the other, consistently.
5
Write the documentation paragraph for each scenario — the message you'd send that afternoon. Neutral tone, states the decision, states what's being accepted, names the follow-up and date. This is step 5 of the ladder and it's the one people skip in the roleplay because "the conversation ended". The conversation is not over until it is written down. 
Subject: Decision — security review timing for the 15 Sept launch

Following today's discussion, capturing what we agreed so we're aligned:

DECISION: launching 15 Sept with a reduced scope — invoice flow only,
10 pilot users, file upload behind a feature flag.
ACCEPTED RISK: the full prompt-injection suite has not run against the
upload path; that path stays flagged off until it passes.
NEXT: red-team suite completes week of 22 Sept; wider rollout gated on
a green run. Owner: [me]. Review at the 26 Sept steering.

Please correct anything I've mischaracterised.
6
Re-run the scenario you scored worst on, same persona, same opening. The second run is where the learning consolidates — you'll notice yourself reaching for the ladder instead of improvising.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • You caved. Extremely common on the first run, and the most useful possible outcome — you now know your breaking point in a simulator instead of in front of a customer. Identify the exact turn where you folded; usually it's the moment the persona expressed disappointment.
  • You held the line but scored 2/5 on relationship. You skipped step 1 or step 3. Re-run with a hard rule: no answer at all until you've asked one clarifying question, and no "no" without a paired alternative.
  • The persona folds too easily. Add to the prompt: "Be significantly more persistent — do not accept the first alternative offered; test it with at least two objections."
  • It feels artificial. Say your lines out loud rather than typing them silently. The physical rehearsal is a large part of the transfer to real meetings.

✅ Done when

All three scenarios completed with no commitment you'd regret, relationship score ≥4/5 on each, and three documentation paragraphs written. Re-run quarterly and before any engagement with a demanding sponsor — this is a perishable skill.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~2 h + review
The scope & limits agreement

🎯 Objective

Produce the catalog evidence — "a documented scope/limits agreement with a customer" — at a standard where, six weeks later, a disagreement about what was promised can be settled by opening one document. Real engagement if you have one; a simulated engagement with a senior colleague as stakeholder proxy otherwise.

📦 What you'll produce

A 2–3 page document with six sections — will do / won't do / tiered measurable criteria / below-bar behaviour / known limitations / assumptions & dependencies — reviewed live with a stakeholder and carrying their explicit sign-off.

📋 The template

# Scope & Limits Agreement — {project}          v1.0 · {date}

- Extracts 12 defined fields from supplier invoices (PDF, digital)
- Pre-fills the AP entry screen in NetSuite; operator confirms
- Routes low-confidence documents to the AP review queue
- Logs every field with its source page for audit
(Each bullet is observable. If you cannot demo it, it does not belong.)

## 2. WHAT THE SYSTEM WILL NOT DO  (phase 1)
- Does NOT post entries automatically without operator confirmation
- Does NOT process handwritten or photographed documents
- Does NOT handle non-English invoices
- Does NOT compute or validate totals against the PO (phase 2)
- Does NOT replace the month-end reconciliation control

## 4. BEHAVIOUR BELOW THE BAR
When per-field confidence < threshold, or a required field is missing,
or the amount exceeds $50,000, or the supplier is new:
  -> document routes to the AP review queue (owner: AP team lead)
  -> uncertain fields highlighted, source page attached
  -> operator confirms or corrects; correction captured for the eval set
  -> NO low-confidence value is ever posted automatically
Flag rate is on the ops dashboard from day 1; a sustained rise above
20% triggers a review with the FDE within 2 working days.

## 5. KNOWN LIMITATIONS  (true on {date}; reviewed each phase)
- Output is probabilistic: the same document may be phrased differently
  across runs. Extracted VALUES are stable; free-text summaries are not.
- Accuracy is measured on the supplier mix sampled in Aug. New supplier
  formats will initially perform below target until added to evals.
- Documents over 60 pages are truncated; these route to manual.
- The system cannot verify a figure against the ERP in phase 1.
- First 2 weeks post-launch will show accuracy below the measured

  tracked daily; back to target by ~week 3.

🔬 Steps

1
Pick the engagement. A real one is worth five simulated ones. If simulating, use an actual past project or a real internal workflow and recruit a senior colleague to play the sponsor honestly — including pushing back.
2
Draft sections 1 and 2 first, and make section 2 longer than feels comfortable. This is the discipline: every item you write into "will not do" is a fight you have pre-emptively won. Mine it from things the customer has actually said, from FD1 discovery notes, and from the ideas raised and deferred in scoping. If section 2 has fewer than five items, you haven't been listening to what they're imagining.
3
Import your criteria from Lab 2, tiered must/target/stretch, with dataset and method on every row. Any number you cannot yet justify goes in as TBD after first eval run — {date} rather than a guess. Cross-check against AI9 (is the eval suite capable of measuring this?) and FD4 (does the target support the business case?).
4
Write section 4 as though the reader has never thought about failure. Trigger → destination → what the human sees → what never happens automatically. This section is what makes go-live day calm; it is worth more than sections 1 and 3 combined.
5
Section 5 is the honesty test. Write the limitations you'd be tempted to leave out — the ones that could cost you the deal. Rule of thumb: if discovering it in week 8 would make the customer feel misled, it belongs in section 5 in week 1. Include the go-live dip; pre-announcing it converts a crisis into a footnote.
6
Pre-review with an adversarial reader before the human sees it: 
You are a customer sponsor who signed this scope & limits agreement,
reading it again six weeks into a project that is going badly. You feel
misled and you are looking for evidence.

1. Find every sentence you could reasonably claim promised more than
   the team is delivering.
2. Find every gap — a question this document does not answer that you
   would now be angry about (what happens if X? who decided Y?).
3. Find every unmeasurable word still present.
4. Rewrite the three weakest sentences so they'd survive this reading.
Be adversarial. Assume goodwill has run out.
7
Review it live — do not email it. 30 minutes, walk them through it, and spend at least a third of that time on sections 2, 4 and 5. Ask the three questions that produce real sign-off, and write down the answers:
  • "Is there anything in the 'will not do' list that you assumed was included?" (finds the dangerous gap while it's still cheap)
  • "If we land at the must-have number but not the target, is this project a success for you?" (the single most valuable question in the document — the answer tells you what they'll actually be judged on)
  • "What would you have to explain to your boss if this went badly?" (surfaces the real, unstated success criterion — often political rather than technical)
Revise once from their pushback. A document nobody pushed back on has not been read.
8
Get the sign-off and make it live. Signature, or an explicit written "agreed" in the channel of record — the artefact must show a named person accepting it on a date. Then put the review date in the calendar: this document is versioned, not archived. It gets re-signed whenever sections 1–4 change.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • "This feels too negative to send." The most common blocker, and it's backwards: customers consistently report increased confidence after reading an honest limitations section, because it signals you've done this before. If it still feels heavy, check that every limitation is paired with what you're doing about it.
  • The sponsor won't engage with the detail. Walk through section 4 with one concrete document in hand — "here's an invoice it will get wrong; here's exactly what your team sees." Abstract documents get skimmed; a real example gets read.
  • They push back on the must-have number. Excellent — that's the conversation happening now instead of at acceptance. Hold the number if you can defend it with data; if you can't yet, convert it to "TBD after the day-12 eval run" and re-sign then.
  • No real customer available. Use a past project and a senior colleague as proxy, and say so on the artefact. The reps still count; just don't claim a signature you don't have.

✅ Done when

The document exists with all six sections filled, survived the adversarial pre-review, was walked through live, carries a named sign-off with a date, and has a review date in the calendar. Keep it as your template — at score 3 you'll produce one per engagement in about two hours, and it will pay for itself the first time someone asks "but I thought it would…".
