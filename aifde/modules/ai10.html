Fine-tuning & Model Adaptation

Decide prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning; LoRA/PEFT, dataset preparation, distillation. The skill here is 90% judgment and 10% training scripts — knowing when the answer is "no".

Why it matters for an FDE: Rarely needed, but the FDE must advise correctly — many customers demand fine-tuning when RAG is enough. Steering a customer away from a six-week training project toward a two-day prompting fix is one of the highest-leverage conversations you'll have.
Part I · The decision tree

The consulting heart: when fine-tuning is (and isn't) the answer

Fine-tuning continues training a model's weights on your own examples. That sounds like the ultimate customization lever, which is why customers reach for it first. In practice it is a specialist tool with a narrow sweet spot, and the FDE's job is to know the boundary precisely — because recommending it wrongly burns weeks and budget, and refusing it wrongly leaves real value on the table.

✓ What fine-tuning CAN do
  • Style & format consistency — always answer in the house voice, always emit the exact XML/JSON dialect, without 2K tokens of instructions per call.
  • Domain jargon fluency — medical shorthand, legal boilerplate, your codebase's idioms, produced natively instead of imitated.
  • Latency & cost via specialization — a small tuned model matching a big model on one narrow task (the distillation play, Part V).
  • Behavior that resists prompting — quirks you've genuinely failed to prompt away after honest iteration, e.g. a strict classification taxonomy the model keeps bending.
✗ What fine-tuning CANNOT do
  • Inject fresh facts — weights are a snapshot; the price list changes next week and the tune is stale. Worse: the model still hallucinates, now with more confidence in your domain's vocabulary (FN2).
  • Replace retrieval — "train it on our 40K documents" does not make the model able to quote them; RAG does (AI4).
  • Fix a bad task definition — if humans disagree on the correct output, training on their labels just bakes in the disagreement.
  • Substitute for evals — without a golden set you cannot even tell whether the tune helped (AI9).

The escalation ladder

Each rung is an order of magnitude more expensive than the last — in time, money, and maintenance. Only climb when the current rung has demonstrably failed on an eval, not on a vibe:

"Why does every customer ask for fine-tuning?"

You will hear "we want to fine-tune the model on our data" in a majority of discovery calls. Two forces produce it:

  • The ownership mental model. "Training our own model" sounds like an asset on the balance sheet — our AI, tuned to our business, a moat. Prompting sounds like renting. The instinct is emotional and organizational, not technical, so answer it on that level: what they actually want is differentiated behavior and control, and you can usually deliver both with prompts + RAG + evals, owned in their repo.
  • 2023-era blog residue. Early-LLM content marketing pushed fine-tuning as the customization path, before long context, cheap strong models, and mature RAG existed. Executives read those posts; the advice fossilized into strategy decks. Gently date-stamp it: "that guidance predates 200K-token contexts and models that follow instructions this well."
The reframe that works: never open with "you don't need fine-tuning" — that hears as "you don't get our special sauce." Open with "let's find the cheapest experiment that proves value" and put fine-tuning on the roadmap as a cost-optimization step behind evidence. You're not saying no; you're sequencing (FD6). The customer keeps their ambition; you keep the project off a six-week detour.
Part II · How it works

Fine-tuning at intuition level: SFT, LoRA, and the failure modes

Supervised fine-tuning: learning from input → output pairs

SFT is the workhorse. You show the model thousands of (prompt, ideal response) pairs and continue gradient descent so its outputs drift toward yours. Nothing magical: the same next-token training that built the model, now on your distribution. Consequences worth internalizing: the model learns the patterns in your pairs — tone, structure, label vocabulary — far more reliably than any facts inside them; whatever biases or errors your pairs contain are learned with equal enthusiasm; and everything the model learns is frozen at training time.

Full fine-tuning vs LoRA/PEFT: the adapter intuition

Full fine-tuning updates every weight — billions of parameters, serious GPU fleets, and a full-size copy of the model per customer per version. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, the flagship of the PEFT family) observes that the change a tune needs is low-rank: instead of updating the big matrix W, freeze it and learn two skinny matrices whose product is the update.

Why this matters commercially: LoRA makes fine-tuning cheap (train on one rented GPU or a provider's managed service), portable (an adapter is a small file, not a 140GB model copy), and stackable (one base model serving many customers, each with their own adapter loaded at request time). Every managed fine-tuning API you'll meet in Lab 3 is LoRA or a close cousin under the hood.

Hyperparameters — awareness level only

KnobWhat it doesFailure it causes
EpochsHow many passes over your datasetToo many on small data → overfitting: parrots training examples, degrades on everything else. 1–3 is the usual answer.
Learning rateStep size of each weight updateToo high → catastrophic forgetting; too low → nothing learned. Trust provider defaults first.
LoRA rank rAdapter capacityHigher r ≠ better; 8–16 suffices for style/format tasks.
Catastrophic forgetting: aggressive training on a narrow dataset degrades the general abilities that made the model useful — a model tuned hard on legal-clause extraction can get worse at following instructions it handled fine before. This is why the before/after eval in Lab 3 always includes a handful of general prompts, not just task prompts. If the tune wins your task and tanks everything else, you shipped a liability.

RLHF & DPO — recognize the names

Beyond SFT sit preference-tuning methods: RLHF (train a reward model from human preference rankings, then optimize against it) and DPO (skip the reward model; optimize directly on chosen vs rejected response pairs). They shape fuzzy qualities — helpfulness, tone, harmlessness — where "the one right answer" doesn't exist and SFT has nothing to copy. As an FDE you should recognize these on a whiteboard and know that frontier labs use them to build the models you call; running them yourself is deep-ML territory that virtually no customer engagement justifies. If a customer needs preference shaping, the practical tool is a better system prompt and few-shot preferences — not a reward model.

Part III · Dataset engineering

Where fine-tuning projects live or die

The model, the provider, the hyperparameters — all second-order. The dataset is the product. 500 excellent examples beat 50,000 scraped ones, because the model learns your pairs' patterns indiscriminately: inconsistent labels teach inconsistency, sloppy formatting teaches sloppiness, and one systematically mislabeled slice becomes a systematic production bug. Budget the majority of any fine-tuning engagement for data work and you'll be estimating correctly.

Format: chat-shaped pairs

Modern instruction models train on chat-formatted JSONL — one conversation per line, with the system prompt you intend to use in production baked in (train and serve with the same system prompt; mismatch quietly costs accuracy): 

{"messages": [
  {"role": "system", "content": "Classify the support message into exactly one intent."},
  {"role": "user", "content": "hey i got charged twice this month??"},
  {"role": "assistant", "content": "billing_question"}
]}

Dedup & decontamination: don't lie to yourself

Two hygiene steps, both cheap, both routinely skipped: deduplicate (exact and near-duplicates concentrate the gradient on repeated examples and inflate apparent dataset size), and decontaminate against your eval set — no training example may appear in, or be a paraphrase of, anything in the golden set from AI9.

Leakage = lying to yourself. If eval questions leak into training data, your "94% accuracy" measures memorization, not capability — and the customer discovers the truth in production, at the worst possible moment for your credibility. Rule of thumb: split first, then never look at the eval slice again while building training data. Lab 2 makes the split mechanical.

The review discipline: read 50 random samples yourself

Before any training run: shuffle, take 50, and read every one — you, not a script. You're checking label correctness ("is this really refund_request?"), consistency (are two near-identical inputs labeled the same?), format uniformity, and coverage gaps (whole intents or phrasings missing). Every serious data team does exactly this ritual; skipping it is how six-figure training runs learn a typo. Twenty minutes of reading routinely finds the bug that would have cost a re-train.

Part IV · Running it

Provider APIs, the open-source path, and the non-negotiable eval

Managed fine-tuning: the typical flow

For hosted-model tuning (OpenAI's fine-tuning API on their mini-tier models) and for open-weight models (Together, Fireworks, and similar platforms hosting Llama/Qwen-class models), the workflow is deliberately boring and near-identical everywhere: 

1. Upload train.jsonl (+ optional validation file)     # chat-formatted pairs from Part III
2. Create a fine-tuning job (base model, epochs, LoRA on)
3. Wait — minutes to hours; watch training/validation loss
4. Eval the resulting model on YOUR golden set           # loss curves are not product metrics
5. Deploy — a new model ID behind the same chat API
6. Route traffic to it like any other model              # PR2 routing patterns apply unchanged

Note what you don't manage: GPUs, checkpoints, serving. That convenience is exactly why the managed path is the right first move for FDE engagements — the differentiating work stays in the dataset and the eval, which are yours either way. Anthropic does not currently offer self-serve fine-tuning of Claude models, which is one more reason the practical pattern is prompted Claude as the teacher/judge, small open model as the student.

The open-source path — awareness level

Tools like axolotl and unsloth run LoRA tuning on a single rented GPU (an A100/H100 hour costs a few dollars; a 7–8B LoRA run over a few thousand examples finishes in well under an hour). Choose this path when the customer requires weights on their own infrastructure — compliance, air-gapped deployments, or full model ownership (AI12). The training config is ~30 lines of YAML; the real added cost is that serving the result — GPUs, autoscaling, monitoring — becomes your problem too. Price that into Part V honestly.

Eval before/after — the non-negotiable

Never ship a fine-tune without measuring base vs tuned on the same golden set (AI9). Training loss going down proves the model memorized your data's shape, nothing more. The only claim that matters is "tuned model scores X on the held-out eval vs Y for the base model and Z for the prompted frontier model" — three numbers, same set, same harness. Lab 3 exists to make this comparison muscle memory. Include a few general-ability prompts to catch catastrophic forgetting (Part II).

Version fine-tuned models like prompts

A fine-tuned model is an artifact with lineage: dataset version + base model + hyperparameters + eval report. Store all four next to each other (the dataset hash in the model's metadata is the cleanest trick), because the day production regresses you must answer "what exactly is this model and how do we rebuild it?" And when the provider deprecates the base model — they will — the re-tune is a re-run of a recorded recipe, not archaeology.

Part V · The economics

The math that decides — and how to say "no" productively

The legitimate business case for fine-tuning in most engagements is distillation for unit cost/latency at high volume. So run the numbers like an owner. Worked example — an intent classifier, ~300 input / ~10 output tokens per call, at 1M calls/month (illustrative list prices; re-check current pricing before you present this to anyone):

OptionUnit economics~ Monthly @ 1M callsHidden costs
claude-sonnet-5
prompt-only, cached system prompt		$3/M in · $15/M out; cache cuts the shared prefix ~90%~$1,100None — zero infra, instant prompt iteration
claude-haiku-4-5
prompt-only		$1/M in · $5/M out~$350Slight accuracy check needed vs sonnet (PR2)
Fine-tuned 8B open model
LoRA, serverless hosting		~$50 one-off training · ~$0.20/M serving tokens~$60 + hosting floorMaintenance: dataset upkeep, re-tune + re-eval on every base-model or taxonomy change, eng-days each quarter

At 1M calls/month the tuned model saves ~$290/month over Haiku — which one engineer-day of quarterly maintenance erases entirely. At 100K calls/month the math is a rout: $35 vs $6 is noise, take the API. At 10M calls/month the tuned model saves thousands monthly and latency drops too — now the engineering is justified. The crossover for a task this small typically sits in the several-million-calls-per-month range; heavier prompts move it earlier, prompt caching moves it later.

The maintenance burden — the line item everyone forgets

  • Every base-model deprecation = re-tune + full re-eval, on the provider's schedule, not yours.
  • Every task change (new intent, changed taxonomy, new document type) = dataset update + re-tune + re-eval. A prompt change ships in an afternoon; a tune ships in a sprint.
  • Frontier drift: next year's cheap general model may simply beat your tuned model at its own task — re-run the Lab 3 comparison on a schedule, or you'll be maintaining a fossil.

When the math says no — and how to say it

Most of the time, it says no. Deliver the verdict as a decision document, not a lecture: options compared on the customer's own volume numbers, the measured accuracy from a real experiment, a recommendation, and — crucially — revisit triggers ("if volume passes 5M calls/month or p95 latency becomes contractual, we re-run this analysis"). A one-way "no" invites the customer to get a second opinion from someone who'll happily sell them a training project; a documented decision with triggers makes you the advisor they come back to (FD4). The CTO got to say "we fine-tuned a model on our data" to the board. The system got the architecture it actually needed. Both facts matter.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Fine-tuning to inject knowledge

The #1 customer request and the #1 failure mode. Facts go stale in weights, and the tuned model hallucinates them fluently. Knowledge → retrieval; behavior → maybe tuning. Hold that line.

✗ Training before evaling

No golden set → no way to know if the tune helped, or to notice catastrophic forgetting. The eval exists before the first training job, or the training job doesn't exist.

✗ Eval contamination

Train/eval overlap after "one quick regeneration" of the dataset is how 94% demos become 70% products. Dedup, split once, freeze the eval slice, and check overlap mechanically.

✗ Ignoring the maintenance tail

The training run is the cheap part. Base-model deprecations, taxonomy changes, and frontier drift all force re-tunes — price the tail into the TCO or the "savings" are fiction.

✓ Read 50 random samples before every run

Twenty minutes of eyeballing catches label errors, format drift, and coverage holes that no metric will. The single highest-ROI habit in this module.

✓ Sequence, don't refuse

"Fine-tuning is on the roadmap, behind this experiment and these volume triggers" keeps the customer's ambition intact and your architecture honest — and it's usually the truth.

2
Generate varied raw examples. Create gen.py. Variety comes from crossing intents with style personas — without it, synthetic data collapses into one clean register no real user writes in: 
import anthropic, json, itertools

client = anthropic.Anthropic()

INTENTS = ["billing_question", "cancel_subscription", "technical_issue",
           "upgrade_plan", "refund_request", "account_access"]
STYLES  = ["terse and typo-ridden", "polite and wordy", "frustrated, in a hurry",
           "non-native English speaker", "mentions an order number and a date"]

raw = []
for intent, style in itertools.product(INTENTS, STYLES):
    msg = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens=800,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"Write 3 realistic customer-support messages whose underlying intent is "
            f"'{intent}'. Writing style: {style}. Vary length, vocabulary and structure; "
            f"never name the intent explicitly. One message per line, no numbering."}])
    for line in msg.content[0].text.split("\n"):
        if line.strip():
            raw.append({"text": line.strip(), "intent": intent})

json.dump(raw, open("raw.json", "w"), indent=1)
print(len(raw), "raw examples")
Expected: ~90 raw examples per run at 3 per cell — run it a few times (or raise to 5 per cell) until you're past ~350 raw, since filtering and dedup will cost you 15–30%.
3
Judge-pass filter. A separate model call checks each example is unambiguous — this is the "quality > quantity" step, in code: 
import anthropic, json

client = anthropic.Anthropic()
raw = json.load(open("raw.json"))
INTENTS = sorted({r["intent"] for r in raw})

def keep(text, intent):
    msg = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens=5,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"Intents: {INTENTS}\nMessage: {text}\n\n"
            f"Answer KEEP only if this message clearly expresses '{intent}' "
            f"and could not reasonably be another listed intent. "
            f"Otherwise answer DROP. One word."}])
    return msg.content[0].text.strip().upper().startswith("KEEP")

kept = [r for r in raw if keep(r["text"], r["intent"])]
print(f"kept {len(kept)}/{len(raw)}")
json.dump(kept, open("filtered.json", "w"), indent=1)
Expect 10–25% dropped. Read 15 of the drops — most will genuinely be ambiguous ("I want to change my plan" — upgrade or cancel?). That's the filter earning its keep.
4
Dedup, split, and write chat-formatted JSONL. Dedup before splitting — near-duplicates straddling the split are eval leakage: 
import json, random, re

kept = json.load(open("filtered.json"))

def norm(t):  # crude near-dup key: lowercase alphanumerics only
    return re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9]", "", t.lower())

seen, unique = set(), []
for r in kept:
    k = norm(r["text"])
    if k not in seen:
        seen.add(k); unique.append(r)
print(f"{len(unique)} unique (removed {len(kept)-len(unique)} dups)")

random.seed(42); random.shuffle(unique)
cut = int(len(unique) * 0.8)
SYSTEM = ("Classify the customer message into exactly one intent. "
          "Reply with only the intent label.")

def write(path, rows):
    with open(path, "w") as f:
        for r in rows:
            f.write(json.dumps({"messages": [
                {"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM},
                {"role": "user", "content": r["text"]},
                {"role": "assistant", "content": r["intent"]}]}) + "\n")

write("train.jsonl", unique[:cut])
write("eval.jsonl",  unique[cut:])
print(f"train={cut} eval={len(unique)-cut}")
Target: ~300 total, ~240 train / ~60 eval, with every intent present in both files (print a per-intent count and fix imbalances by generating more for thin intents).
5
The review ritual. shuf train.jsonl | head -50 (or a Python sample) and read all 50. Log every problem you find in a dataset-notes.md: wrong labels, giveaway phrasings ("I want a refund" for refund_request is too easy — real users are messier), style collapse. Fix or regenerate the offenders. Zero findings on 50 samples is suspicious, not reassuring — look harder.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Generation returns numbered lists despite instructions → strip ^\d+[.)]\s* prefixes in code; don't fight it with more prompt.
  • Judge keeps ~100% → your generator is producing textbook examples; add messier styles and ambiguity pressure ("include a red-herring detail from another topic").
  • Rate limits → batch generation calls with a small time.sleep; the judge pass is ~350 cheap calls, fine sequentially.

✅ Done when

3
One eval harness, three contenders — compare.py. Same eval file, same accuracy definition, wall-clock latency per call: 
import json, time, anthropic
from together import Together

tg, cl = Together(), anthropic.Anthropic()
EVAL = [json.loads(l) for l in open("eval.jsonl")]
SYSTEM = EVAL[0]["messages"][0]["content"]
LABELS = sorted({e["messages"][2]["content"] for e in EVAL})

def run_together(model, text):
    r = tg.chat.completions.create(model=model, max_tokens=10,
        messages=[{"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM},
                  {"role": "user", "content": text}])
    return r.choices[0].message.content.strip()

def run_claude(text):
    r = cl.messages.create(model="claude-haiku-4-5-20251001", max_tokens=10,
        system=SYSTEM + f" Valid intents: {LABELS}",
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": text}])
    return r.content[0].text.strip()

def evaluate(name, fn):
    hits, times = 0, []
    for e in EVAL:
        text, gold = e["messages"][1]["content"], e["messages"][2]["content"]
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        pred = fn(text)
        times.append(time.perf_counter() - t0)
        hits += int(gold in pred)   # tolerant match; log misses for review
    times.sort()
    p50 = times[len(times)//2]
    print(f"{name:28s} acc={hits/len(EVAL):.2%}  p50={p50*1000:.0f}ms")

BASE = "meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-Reference"
FT   = "<your job.output_name here>"
evaluate("base 8B (prompt only)", lambda t: run_together(BASE, t))
evaluate("fine-tuned 8B",         lambda t: run_together(FT, t))
evaluate("claude-haiku-4-5",      lambda t: run_claude(t))

5
Forgetting check. Send both 8B models three general prompts ("summarize this paragraph", "translate to French", "answer this trivia question"). If the tuned model answers everything with an intent label, you've watched catastrophic forgetting live — note it in the report; for a single-purpose classifier it's acceptable, and knowing that trade-off consciously is the point.

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Upload rejected → validate JSONL: every line parses, exactly the messages key, roles in system/user/assistant order.
  • Base model outputs essays instead of labels → tighten the system prompt with "Reply with only the label" + the label list; keep it identical across contenders except the tuned model needs no label list — that asymmetry is a finding.
  • Tuned model no better than base → dataset too small/clean or eval too easy; also check the tuned model is actually being served (wrong model ID fails silently into base behavior on some platforms).
