Fine-tuning & Model Adaptation

Decide prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning; LoRA/PEFT, dataset preparation, distillation. The skill here is 90% judgment and 10% training scripts — knowing when the answer is "no".

Why it matters for an FDE: Rarely needed, but the FDE must advise correctly — many customers demand fine-tuning when RAG is enough. Steering a customer away from a six-week training project toward a two-day prompting fix is one of the highest-leverage conversations you'll have.
Part I · The decision tree

The consulting heart: when fine-tuning is (and isn't) the answer

Fine-tuning continues training a model's weights on your own examples. That sounds like the ultimate customization lever, which is why customers reach for it first. In practice it is a specialist tool with a narrow sweet spot, and the FDE's job is to know the boundary precisely — because recommending it wrongly burns weeks and budget, and refusing it wrongly leaves real value on the table.

✓ What fine-tuning CAN do
  • Style & format consistency — always answer in the house voice, always emit the exact XML/JSON dialect, without 2K tokens of instructions per call.
  • Domain jargon fluency — medical shorthand, legal boilerplate, your codebase's idioms, produced natively instead of imitated.
  • Latency & cost via specialization — a small tuned model matching a big model on one narrow task (the distillation play, Part V).
  • Behavior that resists prompting — quirks you've genuinely failed to prompt away after honest iteration, e.g. a strict classification taxonomy the model keeps bending.
✗ What fine-tuning CANNOT do
  • Inject fresh facts — weights are a snapshot; the price list changes next week and the tune is stale. Worse: the model still hallucinates, now with more confidence in your domain's vocabulary (FN2).
  • Replace retrieval — "train it on our 40K documents" does not make the model able to quote them; RAG does (AI4).
  • Fix a bad task definition — if humans disagree on the correct output, training on their labels just bakes in the disagreement.
  • Substitute for evals — without a golden set you cannot even tell whether the tune helped (AI9).

"Why does every customer ask for fine-tuning?"

You will hear "we want to fine-tune the model on our data" in a majority of discovery calls. Two forces produce it:

  • The ownership mental model. "Training our own model" sounds like an asset on the balance sheet — our AI, tuned to our business, a moat. Prompting sounds like renting. The instinct is emotional and organizational, not technical, so answer it on that level: what they actually want is differentiated behavior and control, and you can usually deliver both with prompts + RAG + evals, owned in their repo.
  • 2023-era blog residue. Early-LLM content marketing pushed fine-tuning as the customization path, before long context, cheap strong models, and mature RAG existed. Executives read those posts; the advice fossilized into strategy decks. Gently date-stamp it: "that guidance predates 200K-token contexts and models that follow instructions this well."
The reframe that works: never open with "you don't need fine-tuning" — that hears as "you don't get our special sauce." Open with "let's find the cheapest experiment that proves value" and put fine-tuning on the roadmap as a cost-optimization step behind evidence. You're not saying no; you're sequencing (FD6). The customer keeps their ambition; you keep the project off a six-week detour.

Teams that delete the prompt path after a successful tune discover, eighteen months later, that they cannot answer "how much worse is this than just calling the current frontier model?" — and the answer by then is usually "worse".

Three questions that settle it in ten minutes

You do not need a two-week discovery to know whether fine-tuning is on the table. You need three answers, and customers can give all three in a single call. What is missing — knowledge or behaviour? What have you already tried, and what did the eval say? How many calls per month, and is latency contractual? Every wrong fine-tuning recommendation I have seen skipped one of these, usually the second. Note the shape: question one rules fine-tuning out, question two rules it out again, and only question three can rule it in.

Answers that mean "no tune"
  • "It doesn't know about our new product line." — knowledge. Weights are a snapshot; retrieval is the tool.
  • "We tried a couple of prompts and it wasn't great." — no eval, no iteration. There is nothing here to escalate from.
  • "A few hundred calls a day." — volume too low to repay any training cost, ever, at any accuracy.
  • "The experts disagree about half the labels." — the task is undefined. Training on that just launders the disagreement into a model.
  • "We want the model to be ours." — an ownership question, not a technical one. Answer it with dataset + eval + prompt ownership.
Answers that keep it alive
  • "Copywriters reject 60% of drafts as off-voice, after six weeks of prompt work." — behaviour, with evidence of prompting failure.
  • "Our eval is at 0.88 and has been for three iterations." — a measured plateau, not a vibe.
  • "8M classifications a month and p95 under 400 ms is in the contract." — economics and latency, the legitimate case.
  • "We have 2,000 human-approved outputs already labelled." — the expensive input already exists.
  • "Weights must stay in our VPC for regulatory reasons." — a constraint no API can satisfy (AI12).

Make it mechanical so you cannot talk yourself into a training project on the way home from a good meeting. This is the whole decision tree as forty lines you can run during the call:

triage.py — the ten-minute verdict, run it live in the call 
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Ask:
    gap: str            # "knowledge" | "behavior" | "cost_latency" | "undefined"
    prompt_iterations: int
    has_eval: bool
    eval_score: float   # best score reached so far, 0..1
    calls_per_month: int
    labeled_examples: int
    latency_contractual: bool

def verdict(a: Ask) -> str:
    if not a.has_eval:
        return "BUILD AN EVAL FIRST — no other question is answerable (AI9)"
    if a.gap == "undefined":
        return "RE-SCOPE — write the labeling guide, then re-ask"
    if a.gap == "knowledge":
        return "RAG — facts do not belong in weights (AI4)"
    if a.prompt_iterations < 5:
        return "PROMPT — you have not tried yet (AI1)"
    if a.gap == "behavior" and a.eval_score < 0.80 and a.labeled_examples >= 500:
        return "FINE-TUNE CANDIDATE — style/format case; eval before and after"
    if a.gap == "cost_latency":
        breakeven = 2_300_000          # steady-state, derived in Part V
        if a.calls_per_month >= breakeven or a.latency_contractual:
            return "DISTILL — the one fine-tune that usually pays (Part VI)"
        return f"NO — {a.calls_per_month:,}/mo is below the {breakeven:,} break-even"
    return "PROMPT + EXAMPLES — nothing here justifies training"

print(verdict(Ask("knowledge", 9, True, 0.71, 400_000, 0, False)))
RAG — facts do not belong in weights (AI4)
print(verdict(Ask("cost_latency", 12, True, 0.93, 8_000_000, 2000, True)))
DISTILL — the one fine-tune that usually pays (Part VI)
print(verdict(Ask("cost_latency", 12, True, 0.93, 400_000, 2000, False)))

The function is not clever and that is the point — it encodes the argument so the argument survives your mood, the customer's enthusiasm, and the fourth hour of a workshop. Paste it into the engagement repo and let the customer edit the constants; the moment they change breakeven to justify their answer, you are having the right conversation instead of the wrong one. The conversation script: how to say no without saying no

Being right is a quarter of the job. The customer has told their board they are building a custom model; an FDE who opens with "that's the wrong approach" has just made the CTO defend a position in front of their own team, and from then on the technical argument is a proxy war. The move that works is agree with the goal, disagree with the sequence — and then make the sequence cheap enough that saying yes to it costs them nothing. Here is the script, close to verbatim, from a discovery call that went well:

Part II · How it works

Fine-tuning at intuition level: SFT, LoRA, and the failure modes

Supervised fine-tuning: learning from input → output pairs

SFT is the workhorse. You show the model thousands of (prompt, ideal response) pairs and continue gradient descent so its outputs drift toward yours. Nothing magical: the same next-token training that built the model, now on your distribution. Consequences worth internalizing: the model learns the patterns in your pairs — tone, structure, label vocabulary — far more reliably than any facts inside them; whatever biases or errors your pairs contain are learned with equal enthusiasm; and everything the model learns is frozen at training time. 

# What SFT optimises, in one line. No magic, no memory, no reasoning:
loss = -Σ log P(next_token | everything_before_it)   # averaged over your rows

# and — the detail that explains most surprises — the loss is usually
# MASKED so it is computed on the assistant turn only:
system   "Classify the customer message..."     ← masked, context only
user     "hey i got charged twice this month??"  ← masked, context only
assistant "billing_question"                     ← THIS is what is trained

# Consequence: putting your instructions in the user turn instead of the
# system turn changes nothing about what is learned, but changes the
# distribution the model expects at serving time. Keep them identical.

The masking detail matters more than it looks. Because the loss lands on the assistant turn only, SFT is extremely efficient at teaching output shape — labels, JSON dialects, house voice — and almost useless at teaching the model new things about the content of the input, which it only ever sees as context. That single asymmetry is the mechanical reason behind the whole knowledge-versus-behaviour rule in Part I.

Full fine-tuning vs LoRA/PEFT: the adapter intuition

Full fine-tuning updates every weight — billions of parameters, serious GPU fleets, and a full-size copy of the model per customer per version. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, the flagship of the PEFT family) observes that the change a tune needs is low-rank: instead of updating the big matrix W, freeze it and learn two skinny matrices whose product is the update.

Why this matters commercially: LoRA makes fine-tuning cheap (train on one rented GPU or a provider's managed service), portable (an adapter is a small file, not a 140GB model copy), and stackable (one base model serving many customers, each with their own adapter loaded at request time). Every managed fine-tuning API you'll meet in Lab 3 is LoRA or a close cousin under the hood.

This is why the before/after eval in Lab 3 always includes a handful of general prompts, not just task prompts. If the tune wins your task and tanks everything else, you shipped a liability.

RLHF & DPO — recognize the names

Beyond SFT sit preference-tuning methods: RLHF (train a reward model from human preference rankings, then optimize against it) and DPO (skip the reward model; optimize directly on chosen vs rejected response pairs). They shape fuzzy qualities — helpfulness, tone, harmlessness — where "the one right answer" doesn't exist and SFT has nothing to copy. As an FDE you should recognize these on a whiteboard and know that frontier labs use them to build the models you call; running them yourself is deep-ML territory that virtually no customer engagement justifies. If a customer needs preference shaping, the practical tool is a better system prompt and few-shot preferences — not a reward model. 

# The one thing worth being able to draw: DPO needs a DIFFERENT dataset shape.
# SFT — one target per prompt:
{"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "Draft the churn email."},
              {"role": "assistant", "content": "<the one good answer>"}]}

# DPO — a pair, and a human who ranked them:
{"prompt":   "Draft the churn email.",
 "chosen":   "<the version the reviewer preferred>",
 "rejected": "<a plausible version they did not>"}

# The cost is in the word "ranked". SFT data can be harvested from a strong
# model (Part VI). Preference data needs a human judgement per PAIR, which is
# roughly 3-5x the labelling cost for a signal that only shapes fuzzy taste.

Be able to say that out loud in a whiteboard session and you have covered everything an FDE needs from preference tuning: it exists, it needs pairs, the pairs need humans, and the humans cost three to five times what SFT labels cost. If someone in the room genuinely needs it, the correct next step is their ML platform team, not your sprint.

Where "continued pretraining" comes up. Occasionally a customer with a genuinely alien corpus — chip-design netlists, clinical coding, a proprietary markup language — asks about continued pretraining on raw text. It is real, it occasionally works, and it is out of scope for an FDE engagement: it needs unlabelled data at the 109-token scale, a team who can debug a diverging loss curve, and an evaluation programme of its own. The FDE answer is "that's a research project with your ML platform team or a lab partnership, not a delivery workstream" — and then you go build the retrieval system that gets them 80% of the benefit this quarter.

The chat template — the silent accuracy tax

Here is the bug that eats a day of every first fine-tuning project and never appears in a tutorial. Your JSONL contains roles and content; the model consumes a flat token sequence. The mapping between them is the model's chat template — special tokens like <|start_header_id|>, a beginning-of-text marker, an end-of-turn marker. Every model family has its own. If your training harness applies a different template from your serving stack, the tuned model sees strings at inference that it never saw during training, and quality collapses in a way no loss curve reveals — training loss looked beautiful, eval accuracy is 12 points down, and nothing in your dataset is wrong.

template_check.py — run this before you spend money on a training job 
from transformers import AutoTokenizer
import json

BASE = "meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct"
tok  = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(BASE)

row = json.loads(open("train.jsonl").readline())

# what the trainer will actually feed the model, tokens and all
rendered = tok.apply_chat_template(row["messages"], tokenize=False)
print(repr(rendered))
'<|begin_of_text|><|start_header_id|>system<|end_header_id|>\n\nClassify the'
' customer message into exactly one intent...<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>user'
'<|end_header_id|>\n\nhey i got charged twice this month??<|eot_id|>...'

# the three assertions that catch 90% of template bugs
assert rendered.startswith(tok.bos_token), "missing BOS — double-BOS or no-BOS is the classic"
assert rendered.count("<|eot_id|>") == len(row["messages"]), "turn markers do not match turns"
assert len(tok(rendered)["input_ids"]) < 4096, "row will be silently truncated"

# and the length distribution, because truncation is the other silent killer
lens = [len(tok(tok.apply_chat_template(json.loads(l)["messages"],
        tokenize=False))["input_ids"]) for l in open("train.jsonl")]
lens.sort()
print(f"n={len(lens)} p50={lens[len(lens)//2]} p99={lens[int(len(lens)*0.99)]} max={lens[-1]}")
n=240 p50=61 p99=118 max=143

A p99 of 118 tokens against a 4,096-token window means nothing is being truncated — which is exactly the boring result you want to confirm rather than assume. On a document-summarisation dataset the same script routinely prints p99=6,204 max=31,880, at which point a third of your training examples were being cut off mid-input and the model was learning to produce summaries of truncated documents. Managed fine-tuning APIs apply the correct template for you, which removes this class of bug entirely and is one more reason to start there.

Train and serve with the identical system prompt. If you train with "Classify the customer message into exactly one intent." and serve with "You are a helpful support assistant. Classify...", you have introduced a distribution shift on every single request. It typically costs 3–8 accuracy points and it is invisible unless you diff the strings. Put the system prompt in one constant, import it in the dataset builder and in the serving path, and assert equality in a test.

What actually happens at request time

The commercial magic of LoRA is not the training, it is the serving. Because the base weights are frozen and shared, one loaded copy of an 8B model can serve dozens of adapters concurrently, with the router selecting an adapter per request from a header or a tenant id. That is the difference between "each customer needs their own GPU" and "each customer needs their own 90 MB file" — and it is the reason a multi-tenant tuned product is economically possible at all (PR12).

serve.py — the two serving shapes, and when each is right 
# SHAPE A — unmerged: one base, adapter chosen per request. Multi-tenant.
#   vLLM: --enable-lora --lora-modules acme=/adapters/acme-v3 globex=/adapters/globex-v1
resp = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="acme",                       # the adapter name, not the base model
    messages=[{"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM},
              {"role": "user", "content": text}],
    max_tokens=10)

# SHAPE B — merged: fold B·A back into W, ship one standalone model. Single-tenant.
from peft import PeftModel
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM

base   = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(BASE, torch_dtype="bfloat16")
tuned  = PeftModel.from_pretrained(base, "./adapters/acme-v3")
merged = tuned.merge_and_unload()          # W ← W + B·A, adapter disappears
merged.save_pretrained("./models/acme-v3-merged")
print(sum(p.numel() for p in merged.parameters()) / 1e9, "B params")
8.03 B params          # same size as the base — the adapter is now invisible

Shape A is what every managed provider runs for you, and what you want if the same task has per-customer variants. Shape B is what you want when one tuned model serves everyone and you would rather not explain adapter loading to the platform team at 2 a.m. The trap in shape B is that you have now created a 16 GB artifact with no lineage on its face — the merge erases the evidence of what was trained into it. Part IV's manifest discipline exists precisely because merge_and_unload() is so convenient.

Part III · Dataset engineering

Where fine-tuning projects live or die

The model, the provider, the hyperparameters — all second-order. The dataset is the product. 500 excellent examples beat 50,000 scraped ones, because the model learns your pairs' patterns indiscriminately: inconsistent labels teach inconsistency, sloppy formatting teaches sloppiness, and one systematically mislabeled slice becomes a systematic production bug. Budget the majority of any fine-tuning engagement for data work and you'll be estimating correctly.

Format: chat-shaped pairs

Modern instruction models train on chat-formatted JSONL — one conversation per line, with the system prompt you intend to use in production baked in (train and serve with the same system prompt; mismatch quietly costs accuracy): 

{"messages": [
  {"role": "system", "content": "Classify the support message into exactly one intent."},
  {"role": "user", "content": "hey i got charged twice this month??"},
  {"role": "assistant", "content": "billing_question"}
]}

Dedup & decontamination: don't lie to yourself

Two hygiene steps, both cheap, both routinely skipped: deduplicate (exact and near-duplicates concentrate the gradient on repeated examples and inflate apparent dataset size), and decontaminate against your eval set — no training example may appear in, or be a paraphrase of, anything in the golden set from AI9.

Leakage = lying to yourself. If eval questions leak into training data, your "94% accuracy" measures memorization, not capability — and the customer discovers the truth in production, at the worst possible moment for your credibility. Rule of thumb: split first, then never look at the eval slice again while building training data. Lab 2 makes the split mechanical.

The review discipline: read 50 random samples yourself

Before any training run: shuffle, take 50, and read every one — you, not a script. You're checking label correctness ("is this really refund_request?"), consistency (are two near-identical inputs labeled the same?), format uniformity, and coverage gaps (whole intents or phrasings missing). Every serious data team does exactly this ritual; skipping it is how six-figure training runs learn a typo. Twenty minutes of reading routinely finds the bug that would have cost a re-train.

The schema, and the validator that enforces it

The chat JSONL above is deceptively simple, which is why it goes wrong so quietly. Providers reject files for structural errors, but they happily accept files that are structurally perfect and semantically broken — a trailing space on half the labels, three examples where the assistant turn is empty, a system prompt that drifted between the morning and afternoon generation runs. Those pass upload and cost you a training run. Write the validator once, run it in CI, and it becomes the thing that stops a bad dataset rather than a good intention.

The first time you run it on a dataset assembled by hand it will not: the two most common findings are two distinct system prompts (someone edited the constant halfway through generation) and trailing whitespace on the assistant turn, which teaches the model to emit a trailing space and quietly halves an exact-match eval score. Both take ten seconds to fix and neither is findable by reading the file.

Dedup and decontamination, in code

The rule stated above needs an implementation, because "check for duplicates" performed by eye finds exact matches and nothing else. What you actually need is near-duplicate detection between rows (they concentrate the gradient and inflate your apparent dataset size) and overlap detection between the training set and the eval set (the failure that turns a 94% demo into a 71% product). Character-shingle Jaccard similarity does both, needs no dependencies, and runs on 10,000 rows in under a second.

decontaminate.py — near-dup within train, and train↔eval leakage 
import json, itertools

def shingles(text, k=5):
    """Character 5-grams of the normalised text — robust to word reordering
    and small edits in a way that exact string matching is not."""
    t = " ".join(text.lower().split())
    return {t[i:i + k] for i in range(max(len(t) - k + 1, 1))}

def jaccard(a, b):
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b) if a | b else 0.0

def load(path):
    rows = [json.loads(l) for l in open(path)]
    return [{"text": r["messages"][-2]["content"],
             "label": r["messages"][-1]["content"],
             "sh": shingles(r["messages"][-2]["content"])} for r in rows]

train, ev = load("train.jsonl"), load("eval.jsonl")

# 1 — near-duplicates INSIDE train: keep the first, drop the rest
DUP = 0.85
drop = set()
for i, j in itertools.combinations(range(len(train)), 2):
    if j in drop:
        continue
    if jaccard(train[i]["sh"], train[j]["sh"]) >= DUP:
        drop.add(j)
print(f"near-dups in train: {len(drop)} of {len(train)}")
near-dups in train: 11 of 240

# 2 — CONTAMINATION: any train row too close to any eval row is a lie detector
LEAK = 0.70          # deliberately looser than DUP — paraphrases leak too
bad = [(i, e_i, round(s, 3))
       for i, t in enumerate(train) if i not in drop
       for e_i, e in enumerate(ev)
       if (s := jaccard(t["sh"], e["sh"])) >= LEAK]
print(f"contaminated train rows: {len({b[0] for b in bad})}")
for i, e_i, s in bad[:3]:
    print(f"  {s}  train[{i}]: {train[i]['text'][:52]}")

Three numbers to internalise from that run: 11 near-duplicates in 240 rows from a generation process that looked varied, 3 contaminated rows despite a clean random split — because the split happened after generation produced two near-identical messages that landed on opposite sides — and a final 226-row training set. Losing 6% of a small dataset feels bad for about a minute, until you notice that the alternative was reporting an eval score you could not defend. Note the asymmetry in step 3: contamination is always fixed by deleting from train, never by editing the eval set. The eval set is frozen the day it is created; the moment you start adjusting it to make results look better, it has stopped measuring anything (AI9).

The 0.70 threshold is a starting point, not a law. On short texts like support messages, character-shingle Jaccard runs high — two unrelated one-line complaints can hit 0.5 just by both being English. Print the score distribution before you pick a cutoff, and eyeball twenty pairs near the line. On long documents the opposite holds and 0.45 is often the right leakage threshold. Whichever you choose, record it in the dataset manifest, because "what counted as contamination" is a question you will be asked the first time results are challenged.

The review protocol, with real numbers

"Read 50 random samples" is the ritual; this is the protocol that makes it a measurement instead of a vibe. Fifty is not an arbitrary number — it is the sample size at which a defect rate of 6% or higher is very unlikely to go unnoticed. With a true defect rate p, the chance of seeing zero defects in 50 independent samples is (1−p)50: at p = 0.06 that is 4.5%, at p = 0.10 it is 0.5%. So zero findings in 50 is a real result — it bounds your defect rate under roughly 6% with 95% confidence — and three findings in 50 is a 6% observed rate that will absolutely show up in production behaviour. The point of the arithmetic is that you stop arguing about whether the dataset is "good enough" and start quoting an interval.

Defects found in 50Observed rate95% upper boundWhat to do

1–22–4%≈ 10–14%Fix those rows, proceed, note the defect classes in the manifest

Sampling by hand and scrolling a JSONL file is how the ritual gets skipped. Make it a thirty-second command that logs its own findings, and it survives contact with a deadline:

review.py — 50 stratified samples, keystroke verdicts, an audit trail 
import json, random, collections, datetime, sys

ROWS = [json.loads(l) for l in open("train.jsonl")]
DEFECTS = {"w": "wrong label", "a": "ambiguous — two labels defensible",
           "g": "giveaway — unrealistically easy", "f": "format/whitespace",
           "u": "unrealistic — no user writes like this", "k": "ok"}

# stratify so every label gets looked at, rather than only the common ones
by_label = collections.defaultdict(list)
for r in ROWS:
    by_label[r["messages"][-1]["content"]].append(r)

random.seed(7)
per = max(1, 50 // len(by_label))
sample = [r for rows in by_label.values()
          for r in random.sample(rows, min(per, len(rows)))]
random.shuffle(sample)

for i, r in enumerate(sample, 1):
    text  = r["messages"][-2]["content"]
    label = r["messages"][-1]["content"]
    print(f"\n[{i}/{len(sample)}] {text}\n   → {label}")

    if key != "k":
        findings.append({"text": text, "label": label,
                         "defect": DEFECTS.get(key, key)})

rate = len(findings) / len(sample)
report = {"reviewed": len(sample), "defects": len(findings),
          "rate": round(rate, 3), "reviewer": sys.argv[1],
          "date": datetime.date.today().isoformat(),
          "by_class": dict(collections.Counter(f["defect"] for f in findings)),
          "examples": findings}
json.dump(report, open("review-report.json", "w"), indent=1)

That output is the one you should expect on a first pass, and the defect breakdown is where the value is: four defects that turn out to be three "giveaway" and one "ambiguous" is a generation-prompt problem you fix in ten minutes, whereas four defects that are all "wrong label" is a task-definition problem that needs a human conversation. review-report.json is also the artifact you show the customer — a dated, signed defect rate on their data does more for trust than any architecture diagram, and it is what makes the eventual accuracy number believable.

Score-4 move: have two people review the same 50 rows independently and report the disagreement rate alongside the defect rate. If your two reviewers disagree on 20% of rows, the ceiling on any model you train is roughly the ceiling on human agreement — and the correct deliverable is not a fine-tune, it is a sharper labelling guide. Teams that measure this discover, about a third of the time, that the "model accuracy problem" was a specification problem wearing a costume.

How many examples do you actually need?

The honest answer is "fewer than you think, and you should measure rather than guess." Customers arrive believing they need tens of thousands because that is what the pretraining literature implies; for a narrow SFT task the returns flatten far earlier. The way to find your own curve is to train on subsamples and plot — three cheap runs at 25%, 50% and 100% of your data tell you whether more labelling is the highest-value next action or a waste of the customer's money.

Provider APIs, the open-source path, and the non-negotiable eval

Managed fine-tuning: the typical flow

For hosted-model tuning (OpenAI's fine-tuning API on their mini-tier models) and for open-weight models (Together, Fireworks, and similar platforms hosting Llama/Qwen-class models), the workflow is deliberately boring and near-identical everywhere: 

1. Upload train.jsonl (+ optional validation file)     # chat-formatted pairs from Part III
2. Create a fine-tuning job (base model, epochs, LoRA on)
3. Wait — minutes to hours; watch training/validation loss
4. Eval the resulting model on YOUR golden set           # loss curves are not product metrics
5. Deploy — a new model ID behind the same chat API
6. Route traffic to it like any other model              # PR2 routing patterns apply unchanged

Note what you don't manage: GPUs, checkpoints, serving. That convenience is exactly why the managed path is the right first move for FDE engagements — the differentiating work stays in the dataset and the eval, which are yours either way. Anthropic does not currently offer self-serve fine-tuning of Claude models, which is one more reason the practical pattern is prompted Claude as the teacher/judge, small open model as the student.

The open-source path — awareness level

Tools like axolotl and unsloth run LoRA tuning on a single rented GPU (an A100/H100 hour costs a few dollars; a 7–8B LoRA run over a few thousand examples finishes in well under an hour). Choose this path when the customer requires weights on their own infrastructure — compliance, air-gapped deployments, or full model ownership (AI12). The training config is ~30 lines of YAML; the real added cost is that serving the result — GPUs, autoscaling, monitoring — becomes your problem too. Price that into Part V honestly.

Eval before/after — the non-negotiable

Never ship a fine-tune without measuring base vs tuned on the same golden set (AI9). Training loss going down proves the model memorized your data's shape, nothing more. The only claim that matters is "tuned model scores X on the held-out eval vs Y for the base model and Z for the prompted frontier model" — three numbers, same set, same harness. Lab 3 exists to make this comparison muscle memory. Include a few general-ability prompts to catch catastrophic forgetting (Part II).

Version fine-tuned models like prompts

A fine-tuned model is an artifact with lineage: dataset version + base model + hyperparameters + eval report. Store all four next to each other (the dataset hash in the model's metadata is the cleanest trick), because the day production regresses you must answer "what exactly is this model and how do we rebuild it?" And when the provider deprecates the base model — they will — the re-tune is a re-run of a recorded recipe, not archaeology.

The managed-API walkthrough, end to end

Here is the whole thing as one script, with the parts people skip left in. It is deliberately unglamorous: upload, launch, poll, smoke-test, record. What makes it production work rather than a tutorial is the last two steps — a smoke test that fails loudly on a malformed label, and a manifest written at the moment of truth rather than reconstructed from Slack six months later. Everything here is Together's API; OpenAI's fine-tuning API is the same six calls with different nouns, and the fact that they are interchangeable is the reason this is the right first path.

The before/after harness — three contenders, one measurement

The eval discipline itself belongs to AI9 — golden sets, grader taxonomy, judge calibration, CI gates. What AI10 owns is the specific comparison that settles a fine-tuning decision, and it has a fixed shape: the same eval file, the same system prompt, the same accuracy function, three models, plus a general-ability probe. Anything less than all three contenders is not evidence, it is advocacy — a tuned model that beats its own base model but loses to a prompted API model has proved only that training does something.

from together import Together

LABELS = sorted({e["messages"][2]["content"] for e in EVAL})

# Prices per 1M tokens (in, out). Re-check before quoting these to anyone.
PRICES = {"tuned": (0.20, 0.20), "base": (0.20, 0.20),
          "haiku": (1.00, 5.00)}

def score(name, call):
    hits, lat, tin, tout = 0, [], 0, 0
    misses = []
    for e in EVAL:
        text, gold = e["messages"][1]["content"], e["messages"][2]["content"]
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        pred, u_in, u_out = call(text)
        lat.append((time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000)
        tin += u_in; tout += u_out
        if pred.strip() == gold:
            hits += 1
        else:
            misses.append({"text": text, "gold": gold, "pred": pred})
    lat.sort()
    p_in, p_out = PRICES[name]
    per_1k = (tin / len(EVAL) * p_in + tout / len(EVAL) * p_out) / 1000
    json.dump(misses, open(f"misses_{name}.json", "w"), indent=1)
    return {"model": name, "acc": hits / len(EVAL),
            "p50_ms": round(statistics.median(lat)),
            "p95_ms": round(lat[int(len(lat) * 0.95) - 1]),
            "usd_per_1k": round(per_1k, 4)}

The misses_*.json files are not optional either; reading twenty misses side by side is how you learn that the tuned model's errors are concentrated in two confusable intents and the fix is a labelling-guide edit, not more training.

Add a general-ability probe to every run. Six prompts that have nothing to do with the task — summarise a paragraph, translate a sentence, answer a factual question, follow a two-step instruction, refuse something it should refuse, format a list — scored pass/fail by hand. A single-purpose classifier that answers all six with billing_question has catastrophically forgotten, and that is fine if you decided it was fine. Shipping it without noticing is how a "successful" tune becomes an incident when someone routes a second use case at it.

Part V · The economics

The math that decides — and how to say "no" productively

The legitimate business case for fine-tuning in most engagements is distillation for unit cost/latency at high volume. So run the numbers like an owner. The maintenance burden — the line item everyone forgets

  • Every base-model deprecation = re-tune + full re-eval, on the provider's schedule, not yours.
  • Every task change (new intent, changed taxonomy, new document type) = dataset update + re-tune + re-eval. A prompt change ships in an afternoon; a tune ships in a sprint.
  • Frontier drift: next year's cheap general model may simply beat your tuned model at its own task — re-run the Lab 3 comparison on a schedule, or you'll be maintaining a fossil.

Make the tail a calendar entry rather than a good intention, or it will be discovered as an incident. The cheapest version of this is a quarterly job that re-runs the harness against whatever the current frontier cheap model is and opens a ticket when the gap closes:

When the math says no — and how to say it

Most of the time, it says no. Deliver the verdict as a decision document, not a lecture: options compared on the customer's own volume numbers, the measured accuracy from a real experiment, a recommendation, and — crucially — revisit triggers ("if volume passes 5M calls/month or p95 latency becomes contractual, we re-run this analysis"). A one-way "no" invites the customer to get a second opinion from someone who'll happily sell them a training project; a documented decision with triggers makes you the advisor they come back to (FD4). Break-even against just using a bigger model

The four break-even numbers above hide the most useful finding in this module: the break-even depends entirely on what you are comparing against, and the cheapest move is almost always to change the comparison. A customer running Sonnet on a classification task reaches tuning break-even at ~840K calls/month, which sounds like a strong case for training. But moving that same workload from Sonnet to Haiku captures $700 of the available $988 per million calls — 71% of the total prize — for two engineer-days and no permanent maintenance. Do that first, and the remaining case for tuning has to clear 2.3M calls/month on its own merits. Most of them do not.

The intercept is the argument. A prompted API model costs nothing when traffic is zero; a tuned model costs $675 a month sitting still. Every customer's volume forecast is optimistic, and the tuned line does not care — it charges the maintenance floor while you wait for the traffic that was promised in the business case. That asymmetry is why "we'll be at 5M calls by Q3" should be treated as a trigger to re-run this analysis, never as an input to it.

Distillation — the fine-tune that actually pays

Distillation is fine-tuning with the dataset problem solved. Instead of paying humans to write ideal outputs, you run a strong model — claude-sonnet-5 or claude-opus-5 with a carefully engineered prompt — over your real production inputs, filter its outputs, and train a small model on the survivors. The distribution is right by construction because the inputs are real. The labels are consistent by construction because one model produced them all. And you already know the ceiling: the teacher's own accuracy on your eval set. This is the shape of virtually every fine-tune that has ever earned its keep in an FDE engagement.

The filter is the whole craft. An unfiltered distillation dataset teaches the student every mistake the teacher made, with the added indignity that the student cannot outgrow them. Two cheap signals do most of the work: ask the teacher for a confidence field and route the bottom decile to humans, and run the teacher twice on a sample and discard the rows where it disagrees with itself — self-inconsistency is a near-perfect detector for genuinely ambiguous inputs, and those are exactly the rows that poison a small model.

distill.py — teacher labelling with a confidence gate and a self-consistency check 
import anthropic, json, collections

cl = anthropic.Anthropic()

SYSTEM = ("Classify the customer message into exactly one intent. "
          "Reply with only the intent label.")
LABELS = ["billing_question", "cancel_subscription", "technical_issue",
          "upgrade_plan", "refund_request", "account_access"]

# Structured output, not prefill — prefill returns 400 on Opus 5 / Sonnet 5.
SCHEMA = {"type": "json_schema", "schema": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {
        "intent":     {"type": "string", "enum": LABELS},
        "confidence": {"type": "number"},
        "why":        {"type": "string"}},
    "required": ["intent", "confidence", "why"]}}

def teach(text):
    m = cl.messages.create(
        model="claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens=200,
        system=[{"type": "text", "text": SYSTEM,
                 "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}}],
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": text}],
        output_config={"format": SCHEMA})
    if m.stop_reason == "refusal":          # check before touching content
        return None
    return json.loads(m.content[0].text)

# Real production inputs — the whole point. PR1 gives you these for free.
inputs = [json.loads(l)["user_message"] for l in open("traces.jsonl")]

keep, review, discard = [], [], []
for text in inputs:
    a = teach(text)
    if a is None:
        discard.append((text, "refusal")); continue
    if a["confidence"] < 0.75:
        review.append((text, a)); continue
    b = teach(text)                        # self-consistency: same input, second pass
    if b is None or b["intent"] != a["intent"]:
        discard.append((text, f"self-disagreement {a['intent']}/{b and b['intent']}"))
        continue
    keep.append({"messages": [
        {"role": "system",    "content": SYSTEM},
        {"role": "user",      "content": text},
        {"role": "assistant", "content": a["intent"]}]})

print(f"keep={len(keep)} review={len(review)} discard={len(discard)} "
      f"of {len(inputs)}")
print(collections.Counter(r for _, r in discard).most_common(3))

    f.writelines(json.dumps(r) + "\n" for r in keep)

Prompt optimisation against an eval — rung 1, done properly

"We tried prompting and it didn't work" nearly always means someone wrote four prompts by hand and picked a favourite. The mechanised version — generate variants, score every one on the frozen eval set, keep the winner — is a two-day build that routinely recovers most of the gap a customer wanted to close with training. It is boring, it is cheap, and it leaves behind a string rather than an obligation. The technique catalogue itself is AI1's; what matters here is that the search is scored, on the same eval set you would have used to judge a tune.

optimise_prompt.py — hill-climbing on the eval set, ~40 minutes end to end 
import anthropic, json, itertools

cl   = anthropic.Anthropic()
EVAL = [json.loads(l) for l in open("eval.jsonl")]
LABELS = sorted({e["messages"][2]["content"] for e in EVAL})

# The axes worth searching. Not "reword it and hope" — named, orthogonal moves.
AXES = {
  "role": ["",
           "You triage customer support messages for a SaaS billing team."],
  "defs": ["",
           "upgrade_plan = wants MORE service. cancel_subscription = wants to "
           "STOP paying. refund_request = wants money back for a past charge."],
  "shots": ["",
            "Examples:\n'charged twice' -> billing_question\n"
            "'cant log in' -> account_access\n'want my money back' -> refund_request"],
  "strict": ["",
             f"Reply with exactly one of {LABELS} and nothing else."],
}

def build(combo):
    return " ".join(p for p in combo if p).strip() or "Classify the message."

def accuracy(system):
    hits = 0
    for e in EVAL:
        m = cl.messages.create(
            model="claude-haiku-4-5", max_tokens=10,
            system=[{"type": "text", "text": system,
                     "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}}],
            messages=[{"role": "user", "content": e["messages"][1]["content"]}])
        if m.stop_reason != "refusal" and \
           m.content[0].text.strip() == e["messages"][2]["content"]:
            hits += 1
    return hits / len(EVAL)

results = []
for combo in itertools.product(*AXES.values()):
    s = build(combo)
    results.append((accuracy(s), sum(1 for p in combo if p), s))

results.sort(reverse=True)

    print(f"{acc:.0%}  axes={n_axes}  {s[:58]}...")

And the 4-axis winner beats the 3-axis one by a single point, which means the last axis is inside the noise on a 60-row eval and you should prefer the shorter prompt.

Guard against overfitting the prompt to the eval. Searching sixteen variants against 60 rows is a small enough search that it is honest; searching three hundred variants against the same 60 rows is prompt-shaped overfitting and your production accuracy will not match. Hold out a second slice you never search against, confirm the winner there, and quote that number. The discipline is identical to the train/eval split in Part III, for identical reasons (AI9).

Retrieval as behaviour-shaping, not just knowledge

Part I is emphatic that RAG is for knowledge and tuning is for behaviour, and that division is the right default. It has one important exception, and it is the exception that dissolves most style-tuning projects: retrieve the examples, not the facts. Instead of eight fixed few-shot examples chosen by whoever wrote the prompt, embed your corpus of approved outputs and retrieve the eight nearest ones for each incoming request. The model gets a demonstration set tailored to the exact task in front of it, which is a large fraction of what a style tune buys — and it updates the moment someone approves a new example, which is something a tune can never do.

Take Lab 1's scenario S3: the e-commerce brand whose copywriters reject 60% of generated product descriptions as off-voice, sitting on 800 approved examples. That is the textbook style-tuning case, and it is also the textbook dynamic-few-shot case. The difference is three days against three weeks.

import anthropic, json, numpy as np, voyageai

cl, vo = anthropic.Anthropic(), voyageai.Client()
gold  = json.load(open("gold.json"))
VECS  = np.load("gold_vecs.npy")
NORMS = np.linalg.norm(VECS, axis=1)

STYLE_GUIDE = open("voice.md").read()      # stays static; it is the constitution

def nearest(brief, k=8):
    q = np.array(vo.embed([brief], model="voyage-3",
                          input_type="query").embeddings[0])
    sims = VECS @ q / (NORMS * np.linalg.norm(q))
    return [gold[i] for i in np.argsort(-sims)[:k]]

Routing the hard subset instead of training

The fourth rung is the one engineers like least and finance likes most. If a cheap model is right 93% of the time and a strong model is right 97%, you do not have to choose: run the cheap model, get a confidence signal, and escalate only the uncertain tail. The economics are excellent because uncertainty is concentrated — escalating the bottom 12% of a distribution typically recovers most of the strong model's advantage, so you buy 96% accuracy at $434 per million calls instead of 97% at $1,050. The routing patterns themselves are PR2's; what belongs here is that this is a direct competitor to fine-tuning for the same budget, and it ships in two days.

cascade.py — cheap model first, escalate only the uncertain tail 
import anthropic, json, math

cl = anthropic.Anthropic()
SCHEMA = {"type": "json_schema", "schema": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {"intent": {"type": "string", "enum": LABELS},
                   "confidence": {"type": "number"}},
    "required": ["intent", "confidence"]}}

def classify(model, text):
    m = cl.messages.create(
        model=model, max_tokens=100,
        system=[{"type": "text", "text": SYSTEM,
                 "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}}],
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": text}],
        output_config={"format": SCHEMA})
    if m.stop_reason == "refusal":
        return {"intent": "__refused__", "confidence": 1.0}, m.usage
    return json.loads(m.content[0].text), m.usage

def route(text, threshold=0.85):
    cheap, u1 = classify("claude-haiku-4-5", text)
    if cheap["confidence"] >= threshold:
        return cheap["intent"], "haiku"
    strong, u2 = classify("claude-sonnet-5", text)   # the 12% tail
    return strong["intent"], "sonnet"

# Sweep the threshold on the eval set — this is the only way to pick it
for th in (0.70, 0.85, 0.95, 1.01):
    hits = esc = 0
    for e in EVAL:
        pred, who = route(e["messages"][1]["content"], th)
        hits += pred == e["messages"][2]["content"]
        esc  += who == "sonnet"
    share = esc / len(EVAL)
    usd_per_m = round((1 - share) * 350 + share * 1050)
    print(f"th={th:.2f}  acc={hits/len(EVAL):.0%}  escalated={share:.0%}  "
          f"${usd_per_m}/1M calls")

Read the sweep as a menu, not a search for an optimum. At th=0.85 you are buying 3 accuracy points for $84 per million calls; the next 1 point costs another $133, and the last fraction costs $483. Put that table in front of the customer and let them pick the row — it is their accuracy/cost trade, and a menu with prices is a far better artifact than a recommendation with a rationale. Compare with the tuned model from Part IV: 92% accuracy for $62 per million calls plus $675/month of overhead. Below 2.3M calls the cascade wins on total cost and beats the tuned model on accuracy, with no dataset, no training run, and no re-tune when the base model moves.

Self-reported confidence is a weak but useful signal. It is not calibrated probability and you should not treat it as one — a model saying 0.9 is not right 90% of the time. What it reliably does is rank: the rows it scores lowest really are the rows it gets wrong most often, which is all a cascade needs. Validate that by plotting accuracy against the confidence decile on your eval set once; if accuracy is flat across deciles the signal is useless for your task and you need a different gate — output entropy, disagreement between two cheap models, or a keyword trigger. On a reasoning-capable model you can also escalate to higher effort rather than to a bigger model (AI13).

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Fine-tuning to inject knowledge

The #1 customer request and the #1 failure mode. Facts go stale in weights, and the tuned model hallucinates them fluently. Knowledge → retrieval; behavior → maybe tuning. Hold that line.

✗ Training before evaling

No golden set → no way to know if the tune helped, or to notice catastrophic forgetting. The eval exists before the first training job, or the training job doesn't exist.

✗ Eval contamination

Train/eval overlap after "one quick regeneration" of the dataset is how 94% demos become 70% products. Dedup, split once, freeze the eval slice, and check overlap mechanically.

✗ Ignoring the maintenance tail

The training run is the cheap part. Base-model deprecations, taxonomy changes, and frontier drift all force re-tunes — price the tail into the TCO or the "savings" are fiction.

✗ Comparing the tune only to its own base model

62% → 92% is a great headline and proves nothing, because nobody was going to ship the untuned 8B model. The comparison that decides is against the cheapest API model that passes the eval — 93% at $0.35 per 1K calls. Three contenders on one eval, or it is advocacy.

✓ Read 50 random samples before every run

Twenty minutes of eyeballing catches label errors, format drift, and coverage holes that no metric will. The single highest-ROI habit in this module.

✓ Sequence, don't refuse

"Fine-tuning is on the roadmap, behind this experiment and these volume triggers" keeps the customer's ambition intact and your architecture honest — and it's usually the truth.

✓ Carry two constants, not an opinion

$675/month of overhead the tuned path adds, $288 saved per million calls per month. Divide them, get 2.3M calls, ask for their volume. A ninety-second answer built from numbers you can derive beats an hour of architecture debate every time.

import anthropic, json

client = anthropic.Anthropic()
raw = json.load(open("raw.json"))

def keep(text, intent):
    msg = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-sonnet-5", max_tokens=5,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"Intents: {INTENTS}\nMessage: {text}\n\n"
            f"Answer KEEP only if this message clearly expresses '{intent}' "
            f"and could not reasonably be another listed intent. "
            f"Otherwise answer DROP. One word."}])

kept = [r for r in raw if keep(r["text"], r["intent"])]

json.dump(kept, open("filtered.json", "w"), indent=1)

SYSTEM = ("Classify the customer message into exactly one intent. "

🧯 Troubleshooting

  • Generation returns numbered lists despite instructions → strip ^\d+[.)]\s* prefixes in code; don't fight it with more prompt.
  • Judge keeps ~100% → your generator is producing textbook examples; add messier styles and ambiguity pressure ("include a red-herring detail from another topic").
  • Rate limits → batch generation calls with a small time.sleep; the judge pass is ~350 cheap calls, fine sequentially.
  • validate.py reports "2 distinct system prompts" → you have two versions of SYSTEM in two files. Move it into one constants.py imported by the generator, the validator and the eval harness, then regenerate the affected rows.
  • Decontamination removes 30+ rows → your generator is producing paraphrases of the same handful of seeds. Raise the style variety and the number of intents-per-call rather than lowering the threshold; a dataset that only survives a loose threshold was never clean.

from together import Together

tg, cl = Together(), anthropic.Anthropic()

def run_together(model, text):
    r = tg.chat.completions.create(model=model, max_tokens=10,
        messages=[{"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM},
                  {"role": "user", "content": text}])
    return r.choices[0].message.content.strip()

def run_claude(text):
    r = cl.messages.create(model="claude-haiku-4-5", max_tokens=10,
        system=SYSTEM + f" Valid intents: {LABELS}",
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": text}])

def evaluate(name, fn):
    hits, times = 0, []
    for e in EVAL:
        text, gold = e["messages"][1]["content"], e["messages"][2]["content"]
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        pred = fn(text)
        times.append(time.perf_counter() - t0)
        hits += int(gold in pred)   # tolerant match; log misses for review
    times.sort()
    p50 = times[len(times)//2]
    print(f"{name:28s} acc={hits/len(EVAL):.2%}  p50={p50*1000:.0f}ms")

BASE = "meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-Reference"
FT   = "<your job.output_name here>"

evaluate("fine-tuned 8B",         lambda t: run_together(FT, t))
