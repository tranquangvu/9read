KPI, ROI & Value Measurement

Define a baseline, success metrics, cost-benefit and measurable production impact — so that the sentence "the pilot worked" can be replaced by a number the customer's finance team accepts.

Why it matters for an FDE: Customers buy outcomes, not technology — an FDE must speak the language of money.
Part I · The buyer's question

Engineers lose deals by talking features

Every budget holder, in every meeting, is silently running one calculation: "what do I get, for what I spend, by when?" Everything you say is scored against that question. An engineer who answers with architecture is answering a question nobody asked — and the sponsor quietly moves the money to a project whose value they can explain to their own boss.

What the engineer saysWhat the buyer hearsWhat to say instead
"We use a hybrid retrieval pipeline with reranking."Cost, complexity, risk"Answer lookup drops from 6 min to 40 s — 92 staff-hours a month."
"Extraction accuracy is 94%.""So it's wrong 1 in 16 times?""94% auto-posts; 6% routes to review. Net: 3.5 → 1.2 min per invoice."
"It'll save a huge amount of time."Unfalsifiable — therefore ignored"164 hours/month at $28 fully loaded = $55K/yr, ±20%."
"The pilot was a success.""Says who?""First-pass match rate went 41% → 76% on 4,187 real invoices in 6 weeks."
"We should also add agents next."Scope creep with no business case"Phase 2 targets the $29K discount leakage — 3 weeks, payback 5 months."

The FDE's unique advantage

A management consultant can build an ROI model, but they estimate the technical side. A backend engineer can build the system, but they rarely price the outcome. The FDE is the only person in the room who can instrument the system and read the meter — you can go into the database, count the actual events, and produce a number that nobody has to take on faith. That is not a soft skill bolted onto engineering; it is engineering pointed at the P&L.

The FD4 reflex: every time you are about to say "it works", finish the sentence with "…which is worth $X per year, measured as Y, and here is the query that produced it." If you cannot finish it, you have a demo (FD2), not a business case.

Three kinds of value — and which ones a CFO actually accepts

Hard — cost avoidedError/rework cost, duplicate payments, penalty fees, missed early-pay discounts, overtime, vendor licences retiredDirectly from the customer's own ledger — a line item that shrinksHigh — this is real cash; it can be booked

The labor-savings trap. "We save 3 FTE" is the most-claimed and least-believed benefit in the industry, because the customer's headcount rarely drops. Two honest framings survive the CFO: (a) capacity — "the same 3 clerks now absorb 40% volume growth without hire #4, deferring $62K/yr", and (b) redeployment — "160 hours/month move from re-keying to supplier dispute recovery, which returned $7K last quarter". Say which one you mean, out loud, before they ask.

Part II · Baselines

The step everyone skips — and regrets in month six

You cannot prove improvement without a before. And the before must be captured while the old world still exists — the week the pilot goes live, the old numbers become unrecoverable and every value claim for the rest of the engagement collapses into opinion. Baseline capture belongs in discovery (FD1), not in the QBR deck three months later.

The most expensive sentence in AI delivery: "We'll figure out the metrics once it's working." By then the manual process has been partly dismantled, the clerks have changed their habits, the peak-season comparison window is gone, and your only honest answer to "did it work?" is "people seem happier". That answer does not renew a contract.

Measure the current state three ways — then reconcile

Never trust a single source. Each of the three has a known, predictable bias, and the disagreement between them is itself a finding:

MethodHowKnown biasCost
Self-report"How long does one take?" in interviews; short survey to the teamUnder-states routine work (people time the happy path), over-states rare pain30 min
Observation / time studySit with 2–3 users, stopwatch 30–50 real transactions, log start/stop/interruptionsHawthorne effect — observed people work faster and skip shortcuts4–6 h
System dataSQL over audit logs, ticket tables, timestamps, correction records (FN6)Includes idle time — a record open for 40 min was not worked for 40 min2–8 h + access

The worked example used throughout this module — a distributor's accounts-payable invoice ↔ PO matching workflow, 3 clerks, 4,200 invoices/month — reconciled like this:

SourcenMin / invoiceImplied h/moWhy it differs
Clerk self-report3 people2.0140Timed the clean invoices they picture when asked
Time study (3 days)42 obs3.8 mean / 2.4 median / 9.5 p90266Long tail of exceptions dominates the mean
ERP audit log4,1874.6 median elapsed321Elapsed ≠ worked; includes coffee, calls, waiting

Note what the reconciliation bought you: the clerks' number (140 h) would have under-sold your project by 43%, and the raw log (321 h) would have over-sold it by 31% — and the first person to check would have destroyed your credibility for the whole engagement. The middle number, with a stated range and a written method, is worth more than a bigger number you cannot defend.

Pulling the baseline out of the customer's database

The single highest-leverage hour in discovery is read access to one production table. These four queries cover ~80% of the baselines an FDE ever needs (FN6 for the SQL craft, PR8 for getting the access):

baseline_queries.sql
-- 1. VOLUME & SEASONALITY — never model on a single month
SELECT date_trunc('month', created_at) AS month,
       count(*)                        AS invoices,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE match_status = 'auto')   AS auto_matched,
       round(100.0 * count(*) FILTER (WHERE match_status = 'auto')
             / count(*), 1)            AS first_pass_pct
FROM ap_invoice
WHERE created_at >= now() - interval '18 months'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;
-- out: 18 rows. Volume 3,410–5,880/mo; Nov–Dec +38%; first_pass_pct 39–44%
-- → baseline volume = 4,200/mo (12-mo mean), first-pass = 41%

-- 2. CYCLE TIME — elapsed per record, from the audit trail
WITH touches AS (
  SELECT invoice_id,
         min(event_at) FILTER (WHERE event = 'opened')  AS t_open,
         max(event_at) FILTER (WHERE event = 'posted')  AS t_post,
         count(*) FILTER (WHERE event = 'edited')       AS edits
  FROM ap_audit_log
  WHERE event_at >= now() - interval '90 days'
  GROUP BY invoice_id
)
SELECT percentile_cont(0.50) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY t_post - t_open) AS p50,
       percentile_cont(0.90) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY t_post - t_open) AS p90,
       avg(edits) AS avg_edits, count(*) AS n
FROM touches WHERE t_post IS NOT NULL;
-- p50 = 00:04:36 | p90 = 00:21:40 | avg_edits = 2.3 | n = 4187

-- 3. ERROR / REWORK RATE — the correction log is where money hides
SELECT reason_code, count(*) AS n,
       round(100.0 * count(*) / sum(count(*)) OVER (), 1) AS pct,
       round(avg(amount_delta)::numeric, 2)               AS avg_delta_usd
FROM ap_correction
WHERE corrected_at >= now() - interval '12 months'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY n DESC;
-- WRONG_PO      612  41.8%   -184.20   ← the AI-addressable slice
-- QTY_MISMATCH  388  26.5%    -96.40
-- DUPLICATE      71   4.9%   -310.00   ← small n, large unit cost

-- 4. LEAKAGE — money already lost, sitting in plain sight
SELECT count(*) AS missed_discounts,
       round(sum(invoice_amount * discount_pct / 100.0)::numeric, 0) AS usd_left
FROM ap_invoice
WHERE discount_pct > 0 AND paid_at > discount_deadline
  AND created_at >= now() - interval '12 months';
-- 604 | 29412     ← $29.4K/yr walked out the door; nobody had counted it
Run query 4 first. "Money you are already losing that nobody has counted" is the fastest way to earn the right to run the other three. In this engagement it produced a $29K number in the first week — before a single line of AI code existed — and that number bought the data access for everything else (FD11 uses exactly this move in pre-sales).

Honest uncertainty is a credibility multiplier. "3.5 minutes" invites a fight; "3.5 minutes, range 3.0–4.1, here's why" ends one. Give ranges, name your adjustments, list what you could not measure. The FDE who volunteers the weakness of their own number is the one whose numbers get believed for the next three years. Never quote to two decimals what you measured with a stopwatch.
Part III · The metric set

Four levels, one chain, no orphans

Teams argue about metrics because they mix levels: someone says "F1 is 0.91" and someone else says "but is it saving money?" — both correct, neither connected. The fix is a strict hierarchy where every metric has a parent. A model metric with no path to a dollar is an orphan; delete it or justify it.

LevelOwnerWorked chain — AP invoice matchingCadence
1 · Business outcomeSponsor / CFOCost per invoice processed: $2.96 → $1.26
Total AP cost pool $149.3K/yr → $63.7K/yr		Quarterly
2 · Operational KPIAP ManagerFirst-pass match rate 41% → 78% · touch time 3.5 → 1.2 min · exception backlog p90 4.2 → 1.5 days · missed discounts 50 → 12/moWeekly
3 · System qualityFDE / eng teamEnd-to-end match precision ≥99.2% · low-confidence flag rate ≤25% · p95 latency ≤8 s · pipeline success ≥99.5% (PR1)Daily / live
4 · Model metricFDEField-level F1: vendor 0.97 · totals 0.99 · line items 0.91 · calibration ECE ≤0.05 (AI9, FN4)Every eval run

Read the chain downward as a translation and upward as a proof. Downward: "cost per invoice falls because first-pass match rises because line-item F1 crosses 0.90." Upward, when line-item F1 drops to 0.84 in a regression run, you can say exactly what it costs: first-pass drops ~9 points, touch time rises ~0.3 min, that is ≈$8K/yr. That sentence is the entire point of FD4 — it turns an eval number into a business decision.

Leading vs lagging — what you watch weekly vs what you report quarterly

Leading (predicts, moves in days)Lagging (proves, moves in months)

If your leading indicators are green for six weeks and your lagging number is flat, one of two things is true: the value chain has a broken arrow (Part I), or the saved time is being absorbed somewhere invisible. Both are findable — and finding them before the QBR is the job.

Adoption metrics are the early truth

Value = quality × adoption. A 95%-accurate system used for 30% of volume delivers less than an 85%-accurate system used for 90%. Adoption is also the earliest honest signal you have: it moves in week two, while your business outcome moves in month five.

MetricDefinitionHealthy (wk 6)What a bad reading means
Coverage% of eligible transactions processed by the system≥80%People route work around it — usually a trust or edge-case problem
Active usersWeekly actives / eligible users≥75%Champion uses it, the team doesn't — training gap (FD10)
RetentionUsers active in wk 8 who were active in wk 2≥85%Novelty wore off; the value wasn't real for them
Override rate% of AI outputs edited or rejected by the human10–25%Too high = quality gap. Near zero = rubber-stamping — a risk, not a win
Time-to-first-valueSignup → first successful task<10 minOnboarding friction is eating your adoption curve
An override rate of 0% is an alarm, not a trophy. It means reviewers stopped reviewing. Pair it with a spot-audit: sample 20 auto-approved items a week and check them independently. If the audit finds errors the reviewers waved through, your "human in the loop" is decorative — and the risk number in your ROI model just changed sign.

Counter-metrics: what must NOT get worse

Any single metric, pushed hard enough, gets gamed. Every headline KPI ships with a counter-metric and a guard threshold written into the success criteria — not discovered in an incident review.

Instrument on day one, not at the QBR

Retrofitting measurement is 5× the work and always loses the early weeks — exactly the weeks that prove the ramp. Emit one value event per transaction from the very first deploy; it costs an hour and it is the raw material of every number you will ever quote (PR1).

value_events.py
from dataclasses import dataclass, asdict
import time, json, uuid

# One row per transaction. Every FD4 number is a GROUP BY over this table.
@dataclass
class ValueEvent:
    event_id: str; tenant: str; workflow: str          # 'ap_invoice_match'
    ts: float; user_id: str
    outcome: str                                       # auto | reviewed | overridden | failed
    confidence: float                                  # drives the flag-rate KPI
    latency_ms: int
    human_seconds: float | None                        # UI-measured, the touch-time KPI
    baseline_seconds: float                            # 210.0 — frozen at project start
    model: str; input_tokens: int; output_tokens: int  # cost side → PR2
    fields_corrected: int                              # override depth, not just yes/no
    money_at_stake: float | None                        # invoice amount → risk weighting

def emit(**kw) -> None:
    ev = ValueEvent(event_id=str(uuid.uuid4()), ts=time.time(), **kw)
    LOG.info("value_event", extra={"payload": json.dumps(asdict(ev))})

# Weekly KPI roll-up — the exact query behind the customer dashboard
KPI_SQL = """
SELECT date_trunc('week', to_timestamp(ts))                       AS wk,
       count(*)                                                   AS volume,
       round(100.0*count(*) FILTER (WHERE outcome='auto')/count(*),1)      AS first_pass_pct,
       round(100.0*count(*) FILTER (WHERE outcome='overridden')/count(*),1) AS override_pct,
       round(avg(human_seconds)/60.0, 2)                          AS min_per_txn,
       round(sum(baseline_seconds - coalesce(human_seconds,0))/3600.0, 1)  AS hours_saved,
       round(sum(baseline_seconds - coalesce(human_seconds,0))/3600.0 * 28, 0) AS usd_saved,
       round(sum(input_tokens)/1e6*3.0 + sum(output_tokens)/1e6*15.0, 2)  AS llm_usd
FROM value_event WHERE workflow = 'ap_invoice_match'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;
"""
# wk 2026-04-06 | 968 | 74.3 | 18.1 | 1.31 | 141.2 | 3954 | 31.80
# usd_saved / llm_usd = 124× — the ratio that ends the "is AI too expensive?" debate
Freeze baseline_seconds in the code. Storing the baseline on every row means savings are computed at query time from an immutable, agreed constant — no spreadsheet drift, no "which baseline did we use?" six months later, and any auditor can re-derive your headline number from the raw table in one query.
Part IV · The ROI model

Both sides of the ledger, honestly

Most engineer-built ROI models are wrong in the same direction: the benefit side is thorough and the cost side stops at "dev time". Then month seven arrives with an infra bill, a maintenance load and a training cost nobody budgeted, and the project's credibility dies of an accounting wound rather than a technical one. Model the full cost first. A model whose cost side impresses the CFO buys enormous trust for the benefit side.

Payback: the picture that closes the meeting

Sensitivity analysis is not finance theater; it is how an FDE decides where to spend the next sprint.

The model in 40 lines

Build it in a spreadsheet for the customer (they will want to poke the assumptions) and in code for yourself (so scenarios and sensitivity are one loop, not 12 copy-pasted tabs):

roi_model.py
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Case:
    vol_mo: int = 4200;    base_min: float = 3.5;   rate: float = 28.0
    auto: float = 0.78;    t_auto: float = 0.3;     t_exc: float = 4.2
    adoption: float = 0.85
    err_pool: float = 37548;  err_cut: float = 0.62
    disc_pool: float = 29400; disc_cap: float = 0.76
    one_time: float = 38000;  run_yr: float = 19000
    ramp = [0,0, .4,.4, .7,.7, 1,1,1,1,1,1]          # 12-month adoption ramp
    yr1_benefit_factor = 8.2/12                      # = sum(ramp)/12 = 0.683
    yr1_run_factor     = 10/12                       # run costs start when it goes live

def annual_benefit(c: Case) -> float:
    after_min = c.auto * c.t_auto + (1 - c.auto) * c.t_exc
    hrs_saved = c.vol_mo * (c.base_min - after_min) / 60 * 12
    labor     = hrs_saved * c.rate
    return (labor + c.err_pool*c.err_cut + c.disc_pool*c.disc_cap) * c.adoption

def payback_month(c: Case, horizon: int = 36) -> int | None:
    monthly = annual_benefit(c) / 12
    cum = -c.one_time
    for m in range(1, horizon + 1):
        f = c.ramp[m-1] if m <= 12 else 1.0
        cum += monthly * f - c.run_yr / 12 * (f > 0)
        if cum >= 0: return m
    return None

def npv(c: Case, years: int = 3, r: float = 0.10) -> float:
    yr1 = (annual_benefit(c) * c.yr1_benefit_factor
           - c.run_yr * c.yr1_run_factor - c.one_time)
    flows = [yr1] + [annual_benefit(c) - c.run_yr] * (years - 1)
    return sum(f / (1 + r)**(t+1) for t, f in enumerate(flows))

# --- scenarios: every LOW-confidence assumption at its pessimistic end ---
cons = Case(auto=.62, adoption=.70, err_cut=.50, disc_cap=.40,
            one_time=45600, run_yr=22000)
opt  = Case(auto=.88, adoption=.95, err_cut=.75, disc_cap=.90,
            one_time=34000, run_yr=17500)
for name, c in [("conservative", cons), ("expected", Case()), ("optimistic", opt)]:
    print(f"{name:13} benefit=${annual_benefit(c):>9,.0f}  "
          f"payback={payback_month(c):>2} mo  npv=${npv(c):>9,.0f}")
# conservative  benefit=$   49,662  payback=26 mo  npv=$   16,366
# expected      benefit=$   85,601  payback=12 mo  npv=$  109,299
# optimistic    benefit=$  112,935  payback= 9 mo  npv=$  176,535

# --- sensitivity grid: the two assumptions you are least sure of ---
for a in (.60, .70, .78, .88):
    row = [annual_benefit(Case(auto=a, adoption=ad)) - 19000
           for ad in (.50, .70, .85, 1.0)]
    print(a, [f"${v/1000:.1f}K" for v in row])
# 0.6 ['$23.1K', '$39.9K', '$52.6K', '$65.2K']
# 0.7 ['$27.7K', '$46.4K', '$60.4K', '$74.4K']
# 0.78 ['$31.4K', '$51.5K', '$66.6K', '$81.7K']
# 0.88 ['$35.9K', '$57.9K', '$74.4K', '$90.9K']

The spreadsheet twin the customer gets: one Assumptions sheet (every yellow cell is an input), one Model sheet (no hard-coded numbers, only references), one Scenarios sheet. Useful formulas: =NPV(0.10, C5:E5)+B5 · =IRR(B5:E5) · payback via =MATCH(TRUE, INDEX(cum>=0,0), 0) · scenarios via a data table on the two sensitivity axes. Ship it unlocked — a model they can break is a model they believe.

Part V · Proving it in production

From projection to evidence

Everything so far was a forecast. Value measurement is what turns it into a fact — and it needs its own plan, agreed and signed before go-live, because measurement designed after the fact is always suspected of being designed to flatter.

Measure everything in your own telemetry for speed — but let the number that appears on the renewal slide come from their books.

Study design — and the two things that will fool you

DesignWhen to useStrengthMain threat
Before / afterSmall team, one workflow, no way to splitCheap, always availableSeasonality & concurrent changes

Trap 1 · Seasonality

Your pilot ran Jan–Feb; the baseline came from Nov–Dec peak (volume ×1.38, error rate ×1.6). Half your "improvement" is the calendar. Fix: compare like months, or use a year-over-year ratio, or keep a control group — and always state the windows.

Trap 2 · Selection effects

Pilot users volunteered — they are the fastest, keenest clerks, and the easy invoice types got routed to the tool first. Your measured lift will not survive full rollout. Fix: compare like-for-like transaction mixes, and discount pilot results ~15–25% when extrapolating.

Trap 3 · Concurrent change

The customer also hired two clerks and upgraded the ERP in the same quarter. Log every co-intervention in the measurement plan as it happens; attribution arguments are unwinnable retrospectively.

Trap 4 · Novelty decay

Weeks 1–3 look spectacular because everyone is paying attention. Never quote a run-rate from fewer than 6 weeks of steady-state usage; report the week-8 number as the headline and show the curve.

The QBR narrative: numbers + one story + next bet

1 · THE NUMBER — one slide, one headline: "AP cost per invoice $2.96 → $1.55; annualized $71K against an $85.6K plan; 83% of target." Include the miss.
2 · THE CHAIN — the three operational KPIs that produced it, each against baseline and target, with the trend line.
3 · THE STORY — one named, human anecdote: "Trang's team cleared the month-end backlog by the 3rd for the first time in two years; she reassigned two afternoons a week to supplier disputes, which recovered $7,100 last quarter." Numbers convince the analyst; the story is what the sponsor repeats to the CFO.
4 · THE HONEST GAP — what missed and why, with a dated fix: "discount recovery is at 31% not 76% — Procurement's approval SLA, not the model. Joint fix proposed, owner named, revisit 15 Sep."
5 · THE NEXT BET — the following use case with its own baseline and a one-line ROI: "freight invoice reconciliation, baseline measured, $46K/yr, 3 weeks, payback 5 months." FD11 — this is how an engagement renews itself.

When the numbers disappoint

Sooner or later the mid-point review shows $31K annualized against an $85K plan. What you do in the next 48 hours defines your career more than any model you have ever built.

StepDoNever
1 · Surface it firstYou raise it before they notice — same week, in writingLet the QBR be where they discover it

Moving the baseline is the one unforgivable act. The moment a customer suspects that your "before" number was adjusted to flatter the "after", every number you have ever given them is retroactively worthless — and so is every number your company gives them. Baselines are frozen, signed, and versioned. If a baseline genuinely must be corrected, correct it loudly, in writing, with the reason, and restate both the old and new results.

The score-1 answer: "Yes! Accuracy is 94% and the team says it's much faster." The sponsor nods, writes nothing down, and the phase-2 budget quietly does not appear.

The score-3 answer takes ninety seconds. "Cost per invoice is $1.55 against a $2.96 baseline — that's $71K annualized, 83% of plan, and we clear payback in month 12 as modelled. Two of three KPIs are at target: first-pass match 76% versus 78%, touch time 1.31 min versus 1.2. The third missed: discount recovery is 31%, not 76%. That's not the model — extraction F1 is 0.93 — it's Procurement's three-day approval SLA eating the 10-day window. Fixing it is worth $11K/yr, it's a workflow change not a build, and Minh has agreed to own it with a review on 15 September. Meanwhile the same baseline method applied to freight invoices says $46K/yr for three weeks of work."

What just happened: the customer got a number from their own finance close, a fair account of a miss, a named owner, and a costed next step. The phase-2 statement of work was signed eleven days later. The model was not what won it — the fact that a miss was reported precisely was. Precision about failure is the strongest possible evidence that the successes are real.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ No baseline

The single most common failure. Without a before, your after is an anecdote. Capture it in discovery week one — the old world stops existing the day you ship.

✗ Cost side that stops at dev days

Omitting maintenance (15–20%/yr), integration, evals, training and infra. The CFO adds them for you, in the meeting, and now every number of yours is suspect.

✗ Fake precision

"$184,732.40/yr in savings" from a 42-observation stopwatch study. Round to the precision you actually measured and give a range; false decimals destroy more credibility than a smaller number ever would.

✗ Ignoring adoption in the model

Modelling 100% coverage from day one. Real ramps are 40/70/100 over six months, and the sensitivity grid usually shows adoption matters more than accuracy.

✗ Only vanity metrics

Queries served, tokens processed, "engagement". None of them map to a dollar. If a metric has no parent in the chain, it is decoration — delete it from the dashboard.

✗ Counting hours as cash

"3 FTE saved" when headcount will not change. Convert to capacity or redeployment and say so — the honest framing is the one that gets funded twice.

✓ Get the rate from Finance, in writing

Never invent the fully loaded hourly rate. Ask the finance manager, quote them, and cite them in the method note. Their number cannot be argued with in their own building.

✓ Ship the unlocked spreadsheet

Give the customer a model they can poke, with every assumption in a yellow input cell. Sponsors who change your numbers themselves become co-authors — and co-authors defend the case internally when you are not in the room.

AIFDE Mastery · FD4 KPI, ROI & Value Measurement
Lab 1 · ~2 h
Baseline measurement, three ways

🎯 Objective

Experience — physically, with a stopwatch — how far apart the three measurement methods land on the same workflow, and produce a methodology note that would survive a finance review.

📦 What you'll produce

  • A time-study log of 30+ real transactions
  • A reconciliation table (self-report vs observation vs system data)
  • A one-page baseline-method-note.md with ranges, adjustments and known gaps

🔬 Steps

1
Pick a real, repetitive workflow. A client's if you're on an engagement; otherwise an internal one — PR review turnaround, sprint ticket triage, client status-report writing, invoice approval, candidate CV screening. It must be (a) done many times, (b) done by someone you can watch, (c) leave a data trail somewhere.
2
Self-report first, before you contaminate it. Ask two or three people: "How long does one take, typically? How many do you do a week? How often does one go wrong?" Write the answers down verbatim with names. This is your optimistic anchor.
3
Time study — 30+ observations. Sit with them (or screen-share) and log every transaction. Do not help, do not comment. Log format: 
#   start     end       min   type      interrupted?  notes
01  09:12:04  09:14:31  2.5   standard  no
02  09:14:40  09:26:18  11.6  exception yes(2)        vendor PDF unreadable, called supplier
03  09:26:55  09:29:02  2.1   standard  no
...
# Report mean AND median AND p90 — a workflow with a fat tail is
# mis-described by any single number. Count the interruptions too.
Two non-obvious rules: include the exceptions (they are where AI value hides or dies), and note the time of day — Monday 9am is not Thursday 4pm.
4
System data. Find the trail: ERP audit log, Jira transitions, Git timestamps, ticket table, email metadata. Write the query (Part II has four templates). If you truly have no access, use a proxy — calendar entries, file modification times, message timestamps — and label it as a proxy. 
-- generic cycle-time template, adapt the table/column names
SELECT percentile_cont(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY finished - started) AS p50,
       percentile_cont(0.9) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY finished - started) AS p90,
       count(*) AS n, min(started) AS window_start, max(finished) AS window_end
FROM   <the_work_table>
WHERE  started >= now() - interval '90 days';
5
Reconcile — the actual lab. Build the Part II table with your three numbers and force yourself to explain every gap: Did self-report ignore exceptions? Did they speed up while watched (compare your first 10 observations to your last 10)? Does system elapsed include idle? Then pick ONE reconciled number with a range, and write the sentence that justifies it.
6
Write the method note using the Part II template: scope, window, sources, headline with ranges, rate used, adjustments made, known gaps, sign-off. Then get it reviewed by the person whose work you measured — "is this a fair description of your work?" Their correction is the most valuable output of the whole lab.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • "They work differently when I watch." Correct — that's the Hawthorne effect and it is why you triangulate. Compare your first vs last 10 observations to size it, then trim 5–10%.
  • "No system data exists." Then say so in the note and widen your range. A documented ±25% beats a fabricated point estimate.
  • "The three numbers are 2×, 4× and 7× apart." You are measuring three different things — check the boundaries. "Start" for one source is probably not "start" for another. Redefine the transaction boundary explicitly and re-run.
  • "It's too variable to measure." Segment it: standard vs exception, by vendor, by type. Variability is a finding — and usually the segment boundary is exactly where AI should be applied.

✅ Done when

You have three numbers that disagree, a written explanation of why, one reconciled figure with a range, and a method note the person you observed agrees is fair. If your three numbers agreed closely, you probably measured the same source three times — go back to step 2.
Lab 2 · ~1.5 h
Metric chain design

🎯 Objective

Turn a vague success statement ("make the process better") into a four-level metric chain where every level is defined, instrumented and connected to the level above it — including the counter-metrics that stop the whole thing being gamed.

📦 What you'll produce

  • A filled metric-chain table with baseline and target per row
  • Counter-metrics with guard thresholds
  • An event schema + the SQL that computes every KPI from it

🔬 Steps

1
Take your Lab 1 workflow (or the AP case if you must). Write the level-1 business outcome as a unit-cost or unit-time: "$ per invoice", "hours per report", "days per candidate hired". Unit metrics beat totals because they survive volume changes — a total that fell because volume fell is not a win.
2
Fill all four levels. Every row needs five things — name · definition · baseline · target · source: 
LEVEL 1 · BUSINESS OUTCOME    owner: sponsor      cadence: quarterly
  cost per invoice | AP cost centre / invoice count | $2.96 | $1.26 | Finance close

LEVEL 2 · OPERATIONAL KPI     owner: AP manager   cadence: weekly
  first-pass match | outcome='auto' / total       |   41% |   78% | value_event
  touch time       | median human_seconds/60      |  3.5m |  1.2m | value_event
  backlog age p90  | now - oldest open exception  | 4.2 d | 1.5 d | ERP

LEVEL 3 · SYSTEM QUALITY      owner: FDE          cadence: daily
  match precision  | correct auto-matches / auto  |    -- | ≥99.2%| audit sample
  flag rate        | confidence < 0.85            |    -- | ≤25%  | value_event
  p95 latency      | request → suggestion         |    -- | ≤8 s  | tracing (PR1)

LEVEL 4 · MODEL METRIC        owner: FDE          cadence: every eval
  field F1 (line items) | golden set n=150        |  0.78 | ≥0.91 | AI9 harness
  calibration ECE       | confidence vs accuracy  |    -- | ≤0.05 | AI9 harness
The discipline: if you cannot name the source, the metric does not exist yet. "We'll get it from the system" is not a source.
3
Draw the arrows. For each level-2 KPI, write one sentence connecting it upward: "first-pass match ↑ 37 points → 164 h/mo saved → $46.8K/yr at 85% adoption". Any KPI you cannot connect gets deleted or promoted to a counter-metric. This step usually kills 2–3 metrics somebody was fond of.
4
Counter-metrics. For each headline KPI ask: "If I were lazy, cynical or under pressure, how would I make this number look good without doing the work?" Every answer becomes a counter-metric with a numeric guard. Minimum three. If you cannot think of a way to game a metric, ask Claude: 
Here is my metric chain and the incentives of each role. For each
headline KPI, list three realistic ways a busy team would make the
number improve WITHOUT delivering the underlying value, and propose
one counter-metric per gaming path with a specific threshold.
5
Specify the instrumentation. Write the event schema (Part III has one) and, for every KPI in your table, the exact SQL that computes it. If a metric needs a field your event doesn't carry, add the field now — this is the whole point of doing it before the build. 
# the completeness test: for each row in your table, answer
metric → which event field(s)? → which aggregation? → which dashboard tile?
# any "manual export from X monthly" is a metric that will quietly die
# in month 3. Automate it or drop it to a quarterly manual review.
6
The 30-second test. Show the chain to an engineer and to a non-technical colleague. The engineer must be able to implement level 3–4 without asking a question; the non-technical person must be able to explain level 1–2 back to you. Rewrite until both pass.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • Level 1 is not measurable by the customer. Very common — "customer satisfaction" with no survey running. Either start the instrument now (so a baseline exists) or pick a level-1 metric they already track.
  • Everything ladders to "time saved". Fine, but then find the second benefit: errors, leakage, throughput, risk. Single-benefit cases are fragile — one challenged assumption and the whole case collapses.
  • Twelve metrics. You have a dashboard, not a chain. Four levels, 1 + 3 + 3 + 2 metrics maximum. Anything else is a drill-down, not a headline.

✅ Done when

Every metric has a parent, a baseline, a target, a named source and a query; every headline KPI has a counter-metric with a numeric guard; and both the engineer test and the non-technical test pass. This table drops straight into the Lab 4 package and into the success criteria of your FD1 problem statement.
Lab 3 · ~2 h
The ROI model

🎯 Objective

Build a cost-benefit model you would put your name on in front of a CFO — complete cost side, three scenarios, sensitivity on the two assumptions you are least sure of, and a payback curve — then have it attacked.

📦 What you'll produce

  • A spreadsheet (Assumptions / Model / Scenarios sheets) or the Python equivalent
  • A 4×4 sensitivity grid with the break-even contour marked
  • A cumulative cash-flow chart with the payback month annotated
  • A written list of your three weakest assumptions

🔬 Steps

1
Assumptions sheet first — every input in one place. Nothing anywhere else in the model may be a literal number. This is what makes scenarios one edit instead of twelve. 
ASSUMPTIONS                     value    source / confidence
volume per month                4,200    ERP query, 12-mo mean · HIGH
baseline minutes per txn          3.5     time study n=42 (±0.6) · MED
fully loaded hourly rate         $28      Finance Mgr, email 17 Feb · HIGH
auto-handle rate (target)         78%     prototype on 150 samples · MED
minutes: auto-handled             0.3     measured in prototype UI · MED
minutes: exception                4.2     time study, exception subset · MED
adoption year 1                   85%     ← WEAKEST ASSUMPTION · LOW
error pool $/yr              $37,548     ap_correction 12 mo · HIGH

discount pool $/yr           $29,400     ERP paid_at vs deadline · HIGH

build effort (person-weeks)      10.0     estimate + 25% UI buffer · MED
blended rate per person-week   $3,800     rate card · HIGH
maintenance % of build/yr          18%    firm standard · MED
discount rate                      10%    customer hurdle, confirmed · HIGH
Tag every row HIGH/MED/LOW. The LOW rows are your sensitivity axes — chosen by evidence, not by taste.
2
Build the cost side completely using the Part IV table as a checklist. Score yourself: discovery, build, integration, eval set, change management, tokens, infra, licences, maintenance, ongoing evals, customer-side effort. Miss any of the eleven and a real CFO will find it. Compute token cost from first principles — volume × calls × tokens × price — and show the arithmetic in a cell comment (PR2).
3
Benefit side, per line, traced to a baseline. Every benefit row must reference a row in your Lab 1 method note. If a benefit has no baseline behind it, either measure it or set it to $0 and label it "unpriced" — an explicit zero is a credibility asset.
4
Monthly cash flow with an adoption ramp, not an annual average. Model 0/0/40/40/70/70/100…, subtract run costs from the month the system is live, and find the first month cumulative ≥ 0. 
month  one-time  benefit  run    net       cumulative   adoption

# The two months of build cost with zero benefit are why an "annual
# average" model reports payback ~3 months earlier than reality.
5
Three scenarios. Conservative = every LOW assumption at its pessimistic end and a 20% build overrun. Optimistic = the version you'd bet on if everything went right. Never let the conservative case be "the expected case minus 10%" — that is a decoration, and experienced buyers recognize it instantly.
6
Sensitivity grid on your two LOW-confidence assumptions (4×4). Mark the break-even contour — the cells where payback exceeds the customer's hurdle. Then answer, in one sentence: "which of these two assumptions should we spend next month's effort improving?" That is the sentence that makes the model a decision tool rather than a slide.
7
Chart the payback curve — cumulative cash flow, zero line, payback month annotated, ramp shaded. One chart, no legend needed. (Part IV's SVG is the reference layout.)
8
Survive the grilling. Present to a colleague, or run the adversarial prompt: 
You are the CFO of a mid-size distributor. I will present an ROI model
for an AI project. Attack it the way a real CFO would:
- find every cost line I have omitted or under-sized
- challenge whether "hours saved" becomes cash in your P&L
- question my attribution: how do you know YOUR system caused this?
- ask what happens if adoption is half of what I assumed
- ask what happens after year 1 when the vendor leaves
- press me on which single assumption the whole case depends on
Be skeptical but fair. After 10 questions, tell me whether you would
fund it, at what size, and what evidence would change your mind.
Log every question you could not answer cleanly. Those gaps — not the model — are the deliverable of this step.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • ROI comes out at 900%. You have double-counted (labor savings and error savings often overlap), used revenue instead of margin, or ignored adoption. Absurd numbers get the whole model dismissed — sanity-check against the customer's total spend on that workflow: your benefit cannot exceed the cost pool that exists.
  • Conservative case is negative. Good — that is real information. Scope down to the highest-value slice and re-model. A smaller project that pays back in 9 months beats a big one that might.
  • "Adoption" feels unknowable. It is knowable within a range: comparable rollouts, the pilot's own coverage curve, how many users are mandated vs voluntary. Give a range and put it on the sensitivity axis.
  • The customer disputes the rate. Never argue — use theirs. If they say $22 not $28, re-run in front of them; the model still works and you just gained an ally.

✅ Done when

All eleven cost lines present, three genuinely different scenarios, a sensitivity grid with a marked break-even contour, a payback chart, and a written list of your three weakest assumptions with what would firm each one up. Bonus: you changed a delivery decision because of what the grid showed.

🔬 Final steps

1
Assemble all three components into one document with a half-page executive summary on top: investment, benefit, payback, the one risk. Sponsors read the top; CFOs read the tables.
2
Consistency pass. Every number in Component 2 must be derivable from Component 1. Pick three at random and trace them back. Inconsistent numbers between your own pages is the fastest way to lose a business-case review.
3
Present it in 5 minutes to a senior colleague or the real stakeholder, in this order: payback → the three benefit lines → the conservative case → the weakest assumption → the measurement plan. Practise the last one aloud: "here's how you'll know in six months whether I was right."
4
Get a decision, not applause. Close with the ask: "Do you accept this baseline as the official before-state, and this measurement plan as how we'll judge it?" A package nobody had to agree to has not been tested. Revise once from their pushback and version it.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • "This is too much detail." Then your executive summary is not doing its job. The detail stays — it is what makes the summary believable — but the first half page must stand alone.
  • They challenge the baseline. Excellent, and expected. Update it with them, version it (v1.3), restate the affected results, and note who requested the change. A baseline improved by the customer is a baseline they now own.
  • No real customer to present to. Use an internal Golden Owl workflow and present to a delivery lead. The mechanics are identical, and internal cases have the advantage that you can actually verify the numbers.

✅ Done when

The package exists as one file, every ROI number traces to a documented baseline, three scenarios and a sensitivity grid are present, the measurement plan names owners and a decision gate, and a real reviewer has either accepted it or forced a revision you made. Keep it as your template — at score 3 you produce one of these per engagement in under a day, and it becomes the spine of every FD5 readout and every FD11 proposal that follows.
