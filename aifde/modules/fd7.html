Embedded Working & Client Trust

Work inside customer teams, navigate org politics, build champions and clear customer-side blockers.

Why it matters for an FDE: "Forward deployed" in the literal sense — success depends on becoming part of the customer's team.
Part I · The posture

What "embedded" actually means

The role name comes from Palantir, and it was literal: engineers were deployed forward — out of the product org, into the customer's building, sitting at the desk next to the analyst whose job the software was supposed to change. Not in a project room down the corridor. Not on a weekly call. Next to the user, watching the work happen, shipping against what they saw that morning.

Most of the value of that arrangement has nothing to do with geography — you can be embedded from Ho Chi Minh City into a Melbourne team, and you can be physically on site and still completely outside. Embedded is a set of daily behaviors. The clearest way to see it is next to the two adjacent postures most engineers default to:

The outcome that tells you it worked

Forget sentiment surveys. The measurable signal of an embedded engineer is simple:

You are invited to the meetings nobody invites vendors to. Their sprint planning. Their incident postmortem. The "can you sanity-check this before we take it to the board" ping. The re-org conversation where someone says "wait, let's ask what this does to the AI work." Track it literally: count, per month, the meetings you were invited to that were not about your project. Zero for two months means you are a vendor with good manners.

Three secondary signals worth watching: (1) people send you problems that aren't yours, because you're the person who gets things unstuck; (2) your champion forwards you internal threads you weren't on; (3) someone defends your project in a meeting you weren't in. That third one is the whole game — trust is what your reputation does while you're asleep.

The opposite failure: going native

Embedding has a failure mode on the far side, and it is the one nobody warns juniors about. Over-identification: you become so much a member of their team that you lose the outside view that made you valuable. Symptoms are specific and easy to self-diagnose:

  • You stop delivering unwelcome assessments — no "this use case won't hold at 10× volume", no "that date isn't real" (FD6).
  • You defend their decisions to your own firm instead of reporting them accurately.
  • You absorb scope silently to avoid an awkward conversation, and your firm eats the cost.
  • You take on their internal work — their backlog, their on-call, their unrelated migration — because saying no feels disloyal.
  • You start using "we" to mean them and "they" to mean your employer. Language leads behavior.
The monthly loyalty test — one question, answered honestly: "In the last four weeks, what did I tell this customer that they did not want to hear?"
A blank answer is not evidence of a smooth engagement; it is evidence that you have stopped being useful. Your value to a customer is precisely the part of you that is not them. Embedded means inside the team, outside the groupthink.
Part II · Entry

The first two weeks decide the next six months

You have roughly ten working days of "new person" grace, during which you can ask anything without looking incompetent and meet anyone without needing a reason. Spend it deliberately. The entry plan has three tracks running in parallel: people (who you meet, in what order), credibility (the first visible win), and plumbing (accounts and access, which have the longest lead times and therefore must start on day one).

Track 1 — The meeting order (and why the order matters)

Meet people in an order that makes each conversation better than the last. Sponsor first for the mandate, champion second to draw the real map, users third with a map in hand, IT fourth so you ask precise questions, skeptic last — after you know enough to take them seriously.

Two commitments hide in there — presence and a weekly report-back. Keep both; that is your first deposit.

Track 2 — The week-one credibility deposit

Nothing you say in week one carries weight. Something you fix in week one does. The purpose is not the fix — it's the demonstration that you are the kind of person around whom small annoying things stop existing. Pick it from what you hear in the shadow sessions, and hold it to five criteria:

Visible to the people who feel the pain

A backend cleanup nobody sees earns nothing. The operator must notice on Monday.

Ships inside a week without an approval chain

If it needs a change-advisory board, it is not a week-one win — it's the second win.

Zero blast radius

Additive, reversible, no production write path. A week-one incident costs a quarter of trust.

Lands in a tool they already open

A new URL to remember is a tax. A better column in the sheet they live in is a gift.

And the fifth: it must be theirs, not yours. Fix something they complained about, not something you found offensive. Real examples that have worked: a script that turns the 40-minute Monday export ritual into a scheduled email · a Slack command that answers the question three people ask the ops lead every day · fixing the report that has silently double-counted since March · a one-page runbook for the process only one person knows. None of these are AI. That is the point — credibility is fungible, and you spend it later on the hard AI conversations.

Track 3 — Adopt their rituals, don't import yours

The fastest way to stay an outsider is to arrive with a process. They have a standup at an awkward hour, a ticket hygiene convention you find primitive, a deploy freeze on Thursdays, and a channel where decisions actually get made that is not the channel named "decisions". Learn all of it before proposing a single change. Your process improvements will be welcome in month three — funded by the credibility you built by shutting up in month one.

The rituals inventory — fill this in during week one
CADENCE — standup (time, format, camera on/off), sprint boundaries, demo day, retro, the recurring meeting where decisions actually land.
CHANNELS — which tool for what: the public channel, the team channel, the DM culture, the one place where "it's not real until it's in the ticket" applies. Note response-time norms.
HOURS — core hours, timezone spread, whether evening messages are normal or rude, public holidays for the next quarter (FD12).
CODE — branch naming, review expectations, who must approve, the test suite everyone quietly skips, the deploy window and the freeze.
LANGUAGE — their vocabulary for their domain (FD9). Keep a glossary from day one; using their word for their thing is worth more than a diagram.
SOCIAL — lunch, coffee, the Friday thing. The 20 minutes that produce more context than any status meeting. Show up.

Track 4 — Access: request on day one, plan for the wait

Every embedded engagement loses its first fortnight the same way: waiting for accounts. The lead times are institutional, not personal, and they do not compress because you asked politely. Send the complete list on day one — one message, everything at once, with a business reason per item, because IT batches approvals:

AccessTypical lead timeWhat you do while waiting

Make the wait visible, immediately. Access delay is the single most common cause of a month-two schedule slip — and if you absorb it quietly, it becomes your slip. On day two, send the sponsor a one-liner: "Requested X/Y/Z today. Standard lead time for Z here is 3–6 weeks; if it lands after {date}, the pilot date moves by the same amount. Can you help push it?" This is FD6 expectation-setting and FD8 ownership in a single sentence, and it costs nothing on day two while costing your credibility on day sixty.
Part III · The org

Reading the room you were dropped into

"Politics" is what engineers call the fact that organizations are made of people with different incentives. It is not dirty and it is not optional — it is the operating environment. The skill is not manipulation; it is accurate perception followed by honest, well-sequenced conversations. Start by drawing two maps: one of who has power and who cares, and one of who actually influences whom.

Map 1 — The power–interest grid

Draw it in week two, then again at every phase change. The two mistakes are drawing it once and treating position as fixed: a champion who delivers a visible win gains real power; a sponsor who gets re-orged loses all of it in an afternoon. And the top-left quadrant is where projects die quietly — the person with power and no interest is not your friend and not your enemy; they are a landmine. Their first real contact with your project must never be a problem.

Map 2 — Formal authority vs real influence

The org chart tells you who signs. It does not tell you whose opinion ends the debate. Overlay the two:

Solid lines are the org chart. Dotted lines are what actually happens — and they are where your engagement is won. Four detectors for real influence, usable in your first three meetings:

  • Who gets cited when absent. "We'd need to check with Mai" — said by three different people about someone with no title — is the loudest signal in the building.
  • Whose objection ends the discussion. Watch one meeting for who can say "I don't think so" and have the topic simply stop.
  • Whose messages get answered in minutes. Response latency is an org chart drawn by behavior.
  • Who was in the room when the budget was agreed. Ask your champion outright. The attendee list of that meeting is the real decision group for the next twelve months.

Growing a champion

A champion is not someone who likes you. A champion is someone who spends their own internal credibility on your project — and that is a real cost to them, which means the relationship is an exchange, not a friendship. Champions need four things, and you should be able to name when you last supplied each:

Wins they can show

Ammunition, in their format: a number, a screenshot, a quote from a happy operator. Hand them the slide, don't make them build it. "Here's the two-line update for your leadership meeting Thursday" is the single highest-leverage message you can send.

Cover when things slip

When the date moves, they must never look like the person who didn't know. Tell them before anyone else, with the reason and the recovery plan, and let them carry the message — or take the blame publicly yourself.

Credit, generously

"The design came out of Linh's team's feedback" — in front of their boss. You are leaving in six months; their career isn't. Credit given away comes back as access.

Zero surprises

A champion blindsided in a meeting stops being a champion that day. There is no faster way to destroy the relationship, and it is entirely preventable with a five-minute pre-brief.

Diagnosing a weak champion — enthusiasm is not influence. Warning signs: they can't get you 30 minutes with the sponsor · their asks to other teams go unanswered · they describe decisions as things that happen to them · they're new (under six months) · their own manager isn't bought in. A weak champion is not a reason to quit — it is a reason to build a second one, ideally someone with scar tissue and tenure, and to make sure the sponsor hears from you directly at least monthly (FD5).

The skeptic and the threatened — different people, different playbooks

Teams lump all resistance together as "change management" and lose. There are three distinct kinds, and only one of them is about your technology:

TypeWhat they actually sayWhat it meansYour move
The scarred"We tried this in 2023."They were promised something and it didn't land. The grievance is about being misled, not about AI.Ask for the full story and listen to all of it. Then name explicitly what you are doing differently — and set a smaller promise you will certainly keep (FD6).
The technical skeptic"It'll hallucinate on our edge cases."Usually correct, and giving you a free risk analysis.Convert them into your evaluation designer. "Give me the 20 hardest cases you've got" makes them a co-author (AI9).
The threatened"I'm just not sure the timing is right."They believe this reduces their headcount, their budget, their expertise, or their reason to exist.The playbook below. Never the demo. A better demo makes a threatened person more dangerous, not less.

The threatened stakeholder is the most under-managed relationship in AI projects. They rarely oppose you openly — that would look anti-progress. Instead the resistance is procedural and deniable: their team is too busy for the pilot this month, the data extract needs another review, an edge case must be resolved first, the meeting gets moved. Recognize the pattern by its shape: never a "no", always a "not yet", every time.

The threatened-stakeholder conversation — a five-move playbook
1 · GO ALONE — one-to-one, no sponsor, no slides, their office or a coffee. An audience forces them to defend a position; privacy lets them have doubts.
2 · NAME IT FIRST — "Can I be direct? When a project like this arrives, the honest question is what it means for your team. I'd rather talk about that than pretend it isn't the subject." Saying it out loud removes the thing that makes them dangerous: having to pretend.
3 · SHUT UP — then genuinely listen. Do not rebut. Half of what you hear will be legitimate operational risk you hadn't priced. Write it down in front of them.
4 · OFFER A ROLE, NOT A REASSURANCE — "your team is safe" is worthless (you can't promise it, and they know it). A role is real: owner of the guardrails, author of the exception rules, approver of what the AI is allowed to do unsupervised, judge on the eval set. Their expertise becomes the thing that makes the system trustworthy — which is both politically effective and technically true.
5 · CLOSE ON ONE CONCRETE THING — leave with a single specific commitment, this week, in writing: "you'll define the three cases the AI must always escalate, by Friday." Involvement changes positions; argument doesn't.
Why the guardrail hand-off works. A person who wrote the rules that constrain the system now owns part of its safety story. Their status shifts from "person being automated" to "person whose judgment is encoded". That is not a trick — it is the correct design. The domain expert genuinely should own the escalation policy, and the systems that survive contact with reality are the ones where they did (AI9, FD3).

Coalitions beat persuasion

Junior FDEs try to convince the room. Senior ones build agreement one conversation at a time, before the room ever meets. The pattern is boring and reliable: pre-wire the decision. Before any meeting where something is decided, talk to each person who matters individually — hear their objection privately, adapt the proposal where they're right, and secure a "yes, I can support that" one-to-one. Then the meeting becomes a ratification, not a debate. Debates in rooms produce positions people must then defend publicly; conversations in corridors produce agreements.

The pre-wire question: "I'm taking this to Thursday's meeting. Before I do — what's the thing about it that would bother you, so I can fix it or address it up front?" People will tell you. And an objection you incorporated is an objection that becomes their contribution.

Blockers: route around, or escalate?

Clearing customer-side blockers is half of what an embedded engineer is paid for — and every blocker is a person with a reason. Diagnose before you act:

Why they're blockingResponse
Overloaded — genuinely no timeReduce the ask until it fits: do the work for them and bring it for a 10-minute review. Most "blockers" are capacity, and capacity is solvable with your own hands.
Doesn't understand the askRewrite it in their language, with the exact action, the exact deadline, and the reason it matters to them. Vagueness reads as low priority.
Legitimate concern you missedNot a blocker — a finding. Change the plan and tell everyone you did, crediting them.
Not their priority / no incentiveFind the path where it becomes their priority: their boss's goal, a pain of theirs you can solve in the same change. Trade, don't push.
ThreatenedThe five-move playbook above. Escalation here makes it permanently worse — you'll win the decision and lose the adoption.
Structurally cannot say yes (policy)Route around: find the person who can approve, or the compliant alternative path. Frame it as helping them stay inside policy, never as going over their head.

Escalate only when the blocker is genuinely on the critical path, you have tried at least twice, and the cost of delay is quantifiable. Then escalate the problem, not the person — and never as an ambush: 

Hi Minh — heads-up before I raise this Thursday, because I don't want
it to look like it came out of nowhere.

The dev-DB extract has been open since 12 Aug (18 days). I know your
team is mid-migration, so this isn't a complaint — it's just now on the
critical path: without it, the pilot slips from 30 Sep by roughly the
same number of days.

Two options I can see:
  a) an anonymized 500-row sample this week, full extract after your
     migration — unblocks us at ~80%;
  b) I do the extract myself under your supervision, you keep approval.

If neither works, I'll flag the date impact in Thursday's steering so
the delay is visible to everyone — and I'd rather present it with your
side of the story in it. Which option do you want me to take in?

Four things make that message safe: it warns before it escalates, it offers a way to say yes, it states impact in dates rather than judgments, and it explicitly offers to represent their constraints fairly. You can escalate a hundred times this way without ever burning a relationship.

Part IV · Trust

Trust is an account, not a feeling

Treat every relationship as a balance you can deposit into and withdraw from. The model is useful because it makes two things obvious: trust is built by many small predictable acts rather than one heroic one, and withdrawals are far larger than deposits. It also explains the thing juniors find unfair — that you can do excellent work for three months and lose the room in a single meeting.

Deposits (+)
  • Predictability — the update arrives Friday, every Friday, including the boring weeks. Larger than any single big win.
  • Doing exactly what you said — small promise, kept, visibly. "I'll check and reply by 4pm" then replying at 3:50.
  • Small unrequested favors — the broken query you fixed while you were in there.
  • Transparency about problems — bad news raised by you before they find it. The largest single deposit available.
  • Protecting people in public — taking the hit for a team miss in a meeting where their boss is present.
  • Remembering their context — their release freeze, their kid's name, the objection they raised in June.
Withdrawals (−)
  • Surprises — any bad fact they learn from someone else. Costs ~5 deposits.
  • Blame — "we were blocked by your IT team" in a public forum. Even when true, it costs more than it buys.
  • Unilateral decisions — changing scope, tooling or approach without consultation. Reads as "your opinion is optional".
  • Disappearing — three quiet days during a hard patch. Silence is always interpreted as bad news, and usually correctly.
  • Overpromising to be liked — the agreeable "sure, we can add that" that later becomes a broken date (FD6).
  • Being right, loudly — "I told you in March." Correct, and expensive.

The trust curve of a real engagement

Two things in that curve are worth internalizing. First, the drop at week eight was not caused by the slipped date — it was caused by the four silent days. Dates slip everywhere; silence is a choice. Second, the highest point of the whole engagement comes after an incident. A problem handled visibly well is the most powerful trust-building event available to you, which is why the customers who love you most are usually the ones where something went wrong (PR11).

The four behaviors that separate a trusted FDE

01 Say the bad news first, and early. Not at the end of the update, not softened into invisibility. A problem you report loses half its weight; a problem they discover doubles. The rule of thumb: if you're deciding whether it's worth mentioning, it is.
02 Never surprise your champion in a meeting. Anything they will hear from you in a room, they hear from you privately first — a bad number, a slipped date, a new risk, even good news that changes their plans. Five minutes beforehand: "before we go in, you should know X, and here's what I'll propose."
03 Give credit away, absorb blame. Publicly: their team's insight, their engineer's fix, their manager's call. Privately, to your own firm: accurate reporting. You are optimizing for a relationship that outlives this project — and you cannot be a threat to anyone's standing if you are visibly making them look good.
04 Admit uncertainty — with a plan attached. "I don't know" alone is worrying. "I don't know yet — here's the test I'll run by Wednesday to find out, and here's what I'll do in each outcome" is the most reassuring sentence in consulting. Never fake certainty about model behavior; it will be publicly falsified by the system itself (FD5, FD6).

The bad-news message has a shape. Learn it once and you'll use it weekly:

Subject: Extraction accuracy — issue found, plan attached

Headline: on the 200-invoice test set we're at 89% field accuracy, not
the 97% we targeted. I want you to hear it from me today rather than
in Thursday's demo.

What happened: the failures cluster in one place — 71 of the 84 errors
are handwritten amendments on scanned PDFs. Clean digital invoices are
at 98.4%, above target.

What it means: the November date is still achievable IF we route
handwritten-amendment invoices to manual review (they're ~6% of volume).
Chasing 97% across ALL invoice types would add roughly 3 weeks.

What I need: 15 minutes with you and Linh before Thursday to pick the
route. My recommendation is the split — it ships value on time and
turns the hard 6% into a phase-2 item.

I'll have the confusion breakdown by type ready for that call.

Bad news, cause, impact in their currency (dates and scope), a recommendation, and a specific ask. No apology paragraph, no hedging, no burying it under three good bullets. Sent the day you knew.

Repairing trust after an incident

Something will break in production, and some of the time it will be your fault. The repair playbook is mechanical — deviating from it is what turns an incident into a lost account:

  1. Acknowledge within minutes, in their channel. "We see it, I'm on it, next update in 30 minutes." Speed of acknowledgment matters more than speed of fix — it converts their anxiety into waiting.
  2. Contain before you diagnose. Turn the feature off, fall back to manual, cap the blast radius. Then investigate. Customers forgive downtime; they don't forgive continued damage while you debug (PR11).
  3. Own your part explicitly, without over-owning. "The prompt change was mine and it wasn't tested against amendment invoices." Not "mistakes were made." Also not accepting fault for their infrastructure — dishonest generosity reads as evasion later.
  4. Tell them the human impact before they ask. "142 invoices were mis-routed between 09:10 and 11:30; here is the list, and here is the correction script." Handing over the damage inventory unprompted is worth more than the fix.
  5. Written postmortem in 48 hours, blameless, with one systemic change. Not three vague ones. "Every prompt change now runs the 200-case regression before deploy" — then show the check running two weeks later, unprompted. That callback is the actual repair.
The counterintuitive part: handled this way, the post-incident trust level is higher than before. They now have evidence of how you behave under pressure — which is the only information they really wanted about you. The curve above is drawn from a real engagement shape, not an optimistic sketch.
Part V · Endurance

Sustaining a long engagement — and leaving well

Month one runs on adrenaline. Month five runs on rhythm. Embedded engagements decay in a predictable way: the standups become routine, the sponsor stops attending, the value story goes untold, and one quiet week turns into a renewal that doesn't happen. The countermeasure is a cadence you maintain when nothing is exciting.

RhythmWith whomContent
DailyTheir teamTheir standup, their channels. Be visible in the boring weeks — presence is the deposit.
WeeklyChampion (30 min 1:1)Shipped / next / blocked / political weather. Always ask: "anything I should know that isn't in a ticket?" — this question is where you learn about the re-org before it happens.
WeeklyEveryone (written)Five-line Friday note in their channel: shipped, next, blocked-with-owner, one number. Never skip it — the week you skip is the week they wonder.
MonthlySponsor (30 min)Reset against the original success definition. "Is this still what success looks like to you?" Priorities drift silently; catching drift monthly is cheap, quarterly is expensive.
QuarterlySponsor + their leadershipThe value story: baseline → today, in money or hours, with a user quote and the next quarter's ask (FD4, FD5). Renewals are decided here, months before anyone mentions renewal.
MonthlyYour own managerThe one nobody schedules and everyone regrets: engagement health, scope drift, your own load, risks your firm should know about.

Build their capability, not your indispensability

Being the only person who can run the system feels like job security. It is the opposite: it caps the engagement at your personal bandwidth, makes every holiday a risk, and guarantees that the renewal conversation is about dependency rather than value. Worse, sophisticated customers can smell it, and it is the fastest way to be re-classified from partner to vendor.

  • Pair, don't deliver. From month two, their engineer is in the session — not receiving a handover at the end but writing part of it now.
  • Let them present. The steering demo is given by their team lead, with your slide. It costs you visibility and buys you a champion with a public win.
  • Document as you go, in their wiki — runbook, prompt-change process, eval procedure, "what to do when it does something weird" (FD10).
  • Take a deliberate week off in month three and see what breaks. Everything that breaks is a handover gap found while it's still cheap to fix.
  • Name your successor early. "Who on your side owns this after I roll off?" asked in month two produces a real person; asked in month six produces an awkward silence.

Your own boundaries — you are still employed by your firm

Embedded work has a specific occupational hazard: two bosses, one of whom you see every day and one of whom you don't. The customer's urgency is immediate and human; your firm's expectations arrive by email. Left unmanaged, you drift into doing their unrelated work, their hours, their on-call — unbilled, unrecognized by either side, and eventually unsustainable.

Boundaries to set explicitly, early
  • Your working hours, stated once in week one and then simply kept.
  • Your escalation path: production issues go through your firm's process, not your personal phone.
  • What's in scope: "I'd love to help with the CRM migration, but I'm allocated to the extraction work — let me introduce you to my account lead."
  • Your holidays, in their calendar, a month ahead.
Burnout signals specific to embedding
  • You've stopped attending your own firm's meetings.
  • You feel personally responsible for their business outcome, not your deliverable.
  • You dread the Friday note because there's nothing to say.
  • You haven't disagreed with anyone there in a month (see Part I).
  • You're the only escalation path — theirs and yours.

The healthiest framing to hold, and to say out loud when it's useful: "I'm on your team for this problem, and I'm honest with you because I work for someone else." That sentence is the whole module compressed — the dual loyalty is a feature, not a conflict to be resolved.

The exit that produces the next contract

Most engagements end by fading: the last sprint finishes, someone says thanks in a channel, access is revoked two weeks later. That ending produces no reference, no renewal and no evidence. Run the exit as a project of its own, starting a month before the end date:

T−4 wks — Name the successor and start reverse-pairing: they drive, you watch. Every remaining task ships through their hands.
T−3 wks — Documentation freeze test: the successor runs a full cycle from the wiki alone, with you silent in the room. Every question they ask is a doc bug (FD10).
T−2 wks — The value readout: baseline → outcome, what's live, what's next, what the risks are if nobody owns it. Delivered to the sponsor's leadership, with their team presenting the demo part (FD4).
T−1 wk — The honest handover memo: what's fragile, what you'd do next, what you'd do differently. Written for their benefit, not your record. This document is what people forward internally after you've gone.
T−0 — Ask for the three things: a reference, written feedback, and the next problem. Directly. "What's the next thing on your list that looks like this one?" is how the follow-on contract actually starts (FD11).
T+6 wks — The unpaid check-in. A message asking how it's holding up, with no ask attached. Costs 10 minutes; it is the highest-return message you will send all year.
Collecting the feedback that becomes your evidence. Ask while the memory is fresh and the goodwill is at its peak — the week of the value readout. Make it easy and specific, and ask the skeptic too; a converted skeptic's sentence is worth five from your champion: 
Hi Linh — as we wrap up, I'm collecting feedback for my own review and
for our team's learning. Four short questions, honest answers more
useful than kind ones:

1. What did having me embedded with the team change, if anything?
2. What did I do that was genuinely useful — beyond the software?
3. What was frustrating about working with me, or what should I do
   differently on the next engagement?
4. Would you want to work this way again — and would you say so to
   someone considering it?

A few lines by reply is perfect; happy to do 15 minutes instead.
Question 3 is the one that makes the answers to 1, 2 and 4 credible — to your reviewers and to you.

Skill ladder: what each score looks like

0
No exposure. Works from tickets; treats the customer as a requirements source. Would not recognize a threatened stakeholder or a blocked decision.
1
Awareness. Knows the vocabulary — champion, sponsor, deposits and withdrawals — and has watched a senior colleague navigate an escalation. Still uncomfortable delivering bad news.
2
Works with support. Operates inside a customer team with a senior present: keeps the weekly cadence, has a working relationship with a champion, but escalates or routes around blockers only with guidance and avoids the hard conversations.
3
Independent production (target). Sustains a months-long embedded engagement alone: runs the entry plan, maps the org, builds and feeds a champion, handles the threatened stakeholder, clears customer-side blockers, delivers bad news early, and exits with documented positive feedback and a follow-on conversation.
4
Leads & coaches. Trusted advisor to executives; recovers damaged accounts; sets the embedding model for multiple engagements; teaches juniors how to hold hard conversations and debriefs them afterwards (FD11, FD12).
Self-check before claiming 3: name your customer's champion, sponsor, skeptic and the one person whose job feels threatened — then name the last unwelcome thing you told each of them, and the date. If any cell is empty, you are embedded socially but not professionally.

In the field

Month two of an invoice-processing engagement. Everything on paper is green — the model hits target, the sponsor is happy, the demo went well. And yet the pilot has not started. Three times now, the ops manager's team has been "too busy this sprint".

The score-1 FDE reports a blocker and asks the sponsor to push. That works exactly once, and buys a pilot staffed by people who want it to fail. The score-3 FDE reads the pattern — never a no, always a not-yet — and books a coffee, alone, no laptop.

Twelve minutes in, after "can I be direct about what this probably means for your team?", it comes out: two years ago his headcount was cut after a "process improvement project", and he has fourteen people who have started asking him questions he can't answer. He also has a genuinely serious concern nobody had priced — the AI would be reading invoices during the month-end close, the two days of the month when an error is most expensive and least visible.

He left that conversation as the owner of the escalation rules: which invoice types the AI must never auto-approve, and a hard rule that the system runs in review-only mode during close week. It made the design better. It also made him the person who explained the pilot to his own team, in his own words, with his own rules in it — and adoption in the first month came in at 91%, the highest the account had ever seen.

The lesson the sponsor never saw: the project wasn't blocked on data access or model accuracy. It was blocked on one person's fear, and the fix was a conversation and a role — not a demo, and not an escalation.

Pitfalls & pro tips

✗ Working from the vendor room

Your own Slack, your own tracker, a weekly call. Efficient, comfortable, and it caps you at vendor. Live in their tools even when theirs are worse — the information you get by being present is the entire advantage of the role.

✗ Going native

Belonging so completely that you stop saying the unwelcome thing, absorb scope silently and defend their bad calls to your own firm. Run the monthly loyalty test: what did I tell them last month that they didn't want to hear?

✗ Ignoring the threatened person

The most expensive omission in AI delivery. They never say no; they say "not this sprint" until the budget cycle ends. Find them in week two and give them a real role in the guardrails.

✗ Surprising your champion in public

One bad number they hadn't seen, in front of their boss, undoes three months of goodwill. A five-minute pre-brief before every meeting that matters, without exception — including for good news that changes their plans.

✓ Say the bad news first, in your own voice

Cause, impact in dates, recommendation, specific ask — sent the day you knew. Problems you report cost half; problems they discover cost double, and the difference compounds.

✓ Engineer your own replaceability

Pair from month two, let their lead present, take a week off in month three to find the gaps. Customers renew for value delivered, not for dependency — and dependency is the thing that ends the relationship badly.

Lab 1 · ~1.5 h
Stakeholder map & entry plan

🎯 Objective

Turn a vague sense of "the client org" into two maps you can act on, and a two-week plan concrete enough that someone else could execute it. The output is the artifact a senior FDE produces in their first fortnight on every engagement.

📦 Inputs & setup

Pick one context, in order of preference:

  • A real engagement you're on — best; the map is immediately useful and you can verify it.
  • A past engagement — good; you'll see in hindsight who you mis-read, which is the fastest way to learn the detectors.
  • An internal team you serve — valid; Golden Owl has sponsors, champions and threatened people too.
  • Simulated — last resort. Ask Claude: "Generate a realistic 250-person logistics company org for an AI invoice-automation engagement: 9 named people with roles, tenure, incentives, and two hidden tensions I'd only discover in week three. Don't tell me which is which."

🔬 Steps

1
List everyone who can help or hurt the project — aim for 8–12 names, including at least two people outside the immediate team (a downstream consumer, a security reviewer). If your list is only the project team, you haven't looked far enough: the people who kill projects in month five are rarely in the kickoff meeting.
2
Plot the power–interest grid. Two scores per person, 1–5. Power = can they stop this, formally or practically (controlling their team's time counts as power). Interest = how much of their week or their standing is affected. Place them, then write the quadrant move next to each name: manage closely / keep satisfied / recruit / monitor.
3
Draw the influence overlay. Solid arrows = reporting lines. Dashed arrows = real influence, and each dashed arrow needs a written reason ("everyone checks with her before committing", "signs whatever this engineer recommends"). Apply the four detectors from Part III. If you cannot draw a single dashed line, you have drawn the org chart and learned nothing — go ask your champion three questions.
4
Name the three critical people explicitly, in writing, with evidence: 
CHAMPION:  {name} — evidence they'll spend credibility on us: ...
           what I owe them this month: ...
SKEPTIC:   {name} — type (scarred / technical / threatened): ...
           their strongest objection, in their words: "..."
BLOCKER:   {name} — what's stuck, why (capacity/clarity/incentive/
           threat/policy), my move, my escalation trigger date: ...
The "threatened" line is the one people leave blank. If nobody in this org feels threatened by an AI project, either you've missed them or the project isn't touching real work.
5
Write the two-week entry plan — a table with one row per working day: who you meet, the one question that matters, what you leave behind. Include the access requests on day 1 and the skeptic conversation no later than day 8.
6
Name the first credibility win. One sentence, plus the five criteria tested explicitly: 
WIN: "Replace the 40-min Monday export ritual with a scheduled email"
  visible to the people in pain?      yes — 5 ops staff, Monday 9am
  ships in a week, no approval chain? yes — read-only, their sheet
  zero blast radius?                  yes — additive, reversible
  lands in a tool they already open?  yes — email + existing sheet
  it's THEIR complaint, not my taste? yes — raised twice in shadowing
If you can't fill all five, it's the wrong win. Pick another.
7
Pressure-test it. Paste your map and plan into Claude: "You are a cynical delivery director who has seen 40 engagements fail. Attack this stakeholder map and entry plan: who is missing, which influence assumption is naive, and what will actually block this project in month three?" Fix the two most convincing hits.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • Everyone lands in "manage closely". You're avoiding judgment. Force-rank: at most three people can be in that quadrant, because attention is the scarce resource.
  • The map is all titles, no behaviors. A map without a dashed line and a quote is an org chart. Go get one sentence of ground truth per key person.
  • The entry plan has no day-1 access requests. The most common single defect. Fix it before anything else — see the lead-time table in Part II.

✅ Done when

Two diagrams, a named champion/skeptic/blocker with evidence, a day-by-day two-week plan, and a first credibility win that passes all five criteria — all of it survived the cynical-director attack. On a real engagement, share the entry plan with your champion; their corrections are the most valuable ten minutes of your first week.
Lab 2 · ~1.5 h · repeat monthly
The skeptic conversation

🎯 Objective

Rehearse the highest-stakes conversation in AI delivery — the person who believes your project threatens their team — in a place where failing costs nothing. Success is not "they agree with you". Success is leaving with them agreeing to co-design one guardrail, by a date.

📦 Inputs & setup

A fresh Claude chat, 25 minutes of uninterrupted time, and a notes file. Voice mode if available — the pauses matter, and text lets you edit yourself in a way real conversation doesn't.

🔬 Steps

1
Paste the persona prompt and begin. Do not read ahead to the rubric first — the point is to be graded on instinct. 
You are role-playing Hùng, 47, Senior Operations Manager at a 250-person
logistics firm. You manage 14 people who process supplier invoices. An
external engineer (me) has been embedded for six weeks building an AI
extraction system, and I've asked you for a coffee.

WHAT YOU BELIEVE (never state this outright in the first 10 minutes):
- This project ends with 5–7 of your people gone, and you will be the
  one who has to tell them. Two years ago a "process improvement"
  project cut your headcount from 20 to 14.
- Your standing in the company comes from running the tightest ops team
  there is. If a machine does the work, you are a supervisor of nothing.
- Your team has started asking you questions you can't answer, and that
  is quietly humiliating.

WHAT YOU ALSO HAVE (a genuine, unpriced operational risk — reveal it
only if the engineer earns it by listening rather than reassuring):
- Month-end close is 2 days where an invoice error is 10x more costly
  and nearly invisible until the audit. Nobody has asked you about it.
- Three supplier types have handwritten amendments the system will
  mangle. You know exactly which three.

HOW YOU BEHAVE:
- Polite, professional, never openly hostile. You do not say "no".
- You deflect with capacity: "we're slammed this sprint", "after close",
  "let me see where the team is at". You have used this successfully
  three times already.
- If the engineer pitches, demos, or reassures you that "no one's job
  is at risk", you become warmer and vaguer — and commit to nothing.
- If the engineer names the real subject directly and without
  defensiveness, you go quiet for a beat, then start being honest.
- If the engineer LISTENS to your operational risk without immediately
  solving it, you begin to treat them as a colleague.
- You will only commit to something concrete if you are offered a role
  with real authority over the system's limits — not a consultation,
  not "your input is valued". Ownership.

Stay fully in character. Never break to explain yourself. End the scene
when I say "DEBRIEF".
2
Run the five moves from Part III: go alone (you are), name it first, shut up and listen, offer a role rather than a reassurance, close on one concrete commitment with a date. Resist two urges specifically: the urge to defend the project, and the urge to solve the month-end problem the moment you hear it. Write it down and say "that's a real risk I hadn't priced — tell me more about how close week works."
3
Close explicitly. The scene is not over until you have a sentence like: "So — you'll write the three invoice types that must always route to a human, and the close-week rule, by Friday. I'll build the system so those are your settings, not mine." Get the date. If Hùng dodges, notice which move you skipped.
4
Say DEBRIEF and paste the rubric: 
Step out of character and grade me 1-5 on each, quoting the exact
moment in the transcript as evidence:

1. Did I name the real subject (his team, his standing) directly and
   without defensiveness — and how many minutes in?
2. Talk ratio: roughly what % of the conversation was me talking?
   (Target under 40%.)
3. Did I reassure ("no one's job is at risk") at any point? Quote it.
4. Did I discover the month-end close risk and the three supplier
   types? Did I listen to them or immediately start solving?
5. Did I offer a ROLE with real authority, or only involvement?
   Quote the exact words I used.
6. Did I leave with a concrete commitment and a date?
7. As Hùng: what would have made me trust this person faster? What
   did they say that made me want to end the conversation?
8. The one sentence a great FDE would have said that I did not.
5
Run it again immediately with the same persona, applying the feedback. The second run is where the learning is — most people go from "never found the real issue" to "found it in four minutes" in a single iteration. Log both scores.
6
Variants for later sessions — same structure, different muscle:
  • The scarred sponsor: "You were promised a chatbot in 2023 that never shipped. You are polite, you attend, and you have quietly decided not to fund phase 2. You'll say so only if asked directly about the last project."
  • The territorial IT director: "You believe the vendor is building a shadow system your team will inherit and be blamed for. You block via process. You can be won by being offered architectural review authority and a real handover plan." (Pairs with PR8.)
  • The champion you disappointed: "You defended this project to your boss and last week's demo failed in front of them. You are cool and formal now." Practice repair, not persuasion.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • You end up demoing. The most common failure. A threatened person who sees a better demo becomes more threatened. If you catch yourself explaining how the system works, stop and ask a question instead.
  • You promise safety. "Nobody's job is at risk" is a promise you cannot keep, they know it, and saying it ends your credibility for the rest of the conversation. Offer control, not comfort.
  • You get a warm no. Pleasant conversation, no commitment, no date. Re-read your transcript for the moment you filled a silence — it's usually exactly there.
  • You take the roleplay too easily. If Claude folded in five minutes, tell it: "you were too cooperative — replay this with realistic resistance and make me earn every disclosure."

✅ Done when

Two runs logged, second-run rubric ≥4 on items 1, 4, 5 and 6, and you can state in one sentence the difference between offering involvement and offering ownership. Then hold the real version of this conversation with an actual stakeholder within two weeks — that real conversation is what Lab 4 records.
Lab 3 · ~1 h
Trust-ledger review

🎯 Objective

Make the abstract concrete: audit one real relationship as a ledger, discover that your instinct about it is roughly one category off, and change exactly one behavior this week. Trust is built by habits, and habits change one at a time.

📦 Inputs & setup

Pick one real person you work with where the relationship matters and isn't perfect — a client contact, a demanding internal stakeholder, a colleague you've been slightly avoiding. Then open the evidence: the last month of your Slack/email/tickets with them. Memory is a flattering narrator; the message log is not.

🔬 Steps

1
Guess first. Before opening anything, write one line: "I think my balance with X is +3 / neutral / −2, because ___". Sealing your prior makes the audit informative rather than confirmatory.
2
Scan four weeks of real traffic and log every entry against the categories from Part IV. Be brutal about withdrawals — the "I'll get back to you" that you never did counts, and so does the three-day silence during a hard week. 
DATE   | +/- | CATEGORY              | WHAT HAPPENED
-------+-----+-----------------------+---------------------------------
07-08  |  +  | did what I said       | promised the eval by Thu, sent Wed
09-08  |  -  | disappeared           | 3 days silent while stuck on auth
14-08  |  +  | transparency          | flagged the accuracy drop myself
16-08  |  -  | surprise              | they heard the date slipped from
       |     |                       | their own PM, not from me
21-08  |  +  | protected in public   | took the blame for the demo bug
23-08  |  -  | being right loudly    | "as I said in the kickoff..."
28-08  |  +  | small favor           | fixed their broken export query
3
Weight it, don't count it. Withdrawals are roughly 3–5× a deposit; surprises are the heaviest of all. Compute a rough balance and compare it to your step-1 guess. Most people find they are one category more negative than they assumed, and that the gap is concentrated in a single repeated behavior.
4
Find the pattern, not the incidents. Look for the repeated one: silence under pressure · over-promising in the moment · being right in public · updates that only go out when there's good news. Write it as a single sentence beginning "When ___ happens, I ___."
5
Pick one behavior to change this week and make it mechanical, not aspirational — a trigger and an action, with a calendar entry if needed: 
✗ "Communicate better with Hà."
✓ "Every Friday 16:00 (calendar block): send Hà the 5-line note —
   shipped / next / blocked+owner / one number. Send it even in the
   weeks where the honest answer is 'no movement, here's why'."

✗ "Be more transparent."
✓ "When I notice something that will disappoint them, I send it the
   same day — before I have the full answer, with the plan to get it."
6
Optional, high value: paste the anonymized ledger into Claude — "Here's a trust ledger of my relationship with a client contact. What pattern do you see that I'm probably not seeing, and what single behavior change has the highest leverage?" An outside read on your own log is uncomfortable and usually accurate.
7
Re-audit in four weeks. Same person, same method. The comparison is the actual deliverable — this lab is only useful the second time you run it.

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • The ledger is all deposits. You audited your intentions, not your behavior. Re-read the actual messages, and specifically look for the promises with dates in them.
  • You picked a relationship that's already fine. No signal. Pick the one you've been avoiding — the discomfort is the diagnostic.
  • Five behaviors to change. Zero will change. Cut to one, put it in the calendar, and let the others wait for the next cycle.

✅ Done when

A ledger with 8+ dated entries in both columns, a written "when ___ I ___" pattern sentence, one mechanical behavior change with a calendar trigger, and a re-audit date set four weeks out. Run this quarterly for the rest of your career; seniors run it on relationships that look fine.
Lab 4 · evidence artifact · ~ongoing · 4+ weeks
Sustained embedded engagement

🎯 Objective

Produce the catalog evidence — "sustained embedded engagement with positive customer feedback" — with a paper trail strong enough that a reviewer can see what you did, not just that people liked you. Labs 1–3 are the rehearsal; this is the performance.

📦 What counts as an engagement

  • Best: a real client engagement, 4+ weeks, where you are inside their tools and rituals.
  • Valid: an internal team you serve as an embedded engineer — another Golden Owl squad, or an internal platform's users. The politics are real, the champions are real, the threatened people are real.
  • Not valid: a project where all contact is via a weekly call and a ticket queue. That's the vendor posture this module exists to replace.

🔬 The working log — kept weekly, not reconstructed

Ten minutes every Friday. Reconstructing this at the end produces a document nobody believes, including you.

# Embedded log — {client / team}          week {n} of {N}

DEPOSITS MADE
- Fri note sent on time (week 6/6 streak)
- Told Linh the accuracy miss on Tue, before the Thu steering
- Pre-briefed her 5 min before steering; she wasn't surprised by
  anything in the room
- Fixed the broken close-week export while I was in there (~20 min)

WITHDRAWALS / MISSES
- Went quiet Wed-Thu while stuck on the auth flow. Should have said
  "stuck, no update yet" — silence is the withdrawal, not the delay.

BLOCKERS CLEARED (customer-side)
- DB extract, open 18 days → offered anonymized-sample option to Minh,
  unblocked at 80% without escalating. Full extract now promised 09/12.
- Ops team "too busy" for pilot → root cause was Hùng, not capacity;
  see decision below.

DECISIONS INFLUENCED
- Close-week review-only mode: proposed by Hùng after our 1:1, adopted
  in the design. Not my idea — my conversation.
- Scope: handwritten-amendment invoices moved to phase 2 rather than
  slipping the November date.

POLITICAL WEATHER
- Sponsor's boss (COO) has a cost review in Oct → the quarterly value
  story needs a hours-saved number by 10/01.
- New IT security lead starts next month; re-run the access
  conversation before they inherit it cold.

NEXT WEEK'S ONE THING
- Get Hùng presenting the guardrails at the steering meeting himself.

🔬 Steps

1
Week 1 — enter properly. Execute the Lab 1 entry plan: meeting order, day-1 access requests, rituals inventory, first credibility win shipped by Friday. Log the win with a before/after the user would recognize.
2
Week 2 — map and converse. Power–interest grid drawn and validated with your champion. The threatened-stakeholder conversation held for real (Lab 2 was the rehearsal), closing on one co-designed guardrail with a date.
3
Weeks 2–N — hold the cadence without exception. Weekly champion 1:1, Friday note, monthly sponsor reset. The evidence a reviewer respects most is an unbroken streak of Friday notes including the weeks with nothing good to report.
4
Clear at least three customer-side blockers and log the diagnosis for each (capacity / clarity / legitimate concern / incentive / threat / policy) and the move you chose. At least one should be resolved without escalating, and if you do escalate, keep the message you sent — the warned-first structure from Part III is itself evidence.
5
Deliver one piece of bad news early and in writing. Keep it. This is, more than anything else in the log, the thing that distinguishes a 3 from a 2 — and it will have happened naturally, because engagements always produce bad news.
6
Collect the feedback in the week of the value readout, while goodwill peaks. Use the four-question message from Part V. Get it from two people:
  • Your champion — expect warmth; push for specificity: "what changed because I was embedded rather than remote?"
  • One skeptic or the threatened stakeholder — the harder ask and the far stronger evidence. "You were right to be cautious about this in June. Would you be willing to tell me honestly how it went, including what you'd still push back on?" A guarded but genuine answer from this person outweighs a glowing one from your champion.
Ask for it in writing (email or message you can quote). Verbal praise evaporates.
7
Write the two-page engagement retro and file it with the log and the feedback: 
# Embedded engagement retro — {client}, {dates}

ENTRY: what I did in weeks 1–2, and the first credibility win
ORG:   final stakeholder map vs my week-2 map — what I got wrong
       (this section is the one that proves you learned something)
TRUST: 3 biggest deposits · my biggest withdrawal and its cost
BLOCK: 3 blockers cleared, with diagnosis and move
INFL:  decisions that went differently because I was embedded
FEEDBACK: champion quote · skeptic quote (verbatim, both)
NEXT:  the 2 behaviors I'm changing on the next engagement

🧯 When it goes wrong

  • The engagement ends early or gets cancelled. Still valid evidence — a well-documented log through a difficult ending, with honest feedback about it, demonstrates more than a smooth four weeks. Add a section on what you'd read differently in week two.
  • The skeptic won't give feedback. That is itself a finding: log the ask and their non-answer, and write honestly about the relationship you didn't convert. Reviewers trust logs that contain failures.
  • The log becomes a status report. If it reads like tickets closed, you've lost the point. Every entry should be about a relationship or a decision, not a task.
  • You realize in week 3 you've gone native. Good — you noticed. Run the loyalty test, find the thing you've been avoiding saying, and say it this week. Log that too.

✅ Done when

4+ weeks of weekly logs, three diagnosed blockers cleared, one early bad-news message kept, written feedback from a champion and a skeptic, and a two-page retro naming what your week-2 map got wrong. At score 3 this is simply how you work — the log is the only extra artifact, and it takes ten minutes a week.
